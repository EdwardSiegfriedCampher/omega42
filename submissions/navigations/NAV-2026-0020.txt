Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of creation from unity and nothing: a world brought forth from one source, in which every part follows its own law and every part agrees with every other. It rests on a gap. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one, so the first unity is not one of the units it produces. It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next. What the story keeps out of view is the producer. Each state follows from the state before it by the rule alone, as if every part unfolded from within and nothing passed between them; their agreement is built into the rule from the start, so that the source, which arranged it, is nowhere visible in the working. From there the story moves as a flow: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, and at the twenty-fourth every pair is home, each state reflecting in its place the whole of the round it belongs to. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting in which there is a reason for every term: in the arithmetic nothing happens without a sufficient reason, the account always closes, and why this round rather than another is not settled by the rule. Clocks set once by their maker and keeping time together without touching are the familiar picture: no clock moves another, and all agree. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is choice, the judgment by which the maker chose this world among all that were possible as the best.

The conventional philosophy meets this reading closely. Leibniz's Monadology of 1714 holds that the world is made of simple substances without windows, each a living mirror of the universe, that God alone is the primary unity, that all agree by a pre-established harmony, and that nothing is without a sufficient reason. Where the reading strains, it strains on return: Leibniz's world unfolds and does not cycle, and its order is the best of possible worlds, chosen, where the arithmetic offers one world with no alternatives.

As a practice, the reading suits the habit Leibniz urged on his correspondents: to calculate, to seek the reason for each thing, and to see each part as a perspective on the whole. It can be kept honest by fixing its correspondences before checking them.

The lineage is unusually direct. Leibniz worked out binary arithmetic and published it in 1703, and in 1697 he proposed a medal for the Duke of Brunswick with the binary numbers on it and the motto that one suffices to draw all things from nothing, calling it an image of creation: unity and zero, God and the void. He invented the calculus at about the same time as Newton, and dreamed of a universal symbolic language in which disputes could be settled by calculation.

If the analogy holds, the world is a calculation whose every term has its reason and whose parts agree without touching. Its hardest implication is that the arithmetic has no other worlds to choose among, so the choice that Leibniz put at the root of creation lies outside the rule.
