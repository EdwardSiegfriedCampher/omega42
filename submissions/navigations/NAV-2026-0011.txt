Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of begetting: a source that brings forth what is like itself, told in a world that cannot contain the source. It rests on a gap. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one, so the ratio never appears there as a thing. It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next. That is the gap the story stands on, and the analogy is strongest exactly there: the infinite is present in the finite world as its law, never as one of its members. What the story keeps out of view is the source itself. The sequence that the rule begets, one, one, two, three, five, eight, approaches the ratio without ever reaching it (thirteen over eight is 1.625, a hundred and forty-four over eighty-nine is 1.6179775, and four thousand one hundred and eighty-one over two thousand five hundred and eighty-four is 1.6180341, always a little over or a little under), and once the numbers are folded into their remainders even that approach is hidden, because the size of the numbers is forgotten and only the law survives. The begotten is visible; the begetter is not. From there the story moves as a flow: the folded sequence goes round, twenty-four steps and home, and at the twelfth step every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, the pair that adds back to nothing. A passage through negation at the half and a return at the end is what the rule does to every state, not to a chosen one. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting in which what was given is weighed against what came back: in the arithmetic the account always balances, every state returns, nothing is lost, and what the balance means is not settled by the rule at all. A sun reflected in a well is the familiar picture: the well never holds the sun, yet the light on its water moves exactly as the sun moves, and the reflection breaks and returns with every ripple. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is that the source is a person who loves, and that the reckoning is a judgment and not only a sum.

The oldest version of this reading is in the language itself: the Greek word that John's Gospel uses for the Word, logos, is also Euclid's word for a ratio, and the golden ratio is Euclid's "extreme and mean ratio". In 1509 Luca Pacioli called it the divine proportion for five reasons, among them its three terms, which he likened to the Trinity, and its incommensurability, which he likened to God's incomprehensibility. Augustine's reading of evil as privation, an absence with no being of its own, fits the remainders three and six, which come to nothing when multiplied by themselves, better than it fits the other six remainders, each of which can be undone by multiplying. Where the conventional theology resists, it resists firmly: creation in the Christian story is out of nothing, by a free and personal God, while this world is given complete; and the Son is of one substance with the Father, not a sequence that only approaches him.

As a practice, the reading is best used for contemplation and for teaching, not as proof. The lineage runs from Pythagoras, for whom all was number, through Plato's Timaeus and Augustine's measure, number and weight, to Pacioli, and to Kepler, who in 1596 saw the Trinity in the sphere: the center the Father, the surface the Son, the space between the Spirit.

If the analogy holds, the finite world is not a fall from the infinite but the place where the infinite is present as law, and redemption reads as the fact that everything that can go round can come back. Its sharpest implication is also its hardest: because every state returns, the arithmetic leans toward a restoration of all things, as Origen taught, rather than toward the separation most Christian traditions hold, and it leaves judgment, which the story places in God and in the choices of persons, entirely outside the rule.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of begetting told in a world that cannot hold its source. This essay carries that single synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the whole figure is short, and every part of it was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair of remainders gives way to the next by adding, has no room for the golden ratio. The ratio is the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, and in the world of nine remainders no member squared equals itself plus one: the nine values of x squared minus x minus one are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none of them is zero. So the ratio is absent as a thing. Yet the rule by which the world moves, add the last two to get the next, is the very rule whose growth the ratio measures in the unbounded numbers. The ratio is therefore present in the finite world in the only way it can be: as the law by which everything there moves, never as anything that moves. That is the oldest and most careful sentence of Christian thought about God and the world, said here in arithmetic: the maker is not one more item among the things made. And because the ratio is no member of that world, the begetter cannot be seen there either. The sequence the rule begets, one, one, two, three, five, eight and on, approaches the ratio and never arrives; its quotients fall alternately just above and just below it, as a pilgrim's path might cross and recross a line it never walks along. Folded into the nine remainders, the sequence gives up even this approach, because folding forgets size. What survives the folding is the law and nothing of the magnitude. A finite world can show the begotten; it cannot show the begetter, nor even the begotten's nearness to the begetter, only the shape of its life. The analogy is strongest where it is most modest: it says that the Son is the visible law of an invisible source, which is close to what the prologue of John's Gospel says of the Word.

What the folded sequence does over time gives the analogy both its drama and its limit. It runs round in twenty-four steps and comes home. At the twelfth step every pair is carried to its exact negative: the pair two and five becomes seven and four, and seven and four is what must be added to two and five to make nothing. The half-way point is a negation, and the end is a return. This is the part of the analogy that the Striker's wording names most directly, the infinite becoming the twenty-four step recurrence, and the arithmetic gives it two distinct places, not one: a place of undoing in the middle and a place of restoring at the end. The arithmetic fits well the telling that calls these the Cross and the Resurrection. It fits less well the claim that they happened once, because the same negation and return happen to every state, not to one chosen state: in this world the passage through negation is not an event in the life of one figure but a property of the law. The same law settles the account. A reckoning weighs what was given against what came back, and here the account always balances: every state returns after twenty-four steps, and nothing is lost or left behind. Even the states built only of three, six and nine, which come to nothing when multiplied by themselves and which a reader might take for the image of a fallen world, either run round a cycle of eight of their own or, the single state of two zeros, stay where they are. If sin is the twisting of a world away from its source, as the Striker's last image suggests, the arithmetic offers no state that is lost for good. This is why the reading notes the hope of Origen, that all things are restored, as the direction the analogy leans. It is also why the reading stops: a balance that always closes is not a judgment. What the faith calls judgment is a person's verdict on persons who were free, and nothing in a rule of adding is free.

Taken together, these make one figure and not four. The gap explains why the source is hidden: what is not a member cannot be seen among the members. The hidden source and the gap together set the course: what is present only as law must show itself in the way the law moves, and the law moves by negation and return. The return answers the begetting: what was begotten comes home whole. And the reckoning that would finish the figure is the one part the rule cannot write.

A sun on the water of a well holds the picture together. The well does not contain the sun, the light moves exactly as the sun moves, every ripple breaks the image and the image returns. Its limits are the reading's limits. The sun does not love the well, and the well does not choose. The analogy is exact about transcendence, self-emptying, negation and return, and it is silent about love, freedom and judgment, which the Christian story puts at its center. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
