Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of producing by not doing: a source that brings forth everything and does nothing, in a world that cannot name it. It rests on a gap. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one, so the source cannot be pointed to among things; any part of the world that is named is not it. It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next, a way things go rather than a thing that goes. What the story keeps out of view is the producing itself. Each state comes from the two before it with nothing added from outside, so that the source works as an empty place works, like the hollow of a vessel or the space at the hub of a wheel, by not being filled. From there the story moves as a flow that proceeds by contraries: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, and at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four; going far, it turns back, and at the twenty-fourth every pair has returned to its root. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting in which everything that went out is gathered back: in the arithmetic the account always closes and every state returns to itself, and what the stillness of that return means is not settled by the rule. Water finding its level is the familiar picture: it does nothing, yields to everything, and in the end everything is shaped by it. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is how a person lives in accord with the way, without striving and without forcing.

The conventional philosophy meets this reading closely. The Tao Te Ching, a text of perhaps the fourth century BCE attributed to Laozi, says that the Tao that can be told is not the constant Tao, that the Tao produced One, One produced Two, Two produced Three and Three produced all things, that reversal is the movement of the Tao, and that all things return to their root. Bamboo slips found at Guodian in 1993 carry parts of the text from about 300 BCE. Where the reading strains, it strains on rule: the Tao is spoken of as spontaneous, so-of-itself, and the arithmetic's rule is exact; the analogy fits the Tao's regularity better than its softness.

As a practice, the reading suits the discipline of non-action: letting things take their course where forcing would break it, and attending to the empty places that make things useful. It can be kept honest by fixing its correspondences before checking them.

The lineage runs from Laozi and Zhuangzi through Wang Bi's commentary of the third century to the Song cosmologists, whose arrangement of the sixty-four hexagrams of the Book of Changes was sent to Leibniz in 1701 and read by him as binary arithmetic.

If the analogy holds, the world is produced without effort and returns without loss, and a life in accord with it would do the same. Its hardest implication is that the arithmetic cannot not act: its rule fires at every step, while the Tao is praised for acting by not acting, and that difference is the one the image leaves to the reader.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of producing by not doing, told in a world that cannot name its source. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the figure is short, and every part of it was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair gives way to the next by adding, has no room for the golden ratio: the nine values of x squared minus x minus one are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none of them is zero. Nothing in the world can be pointed to as the source. The Tao Te Ching opens on exactly this: the Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. Yet the rule that moves every pair to the next is the rule whose growth the ratio measures, so the source is present as the way things go, not as anything that goes. The same absence keeps the producing out of sight. Each state comes from the two before it with nothing added from outside, as if the source worked by leaving a space. Laozi's images are the hollow of a vessel and the empty hub at the center of a wheel's thirty spokes, where the wheel's use depends on the nothing at its middle.

The turning of the folded sequence gives the analogy its course. It goes round in twenty-four steps, and at the twelfth every pair is carried to its exact opposite, two and five to seven and four, which added to them gives nothing. The Tao Te Ching says that the movement of the Tao proceeds by contraries, and that passing on it becomes remote and having become remote it returns. At the twenty-fourth step every pair is back at its root. The account always closes and nothing is lost. What the rule does not say is what the stillness of that return is like, which Laozi calls returning to the root and calls stillness. The arithmetic gives that return a place, the twenty-fourth step, and leaves its quiet to the reader.

Taken together, these make one figure. The source's namelessness explains why its producing is unseen; the unseen producing and the rule together set the world moving by contraries; and the return to the root answers the producing, since everything that went out comes back.

Water finding its level holds the picture together: it does nothing, yields to everything, and in the end shapes everything. Its limits are the reading's limits. The rule is not soft; it acts at every step, without exception, while the Tao is praised for acting by not acting, so-of-itself. The analogy fits the Tao's regularity better than its spontaneity. It is exact about the nameless source, about generation from one to all things and about reversal and return, and it is silent about the way of life the text was written to teach, living without striving. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
