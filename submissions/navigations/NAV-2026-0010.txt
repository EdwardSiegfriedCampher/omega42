### 1\. REQUEST AND NAVIGATION ORDER

QUESTION VERBATIM

> The Mechanism of General Anesthesia: Despite over 170 years of clinical use, the exact molecular mechanism by which chemically diverse general anesthetics (from xenon gas to propofol) reversibly abolish consciousness remains structurally unresolved. The foundational Meyer-Overton correlation demonstrates that anesthetic potency, measured by the Minimum Alveolar Concentration (MAC), is strictly inversely proportional to lipid solubility (the oil/gas partition coefficient, λoil/gas), such that MAC × λoil/gas ≈ 1.3 atm for almost all volatile anesthetics. However, the structural anomaly is that modern pharmacology identifies specific protein targets (e.g., GABA_A receptors, NMDA receptors, TREK-1 potassium channels) rather than the lipid bilayer itself, yet the binding affinities (Kd) of these anesthetics to their protein targets are extraordinarily weak, typically in the 0.1 mM to 1.0 mM range (10^-4 to 10^-3 M). Furthermore, the "cutoff effect"—where the anesthetic potency of homologous series like n-alkanols abruptly drops to zero beyond a specific carbon chain length (e.g., n-decanol, C10)—violates the Meyer-Overton lipid solubility rule. The fundamental mystery is how weak, low-affinity, non-covalent van der Waals interactions with highly promiscuous neuronal membrane proteins can trigger a macroscopic, reversible phase transition that completely collapses the integrated information (Φ) of the cortical network, abolishing consciousness without inducing permanent neurotoxicity.

### 4\. THE ANSWER — SEALED READING

### 4.1 Core Synthesis

The question is organized around a mismatch of scale: weak molecular interactions appear to be asked to explain a profound system-level change.

The structural reading is that the missing explanatory link lies between interaction and organization. A molecule need not exert a large force at one site for its presence to alter the operation of a system; equally, a detectable interaction does not by itself explain the system-level consequence. The unresolved task is to specify the intermediate organization that connects the two.

This separates three levels:

* Local interaction: the drug changes the behavior of one or more molecular components.

* Collective organization: those component-level changes alter the patterns of signaling and coordination available to a neural network.

* System-level outcome: the network no longer sustains the activity associated with conscious responsiveness, while retaining the capacity to return when the drug's effects subside.

The levels are connected, but they are not interchangeable explanations. A molecular target is not yet a network mechanism; a network change is not, by itself, a complete account of consciousness.

The question's most consequential assumption is its proposed “macroscopic, reversible phase transition” and the complete collapse of integrated information . Those are possible ways of framing the problem, but they are not established merely by the stated correlations, affinities, or cutoff effect. The reading therefore treats them as hypotheses to examine, not as results already obtained.

### 4.2 Conventional Connection

The conventional conceptual counterpart is a multiscale mechanism: molecular actions influence cellular excitability and synaptic communication; those effects influence circuit dynamics; and the circuit changes relate to behavioral and experiential endpoints. Different anesthetics may enter this chain at different molecular points while producing overlapping outcomes.

This connection supplies a map of the explanatory levels. It does not, by itself, settle which molecular targets are necessary or sufficient, whether the network undergoes a phase transition, or whether collapses to zero.

### 4.3 Practical Bridge

A useful way to structure the problem is to require every proposed mechanism to state its bridge explicitly:

* Molecular level: What interaction or target is changed?

* Cellular level: What measurable property of the cell changes because of it?

* Circuit level: What pattern of communication or coordination changes?

* Organism level: Which endpoint changes—responsiveness, memory formation, pain processing, or another outcome?

* Recovery: What reverses, on what timescale, and what remains intact?

This prevents a binding result from being treated as a complete explanation and prevents a system-level correlation from being treated as proof of a specific molecular cause.

### 4.4 Historical Lineage

The question places the Meyer–Overton relationship beside later target-based pharmacology. Structurally, this is a shift from a broad physicochemical regularity toward increasingly specific accounts of molecular action. The cutoff effect challenges any account that treats lipid solubility as a sufficient rule for potency across all compounds.

The reading does not resolve this history into “lipids versus proteins.” The two may occupy different levels of the same explanatory chain, and the evidence must determine which role each plays for each anesthetic.

### 4.5 Implications

* Weak binding is not a complete mechanism. Its explanatory significance depends on the functional effect and its place in the larger system.

* A shared outcome need not imply a single molecular route. Different agents could produce overlapping system-level changes by acting at different points.

* Reversibility is part of the explanation. A mechanism must account for the return of function, not only its loss.

* A phase-transition account has extra commitments. It needs a defined state variable, a specified transition, and observations that distinguish it from a continuous change in network dynamics.

* Consciousness must not be collapsed into one proxy. Behavioral unresponsiveness, memory, nociceptive processing, and subjective experience are related questions, but they are not identical measurements.

### 4.6 Takeaway

The missing bridge is the organization that carries local molecular effects into a reversible change in neural-system function. A satisfactory account must explain the molecular route, the intermediate cellular and circuit changes, the resulting loss of responsiveness, and recovery—without assuming in advance that a single phase transition or a complete collapse of has been demonstrated.

### 8.1 Consensus lane

Hansen, Mechanisms of General Anesthesia — Annual Review of Biochemistry, June 2025. Review article; molecular and membrane mechanisms.

The review describes anesthetic action at both ion-channel sites and the plasma membrane. It discusses protein binding, regulatory lipids, and the Meyer–Overton relationship, while retaining chain-length cutoff and stereoselectivity as unresolved questions. This supports the sealed reading's separation of local interaction from system-level effect; it does not supply one universal mechanism for all anesthetics.

Hu et al., Neural Network Mechanisms Underlying General Anesthesia — Neuroscience Bulletin, 2024. Review of cortical and subcortical circuits.

The review describes evidence involving cortico-cortical and thalamocortical networks while stating that the precise mechanism remains unresolved. It extends the network-organization direction of the reading but does not show that one particular network transition is the complete explanation.

Mashour, Anesthesia and the Neurobiology of Consciousness — Neuron, 2024. Review of anesthesia, sensory processing, large-scale networks, arousal, and theories of consciousness.

The review treats anesthesia as a way to investigate consciousness while identifying open questions about network dynamics and emergence. It supports keeping the loss of responsiveness, the neural mechanisms, and the question of experience conceptually distinct.

### 8.2 Foundations lane

Meyer–Overton relationship. The broad relationship between lipid solubility and anesthetic potency is an important historical regularity, but it does not establish that the lipid bilayer is the sole site of action. An educational review updated in February 2026 summarizes exceptions involving cutoff effects, bulky compounds, and stereospecificity. Its design is a clinical educational synthesis, not a new primary experiment.

Protein and lipid sites. Hansen's 2025 review describes specific sites within ion channels and membrane-associated lipid mechanisms. It discusses a proposed chain-length cutoff mechanism involving a constrained binding pocket in phospholipase D2 (PLD2), alongside mechanisms involving TREK-1 and regulatory lipids. This makes the cutoff effect a possible clue about the geometry and environment of particular sites, rather than a simple refutation of every lipid-related mechanism.

Binding affinity needs careful interpretation. A quoted is not the same quantity as a concentration producing a functional effect. Neither one, on its own, determines whether a target is causally important in vivo. The numerical range in the question should therefore be treated as a characterization of the particular targets and measurements it describes, not as a universal value for all anesthetic–target pairs.

### 8.3 Counter-evidence lane

The cutoff and stereospecificity challenge a lipid-only explanation. The 2026 educational summary describes compounds whose potency does not follow a simple monotonic relationship with lipophilicity, as well as stereoisomers with different potencies. Those findings are difficult to reconcile with an account in which only nonspecific partitioning into a uniform lipid environment matters. They are compatible with more specific interactions, but do not establish a single universal protein target.

The phase-transition hypothesis has a history, but is not a complete answer. Steyn-Ross and colleagues' 2004 review presents a theoretical model of anesthesia as a first-order phase transition in population-average cortical membrane voltage. It describes predictions involving EEG fluctuations and hysteresis between induction and emergence. This is a substantive phase-transition proposal; it does not demonstrate that every anesthetic operates through one identical molecular route, or that the measured transition is literally a collapse of .

Integrated Information Theory remains contested. Cerullo's 2015 PLOS Computational Biology perspective argues that is not adequately justified as a sufficient measure of consciousness. This is a theoretical critique rather than a consensus statement, but it is a relevant challenge to treating a value of as equivalent to the presence or absence of experience.
