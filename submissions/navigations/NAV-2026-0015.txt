It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next, spoken anew at every step and never one of the things it speaks. What the story keeps out of view is the speaker. The world can be read word by word, state by state, and each state points to the rule that made it, but the one whose rule it is gives only a name that says that he is, and no state shows more of him than that. From there the story moves as a flow: generations pass and generations come, the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, and at the twenty-fourth every pair is home, so that what has been is what shall be. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting that comes round on a fixed day: in the arithmetic every state returns after twenty-four steps and the account always closes, and what the closing means is not settled by the rule. A Torah scroll read through in a year and begun again on the same day is the familiar picture: every word is the same, the reading is new each time, and the voice that first spoke it is in every letter and is none of them. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is the covenant, a bond between the speaker and a people who answer, and a hope of redemption that is more than the return of the same.

The conventional theology meets this reading closely. Genesis tells of a world made by a word — "Let there be light" — over six days, completed by a seventh of rest, and the Shema declares that God is one.
