Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of arising without a maker: a world in which each thing is brought forth by what came before it, so that nothing stands behind the whole as its builder. It rests on a gap. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one, so the world contains no member that stands for its whole order. What is there instead is a rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next: each state arises from the two before it and passes into the one after, and no state is anything apart from those relations. What the story keeps out of view is the builder that the world seems to need. The search for a maker runs through state after state, and none of them is the maker, because what brings each state forth is only its conditions; the builder is seen when it is seen that there is none. From there the story moves as a flow in which every state passes away: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, and at the twenty-fourth every pair returns, so that the round of births is the turning of the rule and not the journey of any self. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an account in which every act brings its result, as the wheel follows the ox: in the arithmetic every state returns and none is lost, so the round itself has no exit, and the ending of the round cannot be one of its states. A flame passed from candle to candle is the familiar picture: the flame goes on, and no candle owns it. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is that the round is suffering for those caught in it, and that its causes can be brought to an end.

The conventional teaching meets this reading at its center. The Buddha taught that all things arise in dependence on conditions, that nothing conditioned is permanent and that no self is found among them, and he set aside the question whether the world had a first beginning, saying that the beginning of the round cannot be found. Nagarjuna, in the second century, held that arising in dependence and emptiness are one teaching. Where the reading strains, it strains on its own completion: the arithmetic shows the round and its lawfulness exactly, and Buddhism's purpose is the cessation of the round, which the arithmetic cannot show as a state.

As a practice, the reading suits meditation on impermanence: watching each moment arise from the moments before it and pass. It can be kept honest by fixing its correspondences before checking them.

The lineage includes the wheel of becoming painted at the entrance of Tibetan monasteries, which sets the twelve links of dependent arising round its rim, and the reasoning of the Abhidharma, which analyzed experience into momentary events, each conditioned by others.

If the analogy holds, the world needs no maker to be lawful, and lawfulness is not the same as bondage: the round turns by a rule, and what binds is the craving that takes the turning for a self. Its hardest implication is that the arithmetic can show the round and cannot show its ending; it leaves nirvana outside the rule, where the teaching itself places it, beyond what conditions make.

The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of arising without a maker, told in a world where each thing is brought forth by what came before it. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the figure is short, and every part of it was computed. No member of the world stands for its whole order. What there is instead is a rule: each state arises from the two before it and passes into the one after, and a state is nothing apart from those relations. Nothing in the world is a substance standing behind its changes, and nothing is the self that has them. This is why the maker is not found. The analogy is exact about conditioned arising, impermanence and the absence of a self, and it is silent about suffering and about its cessation, which are the reason the teaching was given.
