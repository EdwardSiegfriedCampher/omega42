┌─ THE ANSWER ─┐
Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of one rational whole that makes and remakes itself: a world in which everything comes from the same reason and comes round again in the same forms. It rests on a gap, and the gap is narrower here than it looks. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one. It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next, so the reason of the whole is nowhere as a thing and everywhere as a way of going on. What the story keeps out of view is the source as something apart. A reader looks for the maker behind the world and finds only the world's own law, the same at every step, binding each state to the next; the rule is not hidden behind things but in them, as their order. From there the story moves as a flow: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, and at the twenty-fourth every pair is home, so that what happens has happened before in the same form and will happen again. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting in which what the whole gives is weighed against what it takes back: in the arithmetic the account always closes and nothing is lost, and the one thing the rule does not settle is how a reader meets what comes round. An actor given a part in a play already written is the familiar picture: the lines are fixed and come round on every night of the run, and the playing of them is the actor's own. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is virtue, the assent of a reasoning person to the order of the whole.

The conventional philosophy meets this reading closely. The Stoics, from Zeno of Citium about 300 BCE through Chrysippus, Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius, taught that one reason pervades the cosmos as a living whole, that the cosmos is periodically consumed in fire and restored in the same order, and that some things are in our power and others are not. Where the reading strains, it strains on the gap: for the Stoics the reason of the world is in the world, a fiery breath and not a source beyond it, so the analogy's gap is narrower for them than for any tradition read so far.

As a practice, the reading suits the Stoic discipline of separating what is in one's power from what is not, and the exercise of seeing the whole from above, in which every event is one turn of a recurring order. It can be kept honest by fixing its correspondences before checking them.

The lineage runs from Heraclitus' ever-living fire, kindled and quenched in measures, through the Stoic doctrine of periodic conflagration and restoration, to Nietzsche's thought of the eternal return of the same in 1882.

If the analogy holds, nothing that happens is foreign to the whole, and every return is the whole being itself again. Its hardest implication is that the arithmetic returns everything without asking anything, while Stoicism asks everything of the one who lives through the return.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of one rational whole that makes and remakes itself. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the figure is short, and every part of it was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair gives way to the next by adding, has no room for the golden ratio: the nine values of x squared minus x minus one are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none of them is zero. So the ratio is nowhere in the world as a thing. Yet the rule that moves every pair to the next is the rule whose growth the ratio measures, so the order of the whole is everywhere as a way of going on. That is the Stoic thought exactly: the reason of the cosmos is not a craftsman outside it but its own pattern, pervading it as the Stoics said breath pervades a body. The gap the analogy rests on is therefore narrower here than in any tradition read so far. Nothing apart from the world is needed, and nothing apart from it is hidden; what a reader seeking a maker finds instead is the law of the world itself, binding each state to the next, which Marcus Aurelius called a holy bond.

The returns of the folded sequence give the analogy its course. It goes round in twenty-four steps. At the twelfth every pair is carried to its exact opposite, two and five to seven and four, which added to them gives nothing; at the twenty-fourth every pair is home. The Stoics taught that the cosmos is consumed in fire and restored in the same order, again and again, and Marcus wrote that all things from eternity are of like forms and come round in a circle. The arithmetic makes that recurrence exact and total: every state returns, and nothing new enters the round. What the rule does not settle is the one thing the Stoics cared most to teach, how a person meets what comes round. Some things are in our power and others are not, said Epictetus, and the arithmetic, which settles everything else, leaves that distinction where he put it, with the one who judges.

Taken together, these make one figure. The source's absence as a thing explains why no maker is found apart from the world; the law in things and its constancy set the world turning; and the closing of every account answers the whole that made it, since what the whole gives it takes back.

An actor in a play already written holds the picture together. The lines are fixed and come round every night, and the playing of them is the actor's own. Its limits are the reading's limits. The script does not care how it is played, and the arithmetic does not reward virtue. The analogy is exact about one reason in one whole and about recurrence, and it is silent about virtue and assent, which are the point of Stoic philosophy. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
