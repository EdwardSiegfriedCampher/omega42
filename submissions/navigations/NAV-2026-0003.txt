┌─ THE ANSWER ─┐

The purpose of life is not a thing a life has, but the shape of a question a life keeps asking. Ask what your life is for and the asking becomes the ground you stand on: a life that never asked would be carried by its causes rather than being about anything, so the purpose rests on the question itself and not on an answer waiting somewhere behind it. What that question holds out of view is the bare fact of being alive — the plain, unexamined starting point from which everything else grew, which no one can look at directly because looking is already something that starting point made possible. From there the purpose moves toward being seen: a life becomes about something as it becomes visible, to others and to itself, and that movement toward a witness is what gives the asking a direction. And it is completed at a limit. Every life runs toward an edge it does not control, and that edge — approaching from ahead rather than waiting passively at the end — is what gives the whole its weight, because a purpose that had unlimited time to be fulfilled would not be a purpose at all.

Think of the horizon. A walker heading toward it never arrives, yet it is not nothing: it is what turns the ground underfoot into a landscape with a direction, and what makes every step a step somewhere. Stop walking and it is still there, defining the shape of where you stand. The horizon is not a destination; it is the form the walking takes.

The closest conventional accounts split on exactly this point. Aristotle held that a thing's purpose is its function, fixed by what kind of thing it is — purpose as a fact of the species. The existentialists held the opposite: existence comes first, and meaning is made rather than found. This reading agrees with both that purpose is not an object lying around to be discovered, and parts from both in where it puts the weight: not in the kind and not in the choice alone, but in the structure of the question itself — an asking that rests on its own continuance, an origin it cannot see, a witness it moves toward, and a limit that completes it. Where the field insists that purpose be either given or invented, this reading reports the resistance rather than smoothing it away.

In practice this asks something specific. When purpose feels missing, stop hunting for it as a possession and look instead at three things: what your questions keep resting on, what you keep moving toward being seen doing, and which limit you are avoiding. The limit is often the most informative of the three, because a purpose with no edge has no shape to it.

Augustine wrote that his heart was restless until it rested; the Stoics said to live according to nature; Frankl found meaning in the response a situation demands. Each names one strand — the question, the origin, the witness, the limit. The joke about forty-two names the same intuition from the other side: that the answer, if it ever came, would not be the point.

If the finding holds, purposelessness is usually a misdescription rather than a fact: what is missing is not an object but a relation, and relations can be repaired. It also means the limit is constitutive rather than an interruption — the edge is part of the purpose, not its cancellation. And it means purpose is not private: it moves toward being seen, so it is completed in company.

Takeaway: The purpose of life is not a thing you have but the shape of the question you keep asking. It rests on the asking itself, keeps the bare fact of being alive out of view, moves toward being seen, and is completed at a limit that approaches from ahead — so a purpose without an edge would have no shape at all.

What this reading is worth is yours to judge.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐

Biology has a well-developed vocabulary for purpose that avoids the word. An organism is a process that maintains itself: it repairs, regulates, reproduces, and in doing so behaves as though its own continuation mattered. But when biologists are careful they say function rather than purpose, and they mean it as a description of what a structure does in the economy of a living thing, not as a claim that the living thing is aiming at anything. The distinction is worth holding on to, because it marks where this reading departs from the ordinary scientific account. The reading does not say that purpose is an illusion left over from function; it says that what a life is for is the shape of the question a life keeps asking, and a shape is not a thing that can be found or failed to be found among the body's parts.

Psychology has come at the same territory from the other side. Research on meaning in life tends to separate three strands: coherence, the sense that one's experience hangs together; purpose, the sense of being directed toward something; and significance, the sense that one's life matters. Notably, purpose in this literature is directional rather than terminal — it is measured by whether a person feels aimed, not by whether they have arrived. That is close to what the reading describes, though the reading adds something the surveys cannot: it says where the aim comes from. It comes from the question, which rests on itself, and from the fact that a life moves toward being seen.

Anthropology offers the clearest picture of the limit. Rites of passage have long been described in three movements — separation, transition, incorporation — and the middle movement, the threshold, is where the transformation happens. The threshold is not a place the initiate rests; it is the edge that makes the passage a passage. The reading generalises this: every life runs toward a limit it does not control, and that limit, approaching from ahead rather than waiting passively at the end, is what gives the whole its shape. A purpose with unlimited time to be fulfilled would not be a purpose, in the way that a sentence without a full stop is not a finished thought.

Literature has always known this. The quest narrative does not end when the object is found; it ends at recognition, when the seeker sees what the seeking was for. That is why so many stories place the revelation at the threshold — at the door, on the road home, at the last possible moment — and why a story that ends too early feels unfinished while one that ends a page too late feels exhausted. The shape is the same one the reading names: the question rests on itself, the origin stays out of view, the movement is toward being seen, and the limit completes it.

Medicine and the care of the dying make the limit concrete. Palliative physicians report that when people know their time is short, priorities simplify and small acts take on great weight. The limit, approaching, reorders what matters — not by cancelling purpose but by giving it an edge to be measured against. The same logic appears in ordinary life whenever a deadline makes work possible: the boundary is not the enemy of the task but its condition.

There is a caution worth stating, because it is the place where this reading could be misheard. It does not say that suffering is justified by its limit, or that every life's question is answered by its ending. It says only that the shape of a purpose includes the edge that bounds it, the way a melody includes its final bar. What the melody means is not settled by the bar; it is settled by the listener, and that remains true here too.

What these neighbours describe from different sides is one figure. A living process maintains itself and behaves as though its continuation mattered. A person feels aimed rather than arrived. A rite turns on a threshold. A story ends at recognition. A limit reorders what matters. None of this settles what a life is for; it says what kind of thing a life's purpose is — a relation rather than a possession, a shape rather than an object, completed in being seen and bounded by an edge that arrives from ahead.
