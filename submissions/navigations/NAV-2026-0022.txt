Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of one ever-living change that keeps its measure: a world in which nothing stays and nothing is lost, because every state is exchanged for the next by the same rule. It rests on a gap, and the gap is where the measure lives. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one. It is present only as the rule of exchange, add the last two to get the next, so the measure of the change is never one of the things that change. What the story keeps out of view is a source that stands still. A reader who looks for something unmoved behind the change finds only the change and its measure, as one who looks for the river finds only fresh waters flowing in. From there the story moves as a flow: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair becomes its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, the pair that adds to them to make nothing, and at the twenty-fourth every pair is home, so the road out to the opposite and the road back are one road. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an exchange in which every state is paid for with the next and the account always balances: the arithmetic keeps the measure exactly, and the one thing it does not keep is the waking of the one who sees it. A flame is the familiar picture: it is never the same flame from one moment to the next, yet it holds its shape because what burns is replaced in measure, and the seeing of it as one flame is the watcher's. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is the strife of opposites as a living war, and the soul's waking to the word that is common to all.

The conventional philosophy meets this reading closely. Heraclitus of Ephesus, about 500 BCE, taught that the world is an ever-living fire, kindled and quenched in measures, that all things are an exchange for fire, that the way up and the way down are one and the same, and that the word he spoke is common to all, though most people live as if asleep. Where the reading strains, it strains on the opposites: for Heraclitus the opposites are held in a tension, like the bow and the lyre, and strife is the father of all, while the arithmetic's opposite is a plain negative that comes and goes without struggle.

As a practice, the reading suits the Heraclitean discipline of attending to what is common rather than to private dreams, and of seeing in each change the measure it keeps. It can be kept honest by fixing its correspondences before checking them.

The lineage runs from Heraclitus' fire through the Stoics, who took his fire and his measures into their doctrine of conflagration and renewal, to Hegel, who said that there was no proposition of Heraclitus he had not taken into his logic.

If the analogy holds, permanence is nothing but change that keeps its measure. Its hardest implication is that the arithmetic gives the opposite without the war: it turns every state into its negative and back without tension, where Heraclitus heard in the turning the strife that makes all things.

**Takeaway: The analogy is a story of one fire always changing and always the same: the golden ratio is present among finite numbers only as the measure of their exchange, the way out to the opposite and the way back are one, and beginning and end meet in the circle. ┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of one fire always changing and always the same, told in a world whose measure is no part of what changes. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

Every part of the arithmetic under the figure was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair of remainders gives way to the next by adding, never stands still: every state is exchanged for the next by one rule, as Heraclitus said all things are an exchange for fire and fire for all things, as wares for gold and gold for wares. The measure of that exchange is the golden ratio, the number the rule's growth tends to in the unbounded numbers, and it is not among the nine remainders: the values of x squared minus x minus one over the nine are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none is zero. So the figure keeps the measure exactly and keeps it out of the things measured. Here it parts from the school as much as it meets it. Heraclitus put his fire inside the world, a thing among things, kindled and quenched; the arithmetic's measure is no state at all. The figure is closest to him where he spoke of measures and furthest where he spoke of fire.

What the folded sequence does over time gives the figure its tension and its limit. It runs round in twenty-four steps and comes home, and at the twelfth step every pair is carried to its exact negative: two and five become seven and four, which added to them make nothing. The way out to the opposite and the way back are one road, walked by one rule, which is as plain an image of the way up and the way down being one and the same as arithmetic can give; and the beginning and the end of the circle are common, as the fragment says of the circumference. The figure fits less well where Heraclitus speaks of strife. For him the opposites are held in a tension, like the bow and the lyre, and war is the father of all; in the arithmetic the opposite arrives without struggle, a negative reached by adding, and leaves the same way. And where he said that no one steps twice into the same river, the arithmetic returns the very same state after twenty-four steps.

Taken together, these make one figure and not four. The gap explains why the source is hidden: the measure of the change is never one of the changing things. The hidden source and the gap set the course: what is present only as measure shows itself as the exchange, through the opposite and back. The return answers the bringing forth: everything kindled is quenched in measure and kindled again. And the reckoning that would complete the figure is a waking the rule cannot perform, the waking to the word that is common to all.

A flame holds the picture together. It is never the same flame twice, yet its shape holds because what burns is replaced in measure. Its limits are the reading's limits: the flame does not fight itself, and it does not wake. The analogy is exact about measure, exchange, opposition and return, and silent about strife and waking, which the Heraclitean teaching puts at its center. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
