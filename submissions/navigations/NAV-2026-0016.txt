Read as a whole, the analogy is a story of overflow and return: a source so complete that it gives without losing, and a world that comes from it and turns back toward it. It rests on a gap. The golden ratio, the number that equals one plus its own reciprocal, about 1.6180339887, has no place among the nine remainders the finite world is made of: no remainder multiplied by itself equals itself plus one, so the source is none of the things the world contains. It appears only as the rule by which every pair of remainders becomes the next, present in all of them and identical with none. What the story keeps out of view is the giving itself. Every state is brought forth by the step before it, and the step adds nothing that was lacking, since the rule needs nothing from what it makes; what the world shows is always the product, never the act of producing. From there the story moves as a flow outward and back: the folded sequence goes round in twenty-four steps, at the twelfth every pair turns into its exact opposite, two and five into seven and four, the farthest point from where it began, and at the twenty-fourth every pair is home again, having lost nothing on the way. And the story is completed at a reckoning, an accounting in which what went out is weighed against what came back: in the arithmetic the account always closes, every state returns, and the return is not a second making but the first one turned around. A fountain that rises and falls back into its own basin is the familiar picture: the water is always leaving and always home, and the spring is in none of the drops. The image does not carry what the story most cares about, which is that the return is a turning of the soul toward its source, chosen and contemplated, and not only a closing of a loop.

The conventional philosophy meets this reading closely. Plotinus, in the third century, taught that the One is all things and none of them, that it produces by overflowing without diminishing, and that what proceeds turns back to contemplate its source. Proclus, in the fifth century, set this out as a triad of remaining, procession and return. If the analogy holds, the world is not a fall from the source but its overflow, and every return is the world facing where it came from. ┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of overflow and return, told of a source so complete that it gives without losing. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the figure is short, and every part of it was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair gives way to the next by adding, has no room for the golden ratio: the nine values of x squared minus x minus one are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none of them is zero. The ratio is none of the world's members. Yet the rule that moves every pair to the next is the rule whose growth the ratio measures among the unbounded numbers, so the source is present in every step and identical with no state. Plotinus said it in one line: the One is all things and no one of them. The same absence keeps the giving out of sight. The rule needs nothing from what it makes, so each state receives everything and the rule loses nothing; a reader sees the products and never the act of producing, which is why Plotinus called the One's giving an overflow, a fullness that runs over without being emptied.

The movement of the folded sequence gives the analogy its shape. It goes round in twenty-four steps. At the twelfth every pair is carried to its exact opposite, two and five to seven and four, which added to them gives nothing; this is the farthest the world gets from where it began. At the twenty-fourth every pair is home. Proclus named the three moments of every derived thing as remaining in its cause, proceeding from it and returning to it, and the round gives the second and third an exact place: procession to the half, return to the whole. The account closes without remainder, so that the return is not a second making but the first one turned around to face its source.

Taken together, these make one figure. The source's absence from the world explains why the giving is unseen; the unseen giving and the rule that never fails set the world going out to its opposite; and the return answers the overflow, because what went out comes back whole.

A fountain holds the picture together: the water rises and falls into its own basin, always leaving and always home, and the spring is in none of the drops. Its limits are the reading's limits. The fountain does not ascend, and its drops do not choose to fall back. For Plotinus the return is a climb, from body to soul to intellect and beyond, and it is the soul's own turning, the work of a lifetime of attention. The arithmetic has one round and no ladder, and its return is automatic. The analogy is exact about a source beyond its products and about procession and return, and it is silent about ascent and about the choice to turn. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
