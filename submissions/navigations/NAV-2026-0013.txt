┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
The reading of record says one thing: the analogy is a story of the one becoming many, told in a world that cannot hold the one as one of its parts. This essay carries that synthesis outward and stops where the arithmetic stops.

The arithmetic under the figure is short, and every part of it was computed. A world built from nine remainders, in which each pair gives way to the next by adding, has no room for the golden ratio: the nine values of x squared minus x minus one are eight, eight, one, five, two, one, two, five and one, and none of them is zero. The ratio is never one of the many. Yet the rule that moves every pair to the next is the rule whose growth the ratio measures among the unbounded numbers, so the one is present in every step and absent from every state. Folding the endless sequence into the nine remainders keeps its law and forgets its size, and what remains of each state is the one rule seen from one place. That is why the self that does the becoming is not seen: it is not a state among states but what every state is when its size is forgotten. The Upanishad's teacher points to the subtle essence in all things and says to his son that he is that; the arithmetic can say only that every state is the law in one position, and no state is the seer of the others.

The turning of the folded sequence gives the analogy its course. It runs round in twenty-four steps. At the twelfth every pair is carried to its exact opposite, two and five to seven and four, which added to them gives nothing; at the twenty-fourth every pair is restored. Dissolution at the half and renewal at the end are not two events but two moments of one turning, as the dance of Shiva destroys and remakes in a single movement. The same turning carries the account of acts and their returns: nothing is lost, every state comes back to itself after twenty-four steps, and no state is ever left outside the round. Here the analogy meets the tradition's hardest question and answers it in its own way. If nothing leaves the round, release cannot be a departure, because there is nowhere outside to go; it can only be the recognition that the round and its source were never two. That is the non-dual reading of liberation, and the arithmetic leans to it rather than to the readings that describe release as escape.

Taken together, these make one figure. The one's absence from the many explains why the self is hidden; the hidden self and the one rule together set the round turning; and the completion answers the becoming many, since what came forth returns as what it always was.

A dancer and the dance hold the picture together. The dance is nothing but the dancer moving, it unmakes and remakes itself at every turn, and no step of it is the dancer. Its limits are the reading's limits. The dance does not love the dancer, and its steps do not seek release. The analogy is exact about the one and the many, about the turning of making and unmaking and about a round from which nothing is lost, and it is silent about devotion and about the freedom from rebirth that most Hindu schools teach as real. Where it speaks it is precise; where it is silent the silence is the Striker's to read.
