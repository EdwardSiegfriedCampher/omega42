Ω42 — A 3-adic Dynamical Structure

Revised Mathematical Manuscript, Version 2

Abstract

We study the linear dynamical system generated by

[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\1&1\end{pmatrix}
]

on the finite modules ((\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2) and on their inverse limit (\mathbb Z_3^2). We establish an exact classification of finite orbits, identify a quadratic norm invariant, characterize the closures of nonzero 3-adic orbits, and show that the induced projective dynamics is minimal and uniquely ergodic. We also examine the scalar cocycle arising from a projective section and prove that it cannot be made constant by a continuous change of section. Finally, we distinguish the ergodic properties of individual invariant shells from those of the full state space.

The results establish a coherent arithmetic and dynamical structure. They do not, without further premises, establish that this structure is physically fundamental or ontologically necessary.

1. Foundational definition

Let

[
R_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2
]

and define

[
T:R_k\to R_k,\qquad T(a,b)=(b,a+b).
]

In matrix form,

[
T(x)=Ax,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
]

The determinant and characteristic polynomial are

[
\det A=-1,
\qquad
p(t)=t^2-t-1.
]

The matrix is invertible at every finite level and on the inverse limit.

The compatible inverse-limit system is

[
X=\mathbb Z_3^2,\qquad T:X\to X.
]

The inverse limit is a 3-adic state space. It is not a real continuum, and its elements should not be interpreted as ordinary real-valued coordinates.

The matrix identity

[
A^2=A+I
]

implies the Fibonacci representation

[
A^n=
\begin{pmatrix}
F_{n-1}&F_n\
F_n&F_{n+1}
\end{pmatrix},
]

where (F_0=0), (F_1=1).

In particular,

[
T^n(0,1)=(F_n,F_{n+1}).
]

This gives a direct link between the Ω42 recurrence and Fibonacci arithmetic.

2. The finite orbit theorem

2.1 The first residue level

Modulo (3), the polynomial (t^2-t-1) is irreducible. Therefore

[
\mathbb F_3[t]/(t^2-t-1)\cong\mathbb F_9.
]

Under this identification, (A) acts as multiplication by a root (\alpha) of the polynomial.

The multiplicative group (\mathbb F_9^\times) has order eight, and (\alpha) has order eight. Consequently, every nonzero vector modulo (3) has exact period eight.

2.2 Lifting the period

Direct computation gives

[
A^8=I+3B,
\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}4&7\7&11\end{pmatrix},
]

with

[
\det B=-5\equiv1\pmod3.
]

Thus (B) is invertible modulo (3).

For (q=3^s r), with (3\nmid r), the binomial expansion yields

[
A^{8q}=I+3^{s+1}B_s,
\qquad
B_s\equiv rB\pmod3.
]

The remaining terms are divisible by (3^{s+2}). For a primitive vector (u), invertibility of (B_s) modulo (3) gives

[
v_3((A^{8q}-I)u)=s+1.
]

It follows that a primitive vector modulo (3^m) has exact period

[
\boxed{L_m=8\cdot3^{m-1}}.
]

If (x=3^j u) is nonzero modulo (3^k), with (u) primitive and (m=k-j), then

[
\boxed{\operatorname{per}(x)=8\cdot3^{k-j-1}}.
]

2.3 Orbit count

The number of primitive vectors modulo (3^m) is

[
3^{2m}-3^{2m-2}=8\cdot3^{2m-2}.
]

Dividing by the exact period gives

[
3^{m-1}
]

orbits at scale (m).

Hence the complete classification at level (k) is:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&3^{m-1}\text{ orbits of length }8\cdot3^{m-1},
\[-2pt]
&\hspace{45mm}m=1,\ldots,k,
\
&\text{together with the zero fixed point.}
\end{aligned}
}
]

The total number of orbits is

[
1+\sum_{m=1}^k3^{m-1}
=\boxed{\frac{3^k+1}{2}}.
]

The matrix order is

[
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(A)=8\cdot3^{k-1}},
]

and the antipodal identity is

[
\boxed{A^{4\cdot3^{k-1}}=-I\pmod{3^k}}.
]

At level (k=2), the state space has 81 elements: three orbits of length 24, one orbit of length eight, and the zero fixed point.

3. Quadratic norm representation

Let

[
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
\qquad
K=\mathbb Q_3(\phi),
\qquad
\mathcal O=\mathbb Z_3[\phi].
]

Because (t^2-t-1) is irreducible modulo (3), (K/\mathbb Q_3) is an unramified quadratic extension with residue field (\mathbb F_9).

Identify

[
(a,b)\longleftrightarrow a+b\phi.
]

Multiplication by (\phi) is represented by (A), since

[
\phi(a+b\phi)=b+(a+b)\phi.
]

The field norm is

[
N(a+b\phi)=a^2+ab-b^2.
]

Since (N(\phi)=-1), define

[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2.
]

Then

[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x),
]

and therefore

[
\boxed{I(x)=Q(x)^2}
]

is invariant under the dynamics.

The norm is anisotropic because (K) is a field:

[
Q(x)=0\iff x=0.
]

4. Dense norm subgroup and exact orbit closures

Define

[
G={u\in\mathcal O^\times:N(u)\in{1,-1}}.
]

We first establish

[
\boxed{\overline{\langle\phi\rangle}=G}.
]

Modulo (3), the element (\phi) generates (\mathbb F_9^\times), a group of order eight.

The principal norm-one subgroup is

[
G_1={u\in1+3\mathcal O:N(u)=1}.
]

The 3-adic logarithm identifies (1+3\mathcal O) with (3\mathcal O). Under this identification, (G_1) corresponds to the trace-zero submodule, which has rank one over (\mathbb Z_3).

Now

[
\phi^8=1+3\beta,
\qquad
\beta=4+7\phi.
]

We have

[
\operatorname{Tr}(\beta)=15\equiv0\pmod3
]

and

[
\beta\equiv1+\phi\ne0\pmod3.
]

Thus

[
\frac{\log(\phi^8)}{3}\equiv\beta\pmod3
]

is a nonzero generator of the trace-zero module modulo (3). It follows that the powers of (\phi^8) are dense in (G_1). Combining this with the order-eight residue action proves the density of (\langle\phi\rangle) in (G).

Orbit-closure theorem

For every nonzero (x\in\mathbb Z_3^2),

[
\overline{{A^n x:n\in\mathbb Z}}=xG.
]

If (y\in xG), then (Q(y)=\pm Q(x)), and hence (Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2).

Conversely, if

[
Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2,
]

the integral-domain property of (\mathbb Z_3) gives

[
Q(y)=Q(x)\quad\text{or}\quad Q(y)=-Q(x).
]

The quotient (y/x) in (K) is then a unit of norm (1) or (-1), so it belongs to (G).

Therefore

[
\boxed{
\overline{{A^n x:n\in\mathbb Z}}

{y\in\mathbb Z_3^2:Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2}.
}
]

The nonzero orbit closures are exactly the invariant quadratic shells.

5. Finite and infinite norm geometry agree

Let

[
\mathcal O_m=\mathcal O/3^m\mathcal O.
]

Its unit group has order

[
|\mathcal O_m^\times|=8\cdot3^{2m-2}.
]

The norm map onto ((\mathbb Z/3^m\mathbb Z)^\times) is surjective. This follows from the surjective residue norm and the surjective trace maps governing successive principal-unit lifts.

The norm target has order (2\cdot3^{m-1}). Consequently,

[
|{u\in\mathcal O_m^\times:N(u)=\pm1}|
=8\cdot3^{m-1}.
]

The image of (\langle\phi\rangle) lies in this subgroup and has exactly the same order by the finite-period theorem. The two groups coincide.

Thus the finite orbit classification is precisely the finite-level version of the norm-group description.

For primitive vectors, equality of the squared quadratic invariant modulo (3^m) implies equality up to sign of the quadratic norm. One of the factors

[
Q(u)-Q(v),\qquad Q(u)+Q(v)
]

must be a unit, so the other must vanish modulo (3^m). The norm-(\pm1) subgroup equality then places the vectors in the same finite orbit.

For a vector (x=3^j u), the normalized invariant at its effective level (m=k-j) is

[
Q(u)^2\pmod{3^m}.
]

This normalization is necessary because the unnormalized invariant contains the scale factor (3^{4j}).

6. Projective dynamics

The projective state space is

[
P=\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3).
]

For a fixed primitive vector (x), the map

[
G/{\pm1}\longrightarrow P,
\qquad
[g]\longmapsto[xg]
]

is a homeomorphism.

Surjectivity follows by adjusting the norm of any unit representative with a scalar in (\mathbb Z_3^\times). The two square classes of 3-adic units are represented by (1) and (-1), so the adjusted norm can be made (1) or (-1).

The kernel consists of scalars satisfying (c^2=\pm1). Since (-1) is not a square in (\mathbb Z_3^\times), the kernel is precisely ({\pm1}).

Projective dynamics is therefore conjugate to multiplication by ([\phi]) on (G/{\pm1}).

Because (\langle\phi\rangle) is dense in (G), the projective system is minimal and uniquely ergodic.

At finite level,

[
|\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)|
=4\cdot3^{k-1},
]

which is also the exact projective period.

7. Scale, direction and scalar cocycle

Every nonzero state has a unique decomposition

[
x=3^j c,\sigma(p),
]

where (j\geq0), (c\in\mathbb Z_3^\times), (p\in P), and (\sigma) is the normalized projective section.

The two clopen charts

[
U_0={[a:b]:a\in\mathbb Z_3^\times},
\qquad
U_1={[a:b]:a\in3\mathbb Z_3,\ b\in\mathbb Z_3^\times}
]

give the section

[
\sigma([a:b])=
\begin{cases}
(1,b/a),&[a:b]\in U_0,\
(a/b,1),&[a:b]\in U_1.
\end{cases}
]

The dynamics becomes

[
A\sigma(p)=\kappa(p)\sigma(f(p)),
]

so

[
j'=j,\qquad p'=f(p),\qquad c'=c\kappa(p).
]

The scalar factor is therefore a cocycle over the projective rotation.

Nontriviality under continuous section changes

Suppose there were a continuous (h:P\to\mathbb Z_3^\times) and a constant (\lambda\in\mathbb Z_3^\times) satisfying

[
\kappa(p)=\lambda\frac{h(f(p))}{h(p)}.
]

Continuity and compactness imply that the reductions of (h) and (\sigma) modulo (3) factor through some sufficiently high finite projective quotient, say level (k).

The projective period at that level is

[
L=4\cdot3^{k-1},
]

and

[
A^L=-I\pmod{3^k}.
]

Iterating the section relation gives

[
\kappa_L(p)\equiv-1\pmod3.
]

But the assumed coboundary representation telescopes:

[
\kappa_L(p)
=\lambda^L\frac{h(f^L(p))}{h(p)}.
]

The quotient of (h)-values is (1) modulo (3), while (L) is even and (\mathbb F_3^\times={\pm1}). Hence

[
\kappa_L(p)\equiv1\pmod3,
]

a contradiction.

Therefore

[
\boxed{\kappa\text{ is not continuously cohomologous to a constant}.}
]

This proves a genuine obstruction to removing the scalar extension by continuous section changes. It does not by itself establish randomness or chaos.

8. Invariant shells and spectral structure

Every nonzero orbit closure is a compact abelian group coset (xG). The dynamics on it is conjugate to multiplication by (\phi).

Each nonzero shell is therefore:

- Minimal.
- Uniquely ergodic.
- Equipped with a unique Haar invariant probability measure.
- Pure point in its Koopman spectrum on the corresponding Haar (L^2) space.

The projective system has the same properties, with group (G/{\pm1}).

The full space (X=\mathbb Z_3^2), however, is not uniquely ergodic. The origin supports the invariant measure (\delta_0), while each nonzero shell supports a different Haar invariant measure.

Thus the correct conclusions are:

Domain| Minimality| Unique invariant probability| Spectral conclusion
Individual nonzero shell| Yes| Yes| Pure point
Projective space| Yes| Yes| Pure point
Full state space| No| No| Depends on chosen invariant measure

Invariant probability measures on the full space can be described by decomposing over the invariant shell parameter, with Haar measure on each shell and a possible mass at the origin. No global pure-point conclusion is asserted without specifying the measure.

9. Mathematical conclusions and foundational limits

The finite and 3-adic results are mutually consistent. The same quadratic arithmetic explains:

1. The order-eight residue dynamics.
2. The exact growth of finite periods.
3. The antipodal return.
4. The quadratic invariant.
5. The 3-adic orbit closures.
6. The projective minimal rotation.
7. The scalar cocycle obstruction.

This is a substantial internal mathematical structure.

It does not yet prove that the modulus (3), the quadratic field, or the two-dimensional state space are uniquely required by a minimal principle of existence. Nor does it establish that the formal system is a physical model of the universe.

The distinction is essential: a mathematical system can be internally unified without being ontologically fundamental.

10. Most plausible explanation

The most plausible explanation of the Ω42 results is that they arise from a single arithmetic mechanism: multiplication by a quadratic unit in an unramified extension of the 3-adic field.

The finite recurrence, the norm invariant and the projective dynamics are different descriptions of this mechanism at different levels.

The scalar cocycle identifies information retained by the vector dynamics but removed by projectivization. The full state space is a family of invariant shells rather than one uniquely ergodic dynamical component.

The theory therefore has a coherent mathematical core. Its claim to explain existence remains open.

11. Recommended way forward

The next foundational task is to investigate whether the model's defining choices can be derived rather than stipulated.

The investigation should distinguish:

- Why a two-coordinate state space?
- Why an invertible linear recurrence?
- Why the characteristic polynomial (t^2-t-1)?
- Why the prime (3)?
- Why the quadratic norm condition?
- Which of these choices follow from a minimal set of constitutive principles, and which are additional model assumptions?

A rigorous selection theorem would be a major advance. It would need to specify a class of admissible systems and prove that Ω42 is selected uniquely, or state precisely which optimization criterion selects it.

The next mathematical development should therefore be a minimal constitutive reconstruction theorem: begin with explicitly stated structural principles and determine which parts of Ω42 follow necessarily, which follow conditionally, and which remain free choices.

Empirical consequences and possible tests belong in the separate Scientific Appendix, after the mathematical derivations have been completed.
