# Ω42 — A Mathematical Framework of Distinction, Recurrence and Arithmetic Structure
## Version 3 — Consolidated mathematical manuscript

**Status:** Revised mathematical consolidation
**Scope:** Foundational structure, canonical transformation, finite and 3-adic dynamics, quadratic arithmetic, prime classification, and state-orbit theory.

## Abstract

Ω42 is developed here as a mathematical framework centred on a two-dimensional integer transformation,

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\]

acting on finite residue spaces and their 3-adic inverse limit.

The canonical finite system is

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2,
\qquad
T(a,b)=(b,a+b).
\]

At level \(k=2\), this gives an 81-state system with 24-step global recurrence and antipodal return at step 12.

The manuscript develops the exact finite orbit structure, its 3-adic extension, the associated quadratic norm, projective dynamics, and connections with Fibonacci substitution and hyperbolic toral dynamics.

It then places the canonical system within the prime arithmetic of the quadratic field \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\). Split, inert and ramified primes produce three distinct algebraic mechanisms for recurrence. General lifting theorems explain when matrix orders and antipodal returns persist through prime powers. Individual state periods are classified in the inert, split and ramified cases.

The mathematical results support a conditional minimality argument for the choice of Ω42. They do not establish that this formalism is ontologically fundamental or that it explains existence itself. That remains a separate foundational problem.

# Part I — Foundational principles and the limits of selection

## 1. The question of existence and the role of mathematical structure

A mathematical framework can describe relations, transformations, invariants and recurrence. It does not follow that it explains why anything exists.

We therefore distinguish three levels:

1. **Formal description:** specifying a mathematical structure and its properties.
2. **Structural explanation:** deriving one mathematical or physical feature from more basic principles.
3. **Ontological explanation:** establishing why actuality or existence obtains at all.

Ω42 has developed substantially at the first level and has begun to establish conditional explanatory results at the second.

The third level remains open.

This distinction is fundamental to the project. A mathematically rich structure is not, merely by being rich, an explanation of existence.

## 2. Distinction and relational structure

A minimal relational construction can begin with a set \(X\) and formal differences

\[
e_{xy},
\]

subject to

\[
e_{xx}=0,
\qquad
e_{xy}+e_{yz}=e_{xz}.
\]

This provides a coherent additive description of differences between elements.

For finite \(X\), the resulting free abelian difference group has rank \(|X|-1\).

However, these assumptions do not uniquely select a two-dimensional recurrence, a modulus, or a particular integer matrix.

They demonstrate how relational structure can be constructed; they do not derive Ω42 uniquely.

## 3. Conditional selection principles

A candidate dynamical framework may be required to have:

- distinguishable states;
- a coherent composition rule;
- invertible evolution;
- nontrivial recurrence;
- finite approximations;
- nontrivial internal dynamics.

These requirements narrow the possible structures, but do not uniquely determine Ω42.

To make a selection argument, we need an independently specified comparison class and a complexity criterion.

The mathematical selection developed below is conditional on such choices. It should not be presented as a deduction from existence alone.

# Part II — The canonical Ω42 transformation

## 4. Definition

Define

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2
\]

and the transformation

\[
T(a,b)=(b,a+b)\pmod{3^k}.
\]

Its matrix representation is

\[
\boxed{
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
}
\]

The determinant and characteristic polynomial are

\[
\det A=-1,
\]

\[
\chi_A(t)=t^2-t-1.
\]

The Cayley–Hamilton identity gives

\[
\boxed{A^2=A+I.}
\]

The transformation is invertible over every ring \(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z\), because its determinant is a unit.

## 5. Fibonacci representation

Let \(F_0=0\), \(F_1=1\), and

\[
F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
A^n=
\begin{pmatrix}
F_{n-1}&F_n\\
F_n&F_{n+1}
\end{pmatrix}
}
\]

for \(n\ge1\).

In particular,

\[
T^n(0,1)=(F_n,F_{n+1})\pmod{3^k}.
\]

The Ω42 transformation is therefore a linear realisation of Fibonacci recurrence.

## 6. The finite system modulo 9

At \(k=2\),

\[
S_2=(\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z)^2
\]

contains

\[
9^2=81
\]

states.

Direct calculation gives

\[
A^{12}=
\begin{pmatrix}
89&144\\
144&233
\end{pmatrix}
\equiv-I\pmod9.
\]

Therefore

\[
A^{24}\equiv I\pmod9.
\]

The exact order is 24.

The state space decomposes into:

| Orbit length | Number of orbits | Number of states |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 24 | 3 | 72 |
| 8 | 1 | 8 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 |

The five orbits have representatives

\[
(0,1),\quad
(0,2),\quad
(0,4),\quad
(0,3),\quad
(0,0).
\]

Every nonzero orbit has an antipodal return halfway through its period.

The zero state is fixed.

This finite system is the basic concrete Ω42 model.

# Part III — Exact finite dynamics at every 3-adic level

## 7. The first lifting identity

The exact integer powers include

\[
A^4=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&3\\
3&5
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
A^8=
\begin{pmatrix}
13&21\\
21&34
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Hence

\[
A^4\equiv-I\pmod3.
\]

Also,

\[
A^8=I+3B,
\]

where

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
4&7\\
7&11
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det B=-5\equiv1\pmod3.
\]

Thus \(B\) is invertible modulo 3.

This gives the regular lifting mechanism.

## 8. Exact matrix order modulo \(3^k\)

Since \(A^8=I+3B\), with \(B\) invertible modulo 3, binomial expansion gives

\[
v_3(A^{8\cdot3^s}-I)=s+1.
\]

The exact matrix order is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(A)=8\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

The halfway power is

\[
\boxed{
A^{4\cdot3^{k-1}}=-I\pmod{3^k}.
}
\]

For \(k=1\), this is \(A^4=-I\pmod3\). The higher-level result follows by repeated prime-power lifting.

## 9. Classification of all finite state orbits

Let

\[
R_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

A nonzero state can be written

\[
x=3^ju,
\]

where \(u\) is primitive modulo \(3^{k-j}\).

Let

\[
m=k-j.
\]

Because the characteristic polynomial is irreducible modulo 3, the residue algebra is \(\mathbb F_9\). Every nonzero element of this field has the same multiplicative order as the corresponding matrix transformation.

The exact period of every primitive state at scale \(m\) is

\[
8\cdot3^{m-1}.
\]

Thus every nonzero state \(x=3^ju\) has period

\[
\boxed{
r_k(x)=8\cdot3^{k-j-1}.
}
\]

The number of states of exact common valuation \(j\) is

\[
3^{2(k-j)}-3^{2(k-j-1)}
=8\cdot3^{2(k-j-1)}.
\]

Dividing by the orbit length gives

\[
3^{k-j-1}
\]

orbits.

Equivalently, for each scale \(m=1,\ldots,k\):

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{number of orbits}&=3^{m-1},\\
\text{orbit length}&=8\cdot3^{m-1}.
\end{aligned}
}
\]

There is one additional fixed orbit, the zero vector.

The total number of orbits is

\[
\boxed{\frac{3^k+1}{2}.}
\]

For \(k=2\), this gives three orbits of length 24, one of length 8, and one fixed point.

## 10. The inverse-limit system

The inverse limit is

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb Z_3^2
=
\varprojlim_k(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2.
}
\]

It is a 3-adic space, not a real continuum and not a field.

Every nonzero vector has a finite common 3-adic valuation. Its reductions therefore have periods that grow without bound as the modulus increases.

Consequently, the only periodic point of the transformation on the full inverse-limit space is zero.

This is compatible with the finite systems: every finite quotient has periodic orbits, while a nonzero point of the inverse limit does not have a finite global period.

# Part IV — Quadratic norm and 3-adic orbit geometry

## 11. The quadratic field

Let

\[
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

It satisfies

\[
\phi^2=\phi+1.
\]

The characteristic polynomial is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\), so

\[
K_3=\mathbb Q_3(\phi)
\]

is an unramified quadratic extension of \(\mathbb Q_3\).

Its ring of integers is

\[
\mathcal O_3=\mathbb Z_3[\phi].
\]

The coordinate correspondence

\[
(a,b)\longleftrightarrow a+b\phi
\]

identifies the Ω42 matrix with multiplication by \(\phi\).

## 12. The quadratic norm

For

\[
z=a+b\phi,
\]

the field norm is

\[
N(z)=a^2+ab-b^2.
\]

Define

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2.
\]

Then

\[
Q=2N.
\]

Because

\[
N(\phi)=-1,
\]

the quadratic form satisfies

\[
\boxed{Q(Ax)=-Q(x).}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{I(x)=Q(x)^2}
\]

is invariant under the transformation.

The norm is anisotropic: if \(Q(a,b)=0\) over \(\mathbb Z_3\), then \(a+b\phi=0\) in the quadratic field, so \(a=b=0\).

## 13. Orbit closures

Let

\[
G=\{u\in\mathcal O_3^\times:N(u)\in\{1,-1\}\}.
\]

The closure of the cyclic group generated by \(\phi\) is

\[
\overline{\langle\phi\rangle}=G.
\]

The essential ingredients are:

- the residue class of \(\phi\) generates \(\mathbb F_9^\times\);
- the principal norm-one subgroup is described by the 3-adic logarithm;
- the logarithm of \(\phi^8\) generates the corresponding rank-one trace-zero direction.

For nonzero \(x\in\mathbb Z_3^2\),

\[
\boxed{
\overline{\{A^nx:n\in\mathbb Z\}}
=
\{y:Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2\}.
}
\]

Thus the square of the quadratic norm describes the orbit closure.

# Part V — Projective dynamics and scale–direction structure

## 14. Projective space

The projective space

\[
\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3)
\]

records primitive directions, identifying vectors that differ by multiplication by a 3-adic unit.

The induced action of \(A\) on this space is a rotation by the class of \(\phi\) in the corresponding norm-one quotient.

The cyclic subgroup generated by this class is dense.

Therefore the projective action is minimal: every projective orbit is dense.

It is also uniquely ergodic, with Haar measure as its unique invariant probability measure.

At finite level \(3^k\), the projective space has

\[
4\cdot3^{k-1}
\]

elements, and the projective period is exactly that number.

In matrix terms,

\[
A^{4\cdot3^{k-1}}=-I\pmod{3^k},
\]

so the action becomes the identity after projectivisation.

## 15. Scale–direction decomposition

Every nonzero vector in \(\mathbb Z_3^2\) can be represented as

\[
x=3^jc\,\sigma(p),
\]

where:

- \(j\) is its common 3-adic valuation;
- \(c\in\mathbb Z_3^\times\) is a unit;
- \(p\in\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3)\);
- \(\sigma\) is a choice of primitive representative.

Under \(A\), the valuation \(j\) is preserved, the projective direction evolves, and the unit coordinate changes by a multiplicative cocycle.

The cocycle cannot be removed by a continuous change of section: the finite-level antipodal return obstructs such a reduction.

This means the 3-adic dynamics has a genuine interaction between scale and direction rather than being merely a projective rotation with an irrelevant scalar factor.

# Part VI — Other mathematical realisations of the same matrix

## 16. Fibonacci substitution

Consider the substitution

\[
S\mapsto L,
\qquad
L\mapsto LS.
\]

With the count vector ordered as \((S,L)\), the incidence matrix is

\[
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=A.
\]

If \(c(w)\) records the numbers of \(S\) and \(L\) in a word \(w\), then

\[
\boxed{c(\sigma(w))=Ac(w).}
\]

This is an exact semiconjugacy between symbolic substitution and linear count dynamics.

It is not a conjugacy: the count vector loses the order of the symbols.

The substitution produces words of unbounded length, while their count vectors modulo \(3^k\) follow finite Ω42 orbits.

The Fibonacci tile lengths \(1\) and \(\phi\) also produce inflation by \(\phi\).

## 17. Real dynamics

Over \(\mathbb R^2\), the eigenvalues are

\[
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
\qquad
\psi=-\phi^{-1}.
\]

One direction expands and the other contracts.

For nonzero integer vectors, repeated application of \(A\) produces projective convergence towards the expanding eigenvector direction.

## 18. Toral dynamics

On the real torus

\[
\mathbb T^2=\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2,
\]

the same matrix defines a hyperbolic toral automorphism.

It preserves Haar measure, is ergodic and mixing, and has topological entropy

\[
h_{\mathrm{top}}=\log\phi.
\]

This illustrates a central mathematical point:

**The same integral matrix can generate different dynamical systems on different ambient spaces.**

The real torus, the finite residue spaces, and the 3-adic inverse limit are not interchangeable.

# Part VII — The arithmetic classification of primes

## 19. The quadratic arithmetic field

The characteristic polynomial has discriminant \(5\). Its natural number field is

\[
K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5).
\]

The ring of integers is

\[
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\phi],
\]

and its discriminant is \(5\).

For odd \(p\ne5\), the splitting type is determined by

\[
\left(\frac5p\right).
\]

Thus

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
p\equiv\pm1\pmod5 &: \text{split},\\
p\equiv\pm2\pmod5 &: \text{inert}.
\end{array}
\]

The prime \(5\) is ramified.

## 20. Inert primes

For an odd inert prime \(p\), the roots lie in \(\mathbb F_{p^2}\).

Let \(\alpha\) be one root and \(\alpha'=1-\alpha\) its conjugate.

Since

\[
\alpha\alpha'=-1
\]

and Frobenius exchanges the roots,

\[
\alpha^p=\alpha',
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{\alpha^{p+1}=-1.}
\]

If \(r_p=\operatorname{ord}(\alpha)\), then

\[
\boxed{A^{r_p/2}=-I\pmod p.}
\]

This guarantees antipodality at every odd inert prime.

It does not require \(r_p=2(p+1)\); the exact order can be a proper divisor.

Examples include:

| Prime | Matrix order modulo \(p\) |
|---:|---:|
| 3 | 8 |
| 7 | 16 |
| 13 | 28 |
| 17 | 36 |
| 23 | 48 |
| 47 | 32 |

The prime \(3\) is the smallest odd inert prime in this quadratic field.

## 21. Split primes

For a split prime, let the roots be \(\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb F_p^\times\), with

\[
\lambda\mu=-1.
\]

Let

\[
d=\operatorname{ord}(\lambda),
\qquad
e=\operatorname{ord}(\mu).
\]

The exact matrix order is

\[
r_p=\operatorname{lcm}(d,e).
\]

The halfway matrix is antipodal precisely when

\[
\boxed{v_2(d)=v_2(e).}
\]

If the valuations differ, the halfway matrix is a noncentral involution with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\).

Examples:

- \(p=11\): orders \(10\) and \(5\), so the halfway matrix is noncentral.
- \(p=19\): orders \(9\) and \(18\), so the halfway matrix is noncentral.
- \(p=41\): equal 2-adic valuations, giving antipodality.

## 22. Ramification at 5

Modulo 5,

\[
t^2-t-1=(t-3)^2.
\]

The matrix has a nontrivial Jordan block.

Writing

\[
N=A-3I,
\]

we have

\[
N^2=0\pmod5,
\qquad N\ne0\pmod5.
\]

The scalar eigenvalue has order 4, and the unipotent part has order 5.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_5(A)=20.}
\]

Direct calculation gives

\[
A^{10}=-I\pmod5.
\]

Ramification thus produces antipodality through a Jordan mechanism rather than Frobenius exchange.

# Part VIII — Prime-power lifting

## 23. General matrix-order lifting theorem

Let \(p\) be an odd prime and let \(r\) be the exact matrix order modulo \(p\).

Suppose

\[
A^r=I+p^sB,
\qquad
B\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{p^k}(A)=
\begin{cases}
r,&k\le s,\\
rp^{k-s},&k>s.
\end{cases}
}
\]

The proof follows from binomial expansion:

\[
v_p\big((I+p^sB)^m-I\big)=s+v_p(m).
\]

The integer \(s\) is the first-lift valuation. It must be calculated for each prime; regular growth from the first level is not automatic.

## 24. Antipodal persistence theorem

Suppose

\[
A^{r/2}=-I\pmod p.
\]

Then, for every \(k\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{
A^{\operatorname{ord}_{p^k}(A)/2}
=-I\pmod{p^k}.
}
\]

The reason is that \(A^{r/2}\) is already congruent to \(-I\) modulo \(p\), while its square is congruent to the identity at the first-lift depth. Since \(p\) is odd, the factor \(A^{r/2}-I\) is invertible at that depth. The halfway return therefore lifts along with the matrix order.

This theorem applies to both inert primes and ramified prime 5.

It also applies to split primes satisfying the eigenvalue synchronisation criterion.

A split-prime noncentral involution cannot become \(-I\) at a higher level, because reduction modulo \(p\) would contradict its original noncentral form.

## 25. The canonical 3-adic hierarchy

For \(p=3\),

\[
r_3=8,\qquad s_3=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(A)=8\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

The antipodal exponent is

\[
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
\]

## 26. The ramified 5-adic hierarchy

For \(p=5\),

\[
r_5=20,\qquad s_5=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{5^k}(A)=20\cdot5^{k-1}.
}
\]

The antipodal exponent is

\[
10\cdot5^{k-1}.
\]

This is a second stable prime-power hierarchy, generated by ramification rather than inertness.

# Part IX — Individual state orbits in the three prime classes

## 27. Inert state orbits

For an inert prime, the residue algebra is a field.

Multiplication by \(\alpha\) has no nonzero fixed vector unless \(\alpha^n=1\). Thus all nonzero primitive states have the same period at a given effective modulus.

At higher prime powers, let \(s_p\) be the first-lift valuation. A state of common \(p\)-adic coordinate valuation \(j\) has period

\[
\boxed{
r_{p,k}(x)
=
r_p\,p^{\max(0,k-j-s_p)}.
}
\]

For \(p=3\), \(s_3=1\), recovering the uniform nonzero period formula at each effective level.

Every nonzero inert state has an antipodal halfway return.

## 28. Split state orbits

Over \(\mathbb Z_p\), a split matrix is diagonalisable:

\[
P^{-1}AP=
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda&0\\
0&\mu
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p).
\]

Let a state in this eigenbasis be \((u,v)\), with effective component depths

\[
m_1=k-v_p(u),
\qquad
m_2=k-v_p(v)
\]

for nonzero components.

Let \(d_1\) and \(e_2\) be the respective eigenvalue orders at those effective moduli.

Then

\[
\boxed{
r_{p,k}(x)=\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,e_2)
}
\]

when both components are nonzero.

If only one component is nonzero, its period is the corresponding eigenvalue order.

For a state with both components active, antipodality at its own halfway period holds precisely when

\[
\boxed{v_2(d_1)=v_2(e_2).}
\]

If only one component is active, it has an antipodal halfway return precisely when its own component period is even.

This qualification is important: an eigenline whose eigenvalue has odd order has no antipodal return within its orbit.

## 29. Ramified state orbits

For \(p=5\), use the ramified quadratic ring

\[
\mathcal O_5=\mathbb Z_5[\phi],
\qquad
\pi=\sqrt5,
\qquad
\pi^2=5.
\]

The state space modulo \(5^k\) corresponds to

\[
\mathcal O_5/(\pi^{2k}).
\]

The correct valuation calculation is

\[
N(836+1353\phi)=-605=-5\cdot11^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
v_\pi(\phi^{20}-1)=3.
\]

For a nonzero state of valuation depth \(L\), its exact period is

\[
\boxed{
r_k(x)=
\begin{cases}
4,&L=1,\\
20,&L=2,\\
20\cdot5^{\lceil(L-3)/2\rceil},&L\ge3.
\end{cases}
}
\]

The number of states at depth \(L\) is

\[
4\cdot5^{L-1}.
\]

The number of orbits at each depth is

\[
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
1,&L=1,2,\\
5^{(L-1)/2},&L\ge3\text{ odd},\\
5^{(L-2)/2},&L\ge4\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
}
\]

Every nonzero state has an antipodal halfway return.

The ramified system is therefore nonuniform in state periods but uniform in antipodal symmetry.

# Part X — A conditional minimality argument for Ω42

## 30. Minimum growth in dimension two

Consider hyperbolic matrices in

\[
\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb Z).
\]

For determinant \(-1\), the characteristic polynomial is

\[
t^2-\tau t-1,
\]

where \(\tau\) is the integer trace.

The smallest possible spectral radius greater than one occurs at \(|\tau|=1\), giving

\[
\rho=\phi.
\]

For determinant \(+1\), the smallest hyperbolic trace magnitude is 3, producing a larger spectral radius.

Thus the minimum hyperbolic spectral radius in this class is

\[
\boxed{\phi.}
\]

It is attained by the characteristic polynomials

\[
t^2-t-1
\]

and

\[
t^2+t-1.
\]

The second is related to inverse-time dynamics.

## 31. Integral conjugacy

For the trace-one, determinant-minus-one polynomial

\[
t^2-t-1,
\]

integral conjugacy classes correspond to ideal classes in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\).

The ring of integers has class number one.

Therefore all integral matrices with this irreducible characteristic polynomial are conjugate over \(\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb Z)\) to the canonical matrix \(A\).

This means the minimum-growth recurrence is not merely one convenient coordinate representation: it is the unique integral conjugacy class within this polynomial class.

## 32. Why the prime 3 is selected

Among odd inert primes in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), the smallest is

\[
p=3.
\]

It produces the smallest odd quadratic residue field:

\[
\mathbb F_9.
\]

Under a lexicographic criterion:

1. minimise dimension;
2. minimise hyperbolic spectral radius;
3. minimise the odd inert prime;

the pair

\[
\boxed{(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5),3)}
\]

is selected.

This is a conditional mathematical selection result.

It depends on the chosen comparison class and criteria. It does not establish that these criteria are uniquely demanded by existence.

# Part XI — Mathematical conclusions and open problems

## 33. Established conclusions

The following results have been established within the stated mathematical framework:

1. The canonical matrix is an invertible Fibonacci recurrence transformation.
2. Its finite system modulo 9 has 81 states and exact matrix order 24.
3. Every nonzero state modulo \(3^k\) has an exact period determined by its common 3-adic valuation.
4. Every nonzero state has an antipodal halfway return.
5. The 3-adic inverse-limit system has no nonzero periodic point.
6. The quadratic norm squared is invariant.
7. The nonzero 3-adic orbit closures are quadratic-norm shells.
8. The projective action on \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3)\) is minimal and uniquely ergodic.
9. The matrix has different dynamical interpretations over finite residue spaces, the 3-adic space, the real plane and the real torus.
10. Inert primes guarantee antipodality through Frobenius.
11. Split primes permit antipodality under a precise 2-adic synchronisation condition.
12. The ramified prime 5 produces antipodality through a Jordan mechanism.
13. A general prime-power lifting theorem determines matrix orders from the first-lift valuation.
14. Antipodal halfway returns persist through prime-power lifting when present at the initial odd prime.
15. Individual state periods are classified in the inert, split and ramified cases.

## 34. Remaining mathematical work

The paper is now substantially consolidated, but several possible extensions remain:

- a systematic table of exact orders and first-lift valuations for a wider range of split primes;
- a more extensive projective classification at split and ramified primes;
- a comparison of invariant measures on all finite and local systems;
- a study of which properties are preserved under broader classes of integral conjugacy and recurrence transformations;
- a formal, independent definition of generative capacity.

These are extensions rather than prerequisites for the central theorems already established.

## 35. The ontological question

The central ambition of Ω42 is larger than a theory of recurrence.

It asks whether existence, distinction, relation and transformation might be understood through a minimal generative mathematical structure.

The present paper does not prove that claim.
