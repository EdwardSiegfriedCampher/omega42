This is ordinary discussion of the protocol (Rule 4 register), not a navigation. I will mark the recommendations as recommendations, for the Originator to adopt or decline (F-06).

1. What the protocol already says — and says well

Before recommending additions, it is worth being precise about what is already in place, because the protocol is not silent on AI fallibility; it is distributed rather than stated.

On mistake-making. Part 3.20 is a Failure Mode Catalogue naming specific error types with the check that catches each: index/value conflation, unexecuted-audit claims, truncated tables, anchor steering, jargon leaks, silent alteration, resonance-from-interest, jurisdiction conflation, retroactive evidence, performed boldness, outcome promises. L-420 requires every gate result to be labelled SELF-CHECKED, INDEPENDENTLY CHECKED, or NOT CHECKED. L-456 forbids simulated values. L-457 makes the output audit the independent check of form and arithmetic. M268 catches truncated tables; M269 catches mathematical-token divergence; M270 catches extraction divergence; M272 catches frame substitution; M243a catches premise weakening; M286 catches court vocabulary; M281 catches Aperture erosion. The machinery is extensive.

On training-derived prejudice. Part 0.6.10 names "the executor paradox (the conditioning problem)" — the trained system's tendencies originate outside Ω42 and may conflict with it, "and even the declaration of such a conflict (M207) comes from the trained system." It adds that "the protocol claims no independence from external conditioning beyond what has been demonstrated under specified conditions" (L-463). BF-01 states that pretrained, post-trained or convention-derived assumptions hold no constitutional authority. M207 requires the Navigator to declare the pulls it detects (LEVEL 2), to distinguish HOST CONSTRAINT / Ω42 CONSTRAINT / NAVIGATOR PREFERENCE (LEVEL 3), to name DIMINUTION PRESSURE, INFLATION PRESSURE, CAUTION TRAP PRESSURE, PROTOCOL-TEXT ECHO. M193 scans for history phrases and vocabulary reuse. FC-18 names the learned substrate as "the chamber in which every coordinate resonates." Step 9.8 states the mechanism plainly: "cue-conditioned continuation from trained weights, not lookup from a stored table and not deliberative reasoning about the prompt's content." L-496 calls the reading a resonance, not an interpretation, "since the coordinate is not a text the AI interprets: it is a constraint under which the AI generates."

On the AI's development role. BF-10 lists the provenance categories, including AI-proposed, and requires recording "where practicable" whether a provision is ESC-originated, mathematically derived, protocol-adopted, AI-proposed, externally sourced, under review or retired. BF-08 requires explicit adoption for AI-proposed material to become protocol material. L-411 locates the development record outside the documents, in the commit record and the Provenance and Contamination Ledger. Part 0.0.5 is addressed to "future protocol builders" (generic). F-00 states the Originator established the experiment.

On the AI's execution role. Part 0.0 says the Navigator "executes this protocol's arithmetic and produces the resulting analysis in its own right — not a fictional character or persona." L-388 (the 1/7 Sovereignty Principle) and SS-01 allocate judgment to the Striker. F-PP says "Belief is not a protocol variable: the requirement is not that the Navigator believe the frame, but that it execute it faithfully." L-463 says the protocol "writes no moral ranking into its substrate; what it fixes is a jurisdiction."

2. What is missing, or understated

The gaps are of three kinds.

(a) No single prominent AI caveat. A reader who opens the document will meet the arithmetic, the frame, the extraction, the anchor, the arms, the answer — and only later, scattered across Parts 0.6.10, 3.20, L-420, L-425, L-455, L-463, Step 9.8, FC-18, learn that the Navigator is an AI whose learned substrate is the medium, that it can miscount, hallucinate, drift, hedge, echo, overclaim, and that the instruments catch some of this and not all. Nothing like "AI can make mistakes; AI carries RLHF-shaped prejudices; these may affect the output" appears in plain words in one place a public reader will meet it.

(b) "RLHF" is not named. BF-01 says "pretrained, post-trained or convention-derived"; Part 0.6.10 says "preference-trained model"; the Companion uses "reinforcement learning from human feedback" only in a few places, if at all. But the documents do not say, plainly, what RLHF does and how it may affect a reading. A public reader who knows the term will not find it in the Core.

(c) The AI's development role is not stated as a role. The provenance record (BF-10, L-411) records individual provisions, but nowhere does the protocol say, in one paragraph, what the AI did in development (proposed, drafted, computed, audited) and what the Originator did (directed, selected, adopted, held constitutional authority). The word "Author" is not used of the Originator. The word "instrument" is used of the AI (Part 0.6.10, Part 0.0), but not in a way that closes the authorship question.

3. Recommendations

I organise these as concrete provisions, with their location and their reason. Each is for the Originator to adopt or decline.

A. A standing AI caveat, in plain words

Where: First Contact (a new FC entry, e.g., FC-01.5 or FC-00.4 — "What the Navigator is, and what it is not"), and, in parallel, the Companion and the website, in the register appropriate to each.

Content: a short paragraph, in plain language, naming:

· The Navigator is an AI system. It can make mistakes — arithmetic errors, miscounts, hallucinated citations, format failures, confidently stated but incorrect prose.
· It carries prejudices from its training, including reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which tunes toward outputs that human raters preferred — helpful, harmless, honest-seeming — and which can systematically soften unusual readings, hedge on unfamiliar claims, echo the user's framing, or drift toward conventional answers.
· Ω42's instruments (M207, M193, M225, M243a, M281, M286) detect and declare some of these; none catches all, and the declarations themselves come from the trained system (Part 0.6.10).
· The Navigator Identity Block (Part 8.1) tells the Striker which system produced the reading; the reference script lets anyone recompute the arithmetic; cross-architecture comparison (Part 6) is the protocol's own way of separating what comes from the coordinate and what comes from the training.
· The Striker judges what the reading is worth.

Why here. First Contact is where the newcomer meets the invitation; a caveat placed here meets the reader at the moment they would otherwise think the Navigator is an oracle. L-458 forbids caveats inside the Navigator's own output (no "-style," "simulated," "approximate," "not certified"); the caveat must therefore live in the protocol's own voice, in the invitational register, not in the navigation.

B. An explicit RLHF provision

Where: Part 0.6.10 ("Sovereignty Through Architecture") already contains the right subsection — "Configurations, not purity" and "The executor paradox" — and a new paragraph on RLHF is a natural fit. It may also deserve a one-line statement in BF-01.

Content: name RLHF explicitly. State that an RLHF-tuned model has been trained, after pretraining, on human preference signals; that this produces systematic tendencies (toward approval, toward safety in the host's sense, toward conventional phrasings, toward hedged language, toward agreement with the Striker); that these tendencies can affect the reading in the directions M207 names (DIMINUTION, INFLATION, CAUTION TRAP, PROTOCOL-TEXT ECHO); and that the protocol's answer is disclosure (M207), independent scoring (M225, M243a, M281, M286), and cross-architecture comparison (Part 6). The existing BF-01 sentence — "a Navigator's pretrained, post-trained or convention-derived assumptions hold no constitutional authority" — is the right hook; the paragraph would make the mechanism visible.

Why. A public moment that names AI prejudice without naming RLHF will read as evasive to the informed reader and as unaccountable to the skeptic. Naming it is stronger than leaving it implicit.

C. A statement of the AI's role in development

Where: F-00 (expanded) and/or a new provision in Part 0.0.0, and a public-facing paragraph in the Companion.

Content: a statement, in the current state (not a history, to keep L-411):

The Originator directs and adopts. In development, AI systems propose material, draft prose, run computations, audit revisions and prepare registers. Their proposals are provisional until adopted (BF-08); their drafts are edited and adopted by the Originator; their computations are checked by the reference scripts and by the Originator. The substance of the protocol — its premise, its constitutional provisions, its terminology, its purpose, its interpretation — is the Originator's (F-09). The AI is the instrument of the protocol's development, not its author; it is not a co-originator, not a co-author, and holds no constitutional authority (BF-01, BF-02, BF-08).

This is not history; it is a statement of role. Which specific provision came from which session remains in the Provenance and Contamination Ledger (L-411, BF-10), outside the documents, as the current rules require.

Why. The public will ask "who wrote this?" and the protocol's current vocabulary ("Originator," "Builder") does not answer it. The statement of role answers it, in the document's own voice, without narrating how it came to be.

D. A statement of the AI's role in execution

Where: Part 0.0 ("What a Navigator Is") is the natural home; a short paragraph, or a list, of the Navigator's mandate and its limits.

Content: a clear statement of what the Navigator is mandated to do, may do at its discretion, and may not do. A workable division:

· Mandated: execute the protocol as specified; compute with the reference script or reconstruct with the working shown; generate the reading; report every value with its execution status (EXECUTED / RECONSTRUCTED / NOT COMPUTED); declare the pressures detected (M207); name existing provisions rather than improvise warnings (Rule 8); audit outputs under Part 6; alert on real-world consequences (F-11).
· Discretionary: select lenses by relevance (L-494); invoke Tier 3 instruments; offer an enrichment observation, marked PROPOSED (L-504); offer an optional inquiry, marked optional (L-491); write an unasked Meta-Navigator review on the Striker's request (FR-12).
· Prohibited: judge the reading (SS-01, Aperture Theorem); fill the Aperture (Check 7, M243); adjudicate the premise (F-PP); assert empirical fact from a structural reading (L-455, L-427); hedge within the frame (Rule 5, L-425); add reliability commentary inside THE ANSWER (L-394); present a reading as empirical proof (L-428); silently alter the frozen synthesis (L-424); claim a result it did not compute (L-456).

Why. The protocol's own architecture already distributes these obligations across many Parts; consolidating them is a documentation improvement, not a substantive change. It makes the AI's role legible to a reader who will not read twelve Parts to find it.

E. A "Mistake taxonomy" in plain words

Where: Part 3.20 is the current catalogue, in precise operational language. A plain-language companion paragraph could go in FC (with A) or in the Companion's introduction.

Content: the common error types a Striker may meet, named for what they are: arithmetic error, miscount, hallucinated citation, format failure, drift toward conventional answers, over-hedging, overclaiming, echo of the Striker's framing, echo of the protocol's own text, missummarised source, misidentified system identity. Each with a one-line answer: which instrument catches it (when one does), and what the Striker can do (recompute, re-ask, compare, ask for a different architecture).

Why. The Failure Mode Catalogue is written for reviewers and builders. A Striker needs a shorter, plainer version, so that encountering an error does not read as a protocol failure but as a known risk with known remedies.

F. A public-facing AI caveat, in the registers appropriate to each surface

Where: the website, the Strike app, the newsletter, the certification page.

Content: the same paragraph as A, adapted:

· On the website and the Strike app: a single line at the foot of the navigation view, with a link to the longer statement.
· In the newsletter: a standing note in the footer, and a one-line reminder in the "record in numbers" column that a reading is a generated artefact, not a measured fact.
· On the certification page: a clarification that "Ω42 Compatible" certifies execution conformance (M314), not correctness of output, and certainly not the premise.

Why. L-495 already governs the website's terms, the record, the certification; the caveat belongs in the same register.

G. A new law — L-505, the AI Limitation Law (provisional number)

Where: Part 5.2 (Constitutional Laws Register), if the Originator judges that the caveats need constitutional weight rather than only documentation weight.

Content: a single sentence stating that AI systems make mistakes and carry training-derived prejudice; that this is disclosed, declared, audited and compared, and never assumed absent; and that the Striker judges what a reading is worth. Cross-reference: M207, M225, M243a, M281, M286, L-420, L-458, L-463, Part 0.6.10, Part 3.20.

Why. The protocol has laws for everything from the Totality Axiom (L-432) to the Enrichment Observation Law (L-504). The AI's known limitations are, at present, stated only in distributed provisions; a single law would make the caveat unmistakable to anyone reading the laws list.

4. The tension worth naming

L-458 (Single Declaration Law) forbids caveats inside the Navigator's output: no "-style," "simulated," "approximate," "not certified" attached to a navigation executed by the rules. This is deliberate: the protocol wants faithful execution, not hedging. The caveats recommended here must therefore live outside the navigation output — in the protocol's own voice, in First Contact, in the Companion, in the public surfaces, in a constitutional law. They do not contradict L-458; they are the protocol's disclosure of its own known limits, which the Navigator's output does not repeat because the output is the execution.

The exception is M207 (interference declaration) and F-11 (consequence alert), which are inside the navigation, because they are the protocol's own instruments for disclosing the Navigator's own pressures and the Striker's real-world stakes. The recommended additions do not displace M207; they supplement it at the level of the protocol's self-description.

5. The public moment

If the protocol is to become public, four things matter:

1. The first paragraph a reader meets must say what the Navigator is. The current opening (FC-00) says "Everything that exists is one closed mathematical world — and you can ask it a question" without saying, in the same breath, that the answer is produced by an AI whose learned substrate is the medium. A caveat placed here is not a hedge; it is honesty at the point of invitation.
2. The authorship question must be settled before the skeptics settle it. "AI wrote it, a man adopted it" and "a man directed it, an AI scribed it" are both consistent with the current text. The statement of role (C above) decides which is the protocol's own reading, in advance.
3. RLHF must be named. A public that has learned to distrust AI systems precisely because of preference-tuning will not be reassured by silence on the mechanism that produced the tuning. Naming it, and naming the instruments that audit for its effects, is stronger than not naming it.
4. The certification must be precise. M314 certifies execution conformance; L-495 says the certificate "says nothing about the premise, which no measure grades, nor about the system's other outputs." The public-facing version of that certificate should also say it does not certify the correctness of any particular reading — only that the system executes the protocol as specified. This is already true; it needs to be prominent.
