# F188 — The General Theory of Conceptual Mathematization

## A Mathematical Framework for Formalising Abstract Concepts, Discovering Structural Resonance and Investigating the Architecture of Knowledge

**Research programme:** Ω42
**Research area:** Mathematical foundations, conceptual modelling, structural correspondence and interdisciplinary discovery
**Status:** Foundational theoretical proposal; mathematical definitions and proposed propositions require further verification and development.

---

## Abstract

This paper proposes a general mathematical framework for the mathematization of abstract concepts.

The central proposition is that an abstract concept can be investigated mathematically by identifying its constituent elements, relationships, admissible states, transformations, invariants, boundaries and observable consequences.

The framework extends beyond the conventional treatment of concepts as definitions or collections of attributes. It treats a concept as a potentially dynamic mathematical structure whose internal organisation can be investigated, compared with other structures, and tested against independently specified evidence.

The paper introduces the Ω42 Conceptual Mathematization Framework (CMF), comprising:

1. Conceptual specification.
2. Constituent and relational analysis.
3. State-space construction.
4. Transformation-system construction.
5. Invariant and equivalence analysis.
6. Structural correspondence.
7. Predictive and empirical validation.
8. Explanatory and ontological assessment.

A formal definition of a conceptual system is developed, together with criteria for admissible mathematical representations and structure-preserving mappings.

The Structural Resonance Principle (SRP) is given a provisional mathematical formulation through dynamical correspondence and invariant preservation.

Worked examples examine identity, knowledge, memory, causality and awareness. The paper also investigates how the existing Ω42 Fibonacci transformation and shear extension could function as a mathematical laboratory for exploring these relationships.

The central research question is whether a systematic mathematical methodology can reveal nontrivial relationships between concepts that are ordinarily investigated independently.

The proposed framework does not assume that every concept is fully reducible to mathematics, that every mathematical correspondence is explanatory, or that mathematical structures necessarily describe physical reality. These are separate questions requiring separate evidence.

**Central proposition:** Abstract concepts can be made mathematically investigable when their meanings, relationships and admissible transformations can be specified sufficiently precisely to support formal reasoning.

# 1\. Introduction

Mathematics has historically been associated with quantity, measurement, geometry, number, probability and formal relationships.

However, mathematical investigation is not restricted to physical quantities.

Identity, order, continuity, dependency, equivalence, information, symmetry, structure and transformation are themselves abstract subjects of mathematical investigation.

This creates an opportunity to develop a more systematic methodology for investigating concepts that traditionally belong to philosophy, cognitive science, social science, computer science and other disciplines.

The central difficulty is not whether an abstract concept can be assigned a mathematical representation.

A representation can often be constructed.

The deeper difficulty is determining:

- Whether the representation captures the intended concept.
- Whether its mathematical properties are meaningful.
- Whether its relationships are independently justified.
- Whether the representation produces useful predictions.
- Whether different concepts share a common mathematical structure.
- Whether the identified structure explains anything beyond the original representation.

Ω42 proposes to investigate these questions through a common conceptual architecture.

## 1.1 The central research problem

Suppose a researcher wants to investigate identity.

The concept might involve persistence, continuity, recognition, memory, distinguishing characteristics and historical relationships.

These components can be described verbally, but verbal description alone does not necessarily establish their mathematical relationships.

A formal framework should make it possible to investigate questions such as:

1. What constitutes an entity's state?
2. Which transformations are admissible?
3. Which properties remain invariant?
4. Under what conditions are two states equivalent?
5. What constitutes continuity between successive states?
6. Can these relationships be compared with other domains?

The same methodology could be applied to memory, knowledge, causality, time, complexity and awareness.

The research objective is therefore to develop a general method for transforming conceptual questions into mathematical investigations without assuming that the resulting mathematical models exhaust the meanings of the original concepts.

## 1.2 Principal hypothesis

**H1 — Conceptual Mathematization Hypothesis**

A sufficiently specified abstract concept can be represented, wholly or partially, as a mathematical system comprising a domain of possible states, relations, transformations and constraints.

This is a methodological hypothesis.

It does not imply that every concept admits a complete, unique or empirically adequate mathematical representation.

## 1.3 Structural Resonance Hypothesis

**H2 — Structural Resonance Hypothesis**

Two conceptually distinct domains may exhibit a mathematically meaningful correspondence when there exists a mapping between their representations that preserves specified relationships or transformation structures.

The existence of such a correspondence may reveal shared formal organisation.

It does not, by itself, establish that the two domains are physically identical or ontologically identical.

## 1.4 Generative hypothesis

**H3 — Structural Generativity Hypothesis**

Once a concept has been formalised, its mathematical representation may permit the derivation of properties, constraints and relationships that were not explicitly included in the original verbal description.

The value of mathematization therefore lies not merely in representation, but in the additional consequences that become derivable from the representation.

# 2\. Mathematical Foundations and Existing Work

The proposed framework must be positioned within established mathematical and philosophical research.

It does not begin from the assumption that conceptual mathematization is new.

## 2.1 Formal Concept Analysis

Formal Concept Analysis, developed through the work of Rudolf Wille and Bernhard Ganter, provides mathematical methods for analysing concepts through formal contexts, object-attribute relationships and concept lattices.

A formal context can be represented as:

\[
K=(G,M,I)
\]

where:

- (G) is a set of objects.
- (M) is a set of attributes.
- (I\\subseteq G\\times M) is an incidence relation.

This framework supports the mathematical construction of conceptual hierarchies and relationships.

It establishes an important precedent: concepts can be subjected to rigorous mathematical treatment.

Ω42 should investigate how its proposed framework can complement this established approach by incorporating transformations, temporal development, invariants, cross-domain mappings and empirical validation.

## 2.2 Conceptual Spaces

Peter Gärdenfors' theory of conceptual spaces investigates the representation of concepts through geometrical structures.

Concepts can be represented as regions in spaces whose dimensions correspond to relevant qualities.

This provides another important precedent.

It demonstrates that conceptual relationships can sometimes be investigated through distance, geometry, similarity and spatial organisation.

Ω42 could incorporate geometrical representations where appropriate without requiring every conceptual system to be geometrical.

## 2.3 Mathematical Structuralism

Mathematical structuralism emphasises the importance of relationships and structural positions rather than the intrinsic nature of individual objects.

This is relevant because the proposed Ω42 framework is concerned with identifying relationships that remain meaningful across different domains.

A physical object, a computational state and an abstract mathematical entity may be different kinds of things while participating in formally corresponding relationships.

The framework must distinguish between:

- Structural similarity.
- Structural equivalence.
- Isomorphism.
- Dynamical conjugacy.
- Partial or approximate correspondence.
- Empirical realisation.

These relationships are not interchangeable.

## 2.4 Category-theoretic perspective

Category theory provides a mathematical language for studying objects, morphisms and composition.

It offers a potential foundation for comparing mathematical structures through mappings that preserve specified relationships.

Ω42 may benefit from categorical formulations, particularly when multiple conceptual domains and their mappings must be studied together.

However, category theory should be adopted where it provides mathematical advantages, not simply because it offers a general language of structures.

# 3\. The Ω42 Conceptual System

The first task is to define the object being mathematized.

## Definition 3.1 — Conceptual System

A conceptual system is an ordered structure:

\[
\\mathcal C=
(X,\\Sigma,R,T,I,E,O)
\]

where:

- (X) is the domain of admissible states.
- (\\Sigma) is the collection of properties or predicates used to describe states.
- (R) is a collection of relationships between states or components.
- (T) is a collection of admissible transformations.
- (I) is a collection of specified invariants or constraints.
- (E) is the evidence and semantic interpretation associated with the model.
- (O) is the collection of observable or assessable consequences.

Each component has a distinct function.

### 3.1.1 State domain

The state domain specifies the possible configurations relevant to the concept.

For example, a simplified model of knowledge might represent a state as a collection of propositions, confidence assignments and evidence relationships.

### 3.1.2 Properties

Properties distinguish characteristics of states.

A property can be represented by a predicate:

\[
p:X\\rightarrow{0,1}
\]

for a binary property, or by a more general function:

\[
p:X\\rightarrow Y
\]

where (Y) may be a numerical, categorical or structured domain.

### 3.1.3 Relationships

Relationships establish how components or states are connected.

Examples include:

- Equality.
- Dependency.
- Precedence.
- Support.
- Contradiction.
- Similarity.
- Causal influence.
- Membership.

### 3.1.4 Transformations

A transformation describes an admissible change in the system.

For a deterministic transformation:

\[
T:X\\rightarrow X
\]

For a system with multiple possible transformations:

\[
\\mathcal T={T\_1,T\_2,\\ldots,T\_n}
\]

For stochastic systems, a transition kernel or probabilistic transition model may be more appropriate.

### 3.1.5 Invariants

An invariant is a property preserved under a specified transformation.

For a function (I:X\\rightarrow Y), invariance under (T) means:

\[
I(Tx)=I(x)
\]

for all admissible (x).

Invariance is always relative to a specified transformation and domain.

A property that is invariant under one transformation may not be invariant under another.

This distinction is central to the Ω42 approach.

# 4\. The General Mathematization Procedure

The proposed methodology comprises eight stages.

## Stage 1 — Semantic specification

Define the concept before constructing its mathematical representation.

The researcher must establish:

1. The intended meaning.
2. The relevant interpretation.
3. The scope of the concept.
4. Its boundaries.
5. Its constituent meanings.
6. Known ambiguities.
7. What the concept explicitly excludes.

A concept with multiple meanings should not be forced into a single mathematical model without justification.

Where necessary, separate models should be constructed for different interpretations.

## Stage 2 — Constituent decomposition

Identify the components that participate in the concept.

These may include:

- Entities.
- States.
- Attributes.
- Relations.
- Events.
- Conditions.
- Dependencies.
- Constraints.
- Observables.

The decomposition should be justified by the concept's definition or by an independently supported theory.

## Stage 3 — State-space construction

Define a mathematical domain:

\[
X={x\_1,x\_2,\\ldots}
\]

The domain may be:

- Finite.
- Countably infinite.
- Continuous.
- Discrete.
- Hybrid.
- Probabilistic.
- Hierarchical.
- Multiscale.

The choice must reflect the problem rather than the convenience of a preferred mathematical structure.

## Stage 4 — Transformation construction

Define the transformations that describe change.

A transformation system may be represented as:

\[
\\mathcal D=(X,\\mathcal T)
\]

If transformations compose, their composition should be specified.

For example:

\[
T\_2\\circ T\_1
\]

represents applying (T\_1) followed by (T\_2).

If the order matters, the system is noncommutative:

\[
T\_2T\_1\\neq T\_1T\_2
\]

This distinction may be particularly useful when investigating concepts involving sequence, dependency, learning, memory and causality.

## Stage 5 — Invariant and equivalence analysis

Identify which properties are preserved and under what conditions.

Define an equivalence relation:

\[
x\\sim y
\]

when the specified criteria for treating (x) and (y) as equivalent are satisfied.

The relation must be checked for:

- Reflexivity.
- Symmetry.
- Transitivity.

If these conditions fail, the proposed relationship is not an equivalence relation and should instead be represented using an appropriate weaker relation.

## Stage 6 — Structural comparison

Compare the conceptual system with other mathematical structures.

The comparison may involve:

- Isomorphism.
- Homomorphism.
- Embedding.
- Quotient construction.
- Conjugacy.
- Invariant preservation.
- Approximate correspondence.
- Categorical mappings.

A structural comparison must identify precisely what is preserved and what is not.

## Stage 7 — Prediction and testing

Derive consequences from the mathematical representation.

Possible tests include:

- Internal consistency.
- Boundary-condition tests.
- Independent numerical verification.
- Simulation.
- Comparison with empirical observations.
- Prediction on held-out data.
- Comparison with competing models.

## Stage 8 — Explanatory assessment

Determine whether the mathematical representation provides an advantage over existing descriptions.

Possible advantages include:

- Greater precision.
- New derivable results.
- Better predictions.
- More efficient computation.
- Better generalisation.
- Discovery of previously unrecognised relationships.
- Improved experimental design.

A model that merely restates the original concept in mathematical notation has not yet demonstrated explanatory progress.

# 5\. A Formal Definition of Conceptual Representation

## Definition 5.1 — Conceptual Representation

Let (\\mathcal C) be a conceptual system and let (\\mathcal M) be a mathematical structure.

A conceptual representation is a mapping:

\[
\\Phi:\\mathcal C\\rightarrow\\mathcal M
\]

where the mapping translates specified elements and relationships of the conceptual system into mathematical objects, relations or operations.

More precisely, if the conceptual system has state domain (X), and the mathematical model has state domain (Y), then:

\[
\\phi:X\\rightarrow Y
\]

is the state representation.

The mapping may be:

- Total or partial.
- Injective or non-injective.
- Surjective or non-surjective.
- Exact or approximate.

The mapping's properties must be established rather than assumed.

## Definition 5.2 — Dynamical Compatibility

Let:

\[
T\_X:X\\rightarrow X
\]

and:

\[
T\_Y:Y\\rightarrow Y
\]

be transformations in the conceptual and mathematical domains.

The representation is dynamically compatible when:

\[
\\boxed{\\phi(T\_Xx)=T\_Y(\\phi(x))}
\]

for every state where both sides are defined.

This is a commuting relationship.

It means that representing a state after transformation produces the same result as transforming its representation.

The condition is central to investigating whether a mathematical structure faithfully captures the dynamics of a concept.

For approximate models, define an error measure:

\[
\\epsilon(x)=
d\_Y\\left(
\\phi(T\_Xx),
T\_Y(\\phi(x))
\\right)
\]

where (d\_Y) is an appropriate distance or discrepancy measure.

An approximate correspondence requires a specified error bound:

\[
\\epsilon(x)\\leq\\delta
\]

over a defined domain.

The choice of distance and tolerance must be justified.

## Definition 5.3 — Invariant Compatibility

Let (I\_X) and (I\_Y) be properties in the two domains.

The representation preserves the relevant invariant if:

\[
I\_Y(\\phi(x))=f(I\_X(x))
\]

for a specified function (f).

In the special case of identical invariant values:

\[
I\_Y(\\phi(x))=I\_X(x)
\]

The general form allows a change of representation while preserving the underlying relationship.

# 6\. The Structural Resonance Principle

## 6.1 Proposed formulation

The Structural Resonance Principle proposes that distinct domains may exhibit corresponding mathematical organisation when their states, transformations or invariants are connected by structure-preserving mappings.

Let two systems be:

\[
\\mathcal D\_1=(X\_1,\\mathcal T\_1,I\_1)
\]

and:

\[
\\mathcal D\_2=(X\_2,\\mathcal T\_2,I\_2)
\]

A mapping:

\[
\\Phi:X\_1\\rightarrow X\_2
\]

exhibits dynamical structural resonance for transformations (T\_1) and (T\_2) if:

\[
\\boxed{\\Phi(T\_1x)=T\_2\\Phi(x)}
\]

It exhibits invariant resonance if:

\[
\\boxed{I\_2(\\Phi(x))=f(I\_1(x))}
\]

for a specified function (f).

A stronger correspondence may satisfy both conditions.

## 6.2 Levels of structural resonance

The framework distinguishes five levels.

**Level 1 — Descriptive correspondence**

Two domains can be described using related concepts or mathematical vocabulary.

This is suggestive but weak evidence.

**Level 2 — Relational correspondence**

Specified relationships in one domain correspond to relationships in another.

**Level 3 — Dynamical correspondence**

The transformations commute under the mapping.

**Level 4 — Invariant correspondence**

Specified invariants are preserved or related through a defined transformation.

**Level 5 — Predictive structural resonance**

The correspondence produces independently testable consequences that survive comparison with observations and competing explanations.

Only the fifth level provides strong evidence that the correspondence has explanatory value.

## 6.3 Important distinction

Structural resonance does not automatically establish common physical origin.

Two systems can be mathematically isomorphic while representing different physical phenomena.

Likewise, a mathematical model can capture selected properties of a concept while leaving other aspects unrepresented.

The SRP must therefore remain a principle of formal correspondence until stronger claims are independently established.

# 7\. Worked Example I — Identity

Identity is a particularly useful first test because it involves persistence through change.

## 7.1 Conceptual definition

For this example, identity is restricted to the question:

Under what conditions should states at different times be treated as belonging to the same continuing entity?

This is not a complete theory of personal identity.

It is a formal investigation of continuity and persistence.

## 7.2 State representation

Let an entity have states:

\[
x\_t\\in X
\]

where (t) indexes successive observations or stages.

A transition is:

\[
x\_{t+1}=T\_t(x\_t)
\]

Let the entity have a collection of candidate identity properties:

\[
I(x\_t)=
(i\_1(x\_t),i\_2(x\_t),\\ldots,i\_n(x\_t))
\]

Some properties may change while others remain stable.

## 7.3 Identity criteria

Define a relation:

\[
x\_t\\sim\_I x\_s
\]

when the specified continuity conditions between the states are satisfied.

For example, a digital agent may be regarded as continuous if it retains:

- A specified persistent identifier.
- A traceable state history.
- A defined continuity of operational configuration.
- An authorised transfer history.

These are operational identity criteria, not universal metaphysical conditions.

## 7.4 Invariant analysis

Suppose:

\[
I\_1(x)=\\text{persistent identifier}
\]

and:

\[
I\_2(x)=\\text{historical provenance}
\]

A transformation may change the agent's internal configuration while preserving both properties.

Then:

\[
I\_1(Tx)=I\_1(x)
\]

\[
I\_2(Tx)=I\_2(x)
\]

The system has preserved the selected identity criteria.

A different transformation may replace the persistent identifier or break the provenance chain.

The identity classification may then change.

## 7.5 Potential discovery

A formal model could distinguish:

- State continuity.
- Information continuity.
- Functional continuity.
- Historical continuity.
- Organisational continuity.

This would permit different theories of identity to be compared without assuming that they are equivalent.

**Result:** Identity can be mathematized at the level of specified persistence criteria.

Whether this exhausts personal identity remains an open question.

# 8\. Worked Example II — Knowledge

Knowledge provides a second test because it involves relationships between claims and evidence.

## 8.1 State representation

Define a knowledge system:

\[
K=(V,E,W,P,C)
\]

where:

- (V) is a set of propositions.
- (E) is a set of relationships.
- (W) represents evidence weights or assessments.
- (P) records provenance.
- (C) represents confidence or epistemic status.

Possible edge types include:

\[
E=
{
\\text{supports},
\\text{contradicts},
\\text{depends-on},
\\text{derived-from}
}
\]

This is a typed relational structure rather than a simple unlabelled graph.

## 8.2 Knowledge transformations

A new observation may update the knowledge state:

\[
K\_{t+1}=U(K\_t,e\_{t+1})
\]

where (U) is an update operator and (e\_{t+1}) is new evidence.

The update may:

- Add a proposition.
- Strengthen or weaken a claim.
- Introduce a contradiction.
- Invalidate a dependency.
- Change confidence.
- Require revision of derived conclusions.

## 8.3 Worked scenario

Suppose a scientific hypothesis (H) has three independent supporting experiments.

A fourth experiment contradicts the hypothesis.

The update system should not merely add a fourth record.

It should examine:

1. The independence of the experiments.
2. Their methodological quality.
3. Their measurement uncertainty.
4. Whether the contradiction concerns the same hypothesis.
5. Which dependent claims rely on (H).
6. Whether the contradiction requires revision of those claims.

A mathematical model could propagate the revision through the dependency network.

## 8.4 Potential invariant

One candidate invariant is provenance integrity.

Let:

\[
P(K)
\]

represent the integrity of the evidence-origin records.

A knowledge update should not silently alter the provenance of existing evidence:

\[
P(U(K,e))=P(K)
\]

for previously recorded evidence, unless an explicitly authorised correction is made.

This is an invariant of the record-management system, not a guarantee that the recorded evidence is truthful.

## 8.5 Potential discovery

A formal knowledge system could reveal:

- Which conclusions depend on a single fragile claim.
- Which claims are supported by independent evidence.
- Which contradictions have not been resolved.
- Which conclusions require reassessment after new evidence.
- Where confidence is disproportionate to evidential support.

**Result:** Knowledge can be modelled as a dynamic evidence-and-dependency system.

The epistemic validity of its contents still requires independent assessment.

# 9\. Worked Example III — Memory

Memory can be investigated through storage, retrieval, reconstruction and loss.

## 9.1 State representation

Let:

\[
M\_t
\]

represent the memory state at time (t).

A memory system may contain:

- Encoded information.
- Storage locations.
- Retrieval pathways.
- Associations.
- Reconstruction processes.
- Confidence assessments.

A simplified memory transition is:

\[
M\_{t+1}=R(M\_t,e\_t)
\]

where (R) represents retention, modification or reconstruction following experience (e\_t).

## 9.2 Retrieval

Define a retrieval operator:

\[
Q(M\_t,q)=r
\]

where (q) is a retrieval cue and (r) is the resulting recalled representation.

The retrieved result need not be identical to the original stored representation.

This creates an important distinction between:

- Original encoding.
- Stored representation.
- Retrieval output.
- Confidence in the retrieval.
- Accuracy relative to the original event.

## 9.3 Mathematical investigation

Possible measures include:

\[
L=1-\\frac{\\text{recoverable information}}
{\\text{reference information}}
\]

where (L) is a suitably defined information-loss measure.

The precise information measure must be chosen for the particular experiment.

Researchers could also investigate:

- Retrieval stability.
- Reconstruction error.
- Context sensitivity.
- Decay.
- Interference.
- Confidence calibration.

## 9.4 Structural resonance with knowledge

Memory and knowledge may share a structure involving retention, retrieval and revision.

However, they are not identical.

Memory concerns the retention and reconstruction of information.

Knowledge additionally concerns justification, truth conditions and epistemic status.

A structural mapping could preserve selected information relationships without making memory and knowledge equivalent.

**Result:** Memory provides a candidate domain for testing whether structural correspondence can reveal common mechanisms across different conceptual systems.

# 10\. Worked Example IV — Causality

Causality is an especially demanding test because mathematical dependency does not automatically establish causal influence.

## 10.1 Event structure

Let:

\[
V={e\_1,e\_2,\\ldots,e\_n}
\]

represent events.

Let:

\[
G=(V,E)
\]

be a directed graph of proposed dependencies.

An edge:

\[
e\_i\\rightarrow e\_j
\]

represents a proposed directional relationship.

## 10.2 Structural distinction

A directed edge may represent:

- Temporal precedence.
- Logical dependency.
- Statistical association.
- Proposed causal influence.

These meanings must not be conflated.

A graph alone does not prove causation.

## 10.3 Intervention

Suppose a system has variables:

\[
X\_1,X\_2,\\ldots,X\_n
\]

A causal model may specify structural equations:

\[
X\_i=f\_i(\\operatorname{Pa}(X\_i),U\_i)
\]

where:

- (\\operatorname{Pa}(X\_i)) denotes proposed parent variables.
- (U\_i) represents background factors.

An intervention changes a specified variable or mechanism.

The resulting predictions can be compared with observations.

## 10.4 Ω42 relevance

Ω42 could provide:

- State representation.
- Dependency tracking.
- Transformation analysis.
- Reversible reconstruction.
- Multiresolution event mapping.
- Invariant and constraint checking.

Its existing reversible Fibonacci dynamics could help investigate formal backward reconstruction.

However:

\[
\\text{Mathematical reversibility}
\\not\\Rightarrow
\\text{Physical retrocausality}
\]

A reversible mathematical system does not establish that future events physically influence the past.

**Result:** Causality can be represented mathematically, but causal interpretation requires additional assumptions and empirical intervention.

# 11\. Worked Example V — Awareness

Awareness is an important test because it contains both measurable and unresolved aspects.

The framework must not assume that all aspects of consciousness can already be represented mathematically.

## 11.1 Three distinct variables

Define:

\[
D=\\text{perceptual detection}
\]

\[
M=\\text{metacognitive monitoring}
\]

\[
C=\\text{subjective experience}
\]

These variables must remain conceptually distinct.

Detection and metacognitive monitoring can be investigated through experimental measures.

Subjective experience requires a separate theoretical and empirical treatment.

## 11.2 Experimental state

A simplified experimental state might be:

\[
x\_t=(D\_t,M\_t)
\]

An instrument could record:

- Stimulus presence.
- Detection response.
- Confidence.
- Response time.
- Accuracy.
- Trial conditions.

The mathematical representation can then be evaluated against observed data.

## 11.3 Calibration

For a collection of confidence predictions, a calibration measure may compare predicted confidence with observed accuracy.

For example:

# \[<br />\\operatorname{Brier}

\\frac{1}{N}
\\sum\_{i=1}^{N}(p\_i-y\_i)^2
\]

where:

- (p\_i) is the predicted probability of a correct response.
- (y\_i\\in{0,1}) is the actual outcome.

Calibration does not establish subjective experience.

Nor does a high calibration score establish that an agent is conscious.

## 11.4 Ω42 application

The Ω42 Awareness Research Instrument could investigate whether particular mathematical structures describe:

- Detection transitions.
- Confidence updates.
- Attention-related state changes.
- Memory-monitoring relationships.
- Metacognitive stability.

The candidate Fibonacci transformation:

\[
x\_{t+1}=Ax\_t
\]

must be treated as a hypothesis to test, not an assumed law of awareness.

The model should be compared with alternative transition models.

**Result:** Awareness research provides a demanding empirical test of whether conceptual mathematization can produce measurable explanatory value without overclaiming about subjective experience.

# 12\. The Ω42 Mathematical Laboratory

The existing Ω42 mathematical structures offer a useful environment for testing the methodology.

The canonical transformation is:

\[
A=
\\begin{pmatrix}
0\&1\\
1\&1
\\end{pmatrix}
\]

with:

\[
T(a,b)=(b,a+b)
\]

The shear transformation is:

\[
B=
\\begin{pmatrix}
1\&0\\
3\&1
\\end{pmatrix}
\]

with:

\[
T\_B(a,b)=(a,3a+b)
\]

These transformations have different mathematical properties.

## 12.1 The Fibonacci transformation

The transformation (A) generates the Fibonacci recurrence.

It also has the quadratic form:

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2
\]

with:

\[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x)
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q(Ax)^2=Q(x)^2
\]

The squared quadratic form is invariant under (A).

This gives the laboratory a precise example of a transformation that changes a signed quantity while preserving its square.

## 12.2 The shear transformation

The transformation (B) does not generally preserve (Q^2).

However, it preserves the coordinate valuation:

\[
v\_3(a,3a+b)=v\_3(a,b)
\]

where the valuation is defined using the minimum 3-adic valuation of the coordinates.

This demonstrates that different transformations can preserve different structural properties.

## 12.3 Combined transformation system

The generated group:

\[
G=\\langle A,B\\rangle
\]

provides a richer transformation environment.

# 13\. A General Theorem Candidate

The framework suggests a useful mathematical proposition.

## Proposition 13.1 — Invariant Transfer Under Exact Dynamical Representation

Let:

\[
T\_X:X\\rightarrow X
\]

and:

\[
T\_Y:Y\\rightarrow Y
\]

be transformations.

Suppose:

\[
\\phi:X\\rightarrow Y
\]

satisfies:

\[
\\phi\\circ T\_X=T\_Y\\circ\\phi
\]

Let:

\[
J:Y\\rightarrow Z
\]

be an invariant of (T\_Y), such that:

\[
J(T\_Yy)=J(y)
\]

for every (y\\in Y).

Define:

\[
I=J\\circ\\phi
\]

Then:

\[
\\boxed{I(T\_Xx)=I(x)}
\]

for every (x\\in X).

### Proof

By definition:

\[
I(T\_Xx)=J(\\phi(T\_Xx))
\]

Using dynamical compatibility:

\[
=J(T\_Y\\phi(x))
\]

Since (J) is invariant under (T\_Y):

\[
=J(\\phi(x))
\]

Therefore:

\[
I(T\_Xx)=I(x)
\]

as required.

\[
\\blacksquare
\]

### Interpretation

If a conceptual system can be represented exactly by a mathematical dynamical system, then invariants of the mathematical system transfer back to the conceptual domain through the representation.

This is a direct mathematical consequence of the definitions.

It does not establish that a particular real-world concept satisfies the assumptions.

The practical research challenge is therefore to identify and validate the representation (\\phi).

# 15\. The Problem of Arbitrary Mathematization

One of the greatest risks is constructing mathematical models that fit a concept only because the researcher has chosen definitions that guarantee the desired result.

For example, suppose a researcher defines identity as whatever remains unchanged under a chosen transformation.

The researcher then demonstrates that identity is invariant under that transformation.

This may be mathematically correct but conceptually circular.

The model has not independently discovered identity preservation. It has defined identity preservation into existence.

The framework must therefore include an anti-circularity requirement.

## Principle 15.1 — Independent Semantic Constraint

The core meaning and relevant criteria of the concept must be specified independently of the mathematical result being claimed.

## Principle 15.2 — Nontrivial Consequence Requirement

A successful model should produce at least one consequence not merely restating its defining assumptions.

## Principle 15.3 — Comparative Advantage Requirement

Where existing models are available, the Ω42 representation should be compared against them.

## Principle 15.4 — Failure Disclosure Requirement

The model must identify conditions under which its predictions fail or its representation ceases to be valid.

## Principle 15.5 — Alternative Model Requirement

The researcher should consider whether substantially different mathematical structures could represent the same concept.

This is necessary because a concept may admit several valid representations.

A representation's usefulness does not establish its uniqueness.

# 16\. The Ontological Question

The general theory raises a much larger question:

If different abstract concepts can be represented by corresponding mathematical structures, does this imply that reality itself has a common mathematical architecture?

Not automatically.

Four propositions must be separated.

### Proposition A — Mathematical representability

A concept can be represented mathematically.

### Proposition B — Structural correspondence

Two mathematical representations share a specified structure.

### Proposition C — Empirical realisation

The mathematical structures accurately model relevant features of observed phenomena.

### Proposition D — Ontological fundamentality

The structures are fundamental features of reality rather than useful descriptions of it.

The first three can be investigated through formal mathematics and empirical science.

The fourth requires additional philosophical and scientific argument.

The Ω42 programme should preserve this distinction.

Its central existential question remains open:

Does Ω42 merely provide a powerful mathematical architecture for representing and relating concepts, or does it reveal something fundamental about the structure of existence itself?

This paper establishes a methodology for investigating that question. It does not settle it.

# 18\. Proposed Validation Programme

A general theory should not be judged by how many concepts it can describe.

It should be judged by whether its representations are useful, rigorous, reproducible and capable of producing meaningful results.

The validation programme should proceed in stages.

Select concepts with well-defined mathematical components.

2. Memory as information retention and retrieval.
3. Knowledge as a relational evidence system.

Requirements:

- Independent implementation.
- Reproducible test cases.

## Phase 2 — Cross-domain validation

Select two or more domains with independently specified models.

Test whether nontrivial structure-preserving mappings exist.

This is important: a discovery system must demonstrate an ability to reject false or weak correspondences.

## Phase 3 — Predictive validation

Require the mathematical correspondence to generate consequences.

Predictions should be specified before evaluation wherever possible.

Use:

- Held-out data.
- Independent replication.
- Competing models.
- Perturbation testing.
- Sensitivity analysis.

## Phase 4 — Empirical validation

Develop controlled experiments for concepts that have observable consequences.

For example:

- Identity continuity in digital systems.
- Memory retrieval and reconstruction.
- Metacognitive confidence calibration.
- Causal intervention models.

The mathematical representation must be tested against real observations rather than selected solely for its elegance.

## Phase 5 — Generalisation

Determine whether the framework can formalise new concepts without requiring a completely new methodology each time.

A general framework should demonstrate reusable procedures.

It should not depend on the researcher manually constructing a special mathematical analogy for every example.
