# Ω42 Content Authenticity and AI-Origin Verification
## A Mathematical Framework for Multiresolution Content Analysis, Provenance, Transformation History and Epistemic Calibration

**Research Note F186 — Formal Mathematical Paper**
**Research programme:** Ω42
**Status:** Proposed mathematical framework; theoretical development and empirical validation required
**Subject areas:** Applied mathematics, information theory, statistical classification, digital forensics, artificial intelligence, provenance systems and computational verification.

---

## Abstract

The increasing production of text, images, audio and video through generative artificial intelligence creates a significant problem for digital content verification. Existing detection methods attempt to distinguish human-generated from machine-generated content using statistical, linguistic, forensic and machine-learning techniques. However, content may be generated, edited, paraphrased, translated, combined or transformed by both human and artificial agents. Consequently, a binary classification model is often insufficient to describe the actual production history of digital content.

This paper proposes Ω42 Content Authenticity and AI-Origin Verification (Ω42-CAV), a mathematical framework for representing and evaluating the origin, transformation history, authenticity evidence and uncertainty associated with digital content.

The proposed architecture combines five principal mathematical components:

1. Multiresolution content-state representation.
2. Transformation-history and provenance graphs.
3. Statistical evidence aggregation.
4. Epistemic calibration and uncertainty measurement.
5. Decision theory with explicit abstention.

A central distinction is introduced between statistical evidence of AI involvement and cryptographically verifiable provenance. These are complementary but logically different forms of evidence.

The framework also establishes an important limitation: no detector can reliably infer an author's production process from content characteristics alone when the observable distributions of human-produced and AI-produced content are indistinguishable. Therefore, Ω42-CAV is designed as an evidence-based verification framework rather than an infallible AI detector.

The paper proposes mathematical propositions, decision rules, verification procedures and empirical hypotheses that can be implemented and tested in a future Ω42 Verify product.

The ultimate objective is to establish a general mathematical architecture capable of assessing not merely whether content appears AI-generated, but how it originated, how it changed, what evidence supports its attribution and how confidently a conclusion can be justified.

**Keywords:** Ω42, AI-generated content, content authenticity, statistical classification, provenance, transformation graphs, modular state representation, uncertainty calibration, digital forensics, multiresolution analysis.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The problem

Generative AI systems can produce content that resembles human-created material. They can also modify existing human-created content.

This creates several distinct verification questions:

- Was the original material generated by a human or an AI system?
- Was AI used to edit or substantially rewrite human-created material?
- Was the material translated or paraphrased by AI?
- Has the content been manipulated after creation?
- Can the origin and transformation history be independently verified?
- What level of confidence is justified by the available evidence?

These questions cannot be adequately represented by a simple binary classification:

\[
H\in\{\text{Human},\text{AI}\}.
\]

A document may have been created through several successive processes. For example:

\[
C_0
\xrightarrow{\text{Human}}
C_1
\xrightarrow{\text{AI editing}}
C_2
\xrightarrow{\text{Human revision}}
C_3.
\]

The final document is neither exclusively human-generated nor exclusively AI-generated in a meaningful production-history sense.

Ω42-CAV therefore treats content origin as a potentially mixed and evolving property.

## 1.2 Research objective

The principal objective is to construct a mathematical framework that combines:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Content}
+
\text{Origin}
+
\text{History}
+
\text{Evidence}
+
\text{Uncertainty}
}
\]

into a unified verification process.

The framework should satisfy the following requirements:

1. Represent content at multiple resolutions.
2. Preserve the history of known transformations.
3. Distinguish statistical detection from provenance verification.
4. Measure uncertainty and calibration.
5. Identify mixed human–AI production.
6. Allow a system to abstain when evidence is inadequate.
7. Produce reproducible, auditable results.
8. Support independent testing and future certification.

## 1.3 Scope and limitations

This paper does not claim that the existing Ω42 Fibonacci transformation system independently detects AI-generated content.

The established mathematical components of Ω42 provide a foundation for structured state representation and transformation analysis. The proposed content-verification architecture is an application requiring additional mathematical development and empirical validation.

The distinction is essential:

\[
\text{Mathematical representability}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{Detection accuracy}.
\]

A successful mathematical representation must still demonstrate useful discrimination on independent data.

# 2. Mathematical foundations

## 2.1 Content space

Let \(\mathcal C\) denote a space of digital content objects.

An element \(c\in\mathcal C\) may represent:

- A text document.
- An image.
- An audio recording.
- A video.
- A combination of different media.

For the initial implementation, the principal domain is textual content.

Define a feature extraction mapping:

\[
\Phi:\mathcal C\rightarrow\mathcal X,
\]

where \(\mathcal X\) is a measurable feature space.

For a given content object \(c\), define its Ω42 feature representation:

\[
\boxed{
X(c)=
\left(
L,S,R,P,M,H,C
\right).
}
\]

The components are:

- \(L\): linguistic or structural features.
- \(S\): statistical generation indicators.
- \(R\): repetition and regularity measurements.
- \(P\): provenance evidence.
- \(M\): metadata.
- \(H\): transformation history.
- \(C\): contextual and source-related evidence.

These components are not assumed to be statistically independent.

Their joint distribution must be estimated or modelled appropriately.

## 2.2 Content-origin hypotheses

Define the latent production variable:

\[
Z\in\{H,A,M\},
\]

where:

- \(H\): human-originated content.
- \(A\): AI-originated content.
- \(M\): mixed human–AI production.

The mixed category is important because AI involvement can occur at different stages and at different intensities.

A more detailed representation introduces an involvement parameter:

\[
\lambda\in[0,1],
\]

where \(\lambda\) represents an operationally defined degree of AI contribution.

The interpretation of \(\lambda\) must be specified for each application. It might represent the proportion of text generated by AI, the proportion of transformations performed by AI, or a task-specific measure of AI contribution.

These definitions are not interchangeable.

There is no universal mathematical definition of authorship that makes every form of AI assistance reducible to one scalar.

Accordingly, Ω42 should retain both:

\[
Z=\text{production category}
\]

and

\[
\lambda=\text{defined contribution measure}.
\]

## 2.3 Bayesian evidence representation

Let \(E\) denote all available evidence.

The posterior probability of a production hypothesis is:

\[
P(Z=z\mid E)
=
\frac{P(E\mid Z=z)P(Z=z)}
{\sum_{z'}P(E\mid Z=z')P(Z=z')}.
\]

For three production categories:

\[
\boxed{
P(Z\mid E)=
\begin{pmatrix}
P(H\mid E)\\
P(A\mid E)\\
P(M\mid E)
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\]

The posterior probabilities sum to one.

However, these probabilities are meaningful only when the model, its training population, its assumptions and its calibration have been appropriately validated.

A numerical probability is not automatically a reliable probability.

# 3. The Ω42 multiresolution content architecture

## 3.1 Resolution hierarchy

A document should not be treated as an indivisible object.

Let a document \(c\) contain \(n\) ordered segments:

\[
c=(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n).
\]

Each segment may be analysed independently, subject to contextual relationships with neighbouring segments.

Define resolution levels:

\[
\mathcal R=
\{r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4\},
\]

where:

- \(r_0\): complete document.
- \(r_1\): document sections.
- \(r_2\): paragraphs.
- \(r_3\): sentences.
- \(r_4\): selected phrases or smaller units.

Let:

\[
\Phi_r(c)
\]

represent the feature mapping at resolution \(r\).

The collection of representations is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal X(c)=
\{\Phi_r(c):r\in\mathcal R\}.
}
\]

This structure permits a document-level assessment and a more detailed passage-level assessment.

## 3.2 Hierarchical consistency

A lower-resolution segment belongs to a higher-resolution parent segment.

Define a parent mapping:

\[
\pi_r:r_{i+1}\rightarrow r_i.
\]

The framework should preserve the relationship between parent and child segments.

For example, a paragraph-level finding must remain traceable to its position in the original document.

This creates a hierarchical evidence structure:

\[
\text{Document}
\rightarrow
\text{Section}
\rightarrow
\text{Paragraph}
\rightarrow
\text{Sentence}.
\]

A proposed consistency condition is:

\[
\mathcal E(r_i)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Trace}(\mathcal E(r_{i-1})),
\]

where \(\mathcal E(r)\) is the evidence associated with a resolution level.

This means that a finding at a lower level must be traceable to the larger document and must not become detached from its context.

The condition is a proposed design requirement, not an established theorem about all classification systems.

## 3.3 Mixed-authorship detection

Let:

\[
p_i=P(Z_i=A\mid E_i)
\]

be the estimated probability of AI-originated production for segment \(i\), under a binary segment-level model.

The document may contain a mixture of segment-level production categories.

A simple weighted summary is:

\[
\widehat{\lambda}
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i p_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i},
\]

where \(w_i\) represents a defined segment weight, such as word count.

This quantity estimates a weighted average of model outputs.

It must not automatically be interpreted as the actual percentage of AI-written words. That interpretation would require validated segment-level probabilities and an operational definition of AI contribution.

The system should therefore report:

1. Segment-level evidence.
2. Document-level summary.
3. Uncertainty.
4. The definition of the aggregation method.

## 3.4 Why multiresolution analysis matters

Suppose a document contains:

- 700 words of human-written introduction.
- 1,000 words of AI-assisted literature review.
- 300 words of human-written conclusion.

A single document-wide detector could obscure this mixed production history.

Ω42-CAV instead attempts to identify local evidence and preserve its relationship to the whole document.

This is a potentially useful architectural contribution because it changes the question from:

"Is this document AI-generated?"

to:

"Which parts of this document show evidence of particular production processes, and how strong is that evidence?"

# 4. Transformation-history mathematics

## 4.1 Content transformations

Let a transformation be a mapping:

\[
T_i:\mathcal C\rightarrow\mathcal C.
\]

Examples include:

- Human editing.
- AI generation.
- AI paraphrasing.
- Translation.
- Summarisation.
- Formatting.
- Image manipulation.
- Audio enhancement.

A content history is a sequence:

\[
c_0
\xrightarrow{T_1}
c_1
\xrightarrow{T_2}
c_2
\cdots
\xrightarrow{T_n}
c_n.
\]

The final content is:

\[
c_n=
(T_n\circ T_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ T_1)(c_0).
\]

Because transformations generally do not commute:

\[
T_i\circ T_j
\neq
T_j\circ T_i.
\]

For example, translating a document and then summarising it may produce a different result from summarising it first and translating the summary.

This noncommutativity is relevant to Ω42 because its transformation-history architecture already recognises that operation order matters.

## 4.2 Provenance graph

Define a directed graph:

\[
G=(V,E_G),
\]

where:

- \(V\) is the set of content versions and associated actors or tools.
- \(E_G\) is the set of recorded transformation relationships.

Each content node may be represented by:

\[
v_i=
(ID_i,h_i,t_i,a_i,\tau_i),
\]

where:

- \(ID_i\): content identifier.
- \(h_i\): cryptographic content hash.
- \(t_i\): recorded timestamp.
- \(a_i\): associated actor or system.
- \(\tau_i\): transformation type.

The graph is intended to preserve the sequence of known operations.

For an ordinary version history, the graph should be acyclic:

\[
G\text{ is a directed acyclic graph}.
\]

A more complex content object may have multiple parent versions, for example where a report combines human research notes, AI-generated summaries and external source material.

## 4.3 Cryptographic integrity

Let:

\[
h(c)=\operatorname{Hash}(c)
\]

be a cryptographic hash of content \(c\).

A transformation record may include:

\[
R_i=
(h(c_{i-1}),h(c_i),T_i,t_i,a_i,\sigma_i),
\]

where \(\sigma_i\) is a digital signature over the relevant record.

A valid signature and matching hash provide evidence that a particular record is associated with a particular content version and has not been altered in the specified manner.

However:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Cryptographic integrity}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{Truth of authorship claims}.
}
\]

A signed statement that an individual created a document does not independently establish that the individual actually wrote it.

Cryptography protects the integrity of the signed record. It does not guarantee the truthfulness of every assertion contained in that record.

This distinction is fundamental to Ω42-CAV.

## 4.4 Provenance completeness

A provenance graph may be incomplete.

Define a completeness indicator:

\[
\kappa(G)\in[0,1].
\]

This represents the proportion of required transformation information that has been captured under a specified recording policy.

For example:

\[
\kappa(G)=1
\]

would indicate that all required events were recorded and verified under that policy.

It would not establish that no unrecorded activity occurred outside the recording system.

The measure therefore concerns recorded provenance completeness, not absolute historical completeness.

# 5. Statistical detection and evidence fusion

## 5.1 Multiple detection channels

Let the system employ \(m\) detection channels:

\[
D_1,D_2,\ldots,D_m.
\]

For text, these could include:

- Linguistic feature analysis.
- Statistical predictability.
- Repetition analysis.
- Classifier-based detection.
- Semantic consistency.
- Comparison with known source material.
- Human expert assessment.

For images and other media, specialised forensic channels would be required.

The combined evidence is:

\[
E=(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_m).
\]

A naïve approach would multiply the likelihoods of the evidence channels:

\[
P(E\mid Z)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}P(E_j\mid Z).
\]

This factorisation is valid only under the appropriate conditional-independence assumptions.

In practice, multiple AI detectors may use similar models, datasets or linguistic features. Their outputs may therefore be strongly correlated.

Ω42 must not treat correlated detectors as independent witnesses.

A more general model is:

\[
P(E_1,\ldots,E_m\mid Z).
\]

This joint distribution may be estimated using a suitably validated statistical model.

## 5.2 Likelihood ratios

For two hypotheses \(H_A\) and \(H_H\), define:

\[
LR(E)=
\frac{P(E\mid H_A)}
{P(E\mid H_H)}.
\]

If:

\[
LR(E)>1,
\]

the observed evidence is more likely under the AI-origin hypothesis.

If:

\[
LR(E)<1,
\]

the evidence is more likely under the human-origin hypothesis.

A likelihood ratio is not itself a posterior probability.

Prior probabilities and the relevant decision context remain necessary.

## 5.3 Provenance evidence versus statistical evidence

Ω42 should maintain two separate evidence channels:

\[
E=(E_s,E_p),
\]

where:

- \(E_s\): statistical evidence.
- \(E_p\): provenance evidence.

Statistical evidence concerns characteristics of the content.

Provenance evidence concerns recorded creation and transformation events.

The two channels may reinforce each other, but they should not be collapsed into one unqualified score.

For example:

- A valid signed provenance record may explicitly identify an AI-generation event.
- Statistical analysis may independently identify characteristics consistent with AI generation.
- The two results can be compared.
- Disagreement should be reported rather than hidden.

# 6. An important mathematical limitation: identifiability

A content detector cannot always identify its subject's origin.

This is not merely a practical engineering problem. It is a mathematical limitation.

## Proposition 1 — Non-identifiability under observational equivalence

Let \(P_H\) be the probability distribution of observable evidence generated by human-originated content, and \(P_A\) the corresponding distribution for AI-originated content.

If:

\[
P_H=P_A,
\]

then no classifier based solely on the observed evidence can distinguish the two production categories with accuracy greater than the optimal prior-based decision.

### Proof

For any classifier:

\[
f:\mathcal E\rightarrow\{H,A\},
\]

the classifier receives evidence drawn from identical distributions under both hypotheses.

Consequently, the conditional distribution of its output is identical under the two hypotheses.

The classifier therefore cannot obtain information about the production category from the evidence.

Its optimal decision is to select the category with the larger prior probability.

It cannot systematically improve on that decision using the observed content.

\[
\Box
\]

### Interpretation

If human and AI-generated content become statistically indistinguishable under the selected feature representation, content-only detection cannot reliably determine origin.

This is why provenance and transformation records are important.

They provide additional information that may not be present in the content itself.

## Proposition 2 — Total-variation bound on detection

Assume equal prior probabilities for human and AI origin.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{TV}(P_H,P_A)
=
\sup_B|P_H(B)-P_A(B)|
\]

be the total variation distance between the two evidence distributions.

The optimal classification accuracy is:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Acc}^{*}
=
\frac{1+\operatorname{TV}(P_H,P_A)}{2}.
}
\]

### Interpretation

If:

\[
\operatorname{TV}(P_H,P_A)=0,
\]

then:

\[
\operatorname{Acc}^{*}=\frac12.
\]

The best possible classifier performs no better than chance under equal priors.

If the distributions are highly distinguishable, the attainable accuracy can increase.

This proposition provides a theoretical basis for evaluating whether an Ω42 detector is learning genuine differences between production categories or merely exploiting temporary dataset characteristics.

# 7. Decision theory and abstention

## 7.1 Why the system should be allowed to abstain

A detector should not be forced to make a definitive decision for every submitted item.

Define the possible decisions:

\[
\mathcal D=
\{H,A,M,\bot\},
\]

where \(\bot\) represents abstention or insufficient evidence.

Let:

\[
p=P(Z=A\mid E)
\]

for a binary decision setting.

Define:

- \(C_{FP}\): cost of falsely identifying human content as AI-generated.
- \(C_{FN}\): cost of failing to identify AI-generated content.

The expected loss of classifying the content as AI-generated is:

\[
L(A\mid E)=(1-p)C_{FP}.
\]

The expected loss of classifying it as human-generated is:

\[
L(H\mid E)=pC_{FN}.
\]

Choose the AI classification when:

\[
(1-p)C_{FP}<pC_{FN}.
\]

Rearranging:

\[
\boxed{
p>\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}.
}
\]

This is the optimal threshold for the simplified two-decision loss model.

## 7.2 Consequences for academic institutions

In an academic disciplinary setting, a false accusation can have serious consequences.

It may therefore be appropriate to assign a relatively high false-positive cost.

That produces a higher threshold for making an AI-origin classification.

In a low-consequence screening environment, a lower threshold might be acceptable, provided that the result is treated only as a request for further review.

The threshold should depend on the decision being made, not merely on the detector's technical convenience.

## 7.3 Abstention rule

Introduce an abstention cost \(C_\bot\).

The system should abstain whenever:

\[
L(\bot\mid E)
<
\min\{L(H\mid E),L(A\mid E)\}.
\]

This creates a mathematically defined reason for the system to report insufficient evidence.

The result is a more defensible verification framework than a system that always returns a binary answer.

# 8. Epistemic calibration

## 8.1 Calibration definition

Let \(p_i\) be a model's predicted probability and \(Y_i\) the actual outcome.

A model is calibrated when:

\[
P(Y=1\mid p=q)=q
\]

for the relevant prediction population.

For example, among cases assigned a probability near 0.8, approximately 80% should be positive under the defined ground-truth criterion.

Calibration does not mean that the model is always correct.

It means that its confidence corresponds appropriately to observed frequencies.

## 8.2 Brier score

For binary predictions:

\[
\boxed{
BS=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2.
}
\]

Lower values indicate better probabilistic accuracy.

## 8.3 Expected calibration error

Partition predictions into \(B\) bins.

Then:

\[
ECE=
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\frac{|S_b|}{N}
\left|
\operatorname{acc}(S_b)
-
\operatorname{conf}(S_b)
\right|.
\]

ECE is useful but depends on the binning method and should not be used alone.

The system should additionally report:

- Reliability diagrams.
- Log loss.
- Precision.
- Recall.
- False-positive rate.
- True-positive rate.
- AUROC.
- Area under the precision–recall curve.
- Coverage and selective risk.
- Performance across relevant subgroups.

## 8.4 Domain-dependent calibration

Let \(D\) represent the application domain.

Examples include:

\[
D\in
\{
\text{academic},
\text{journalism},
\text{business},
\text{legal},
\text{creative}
\}.
\]

The conditional distribution of features may differ across domains.

Therefore:

\[
P(Z\mid E,D_1)
\neq
P(Z\mid E,D_2)
\]

in general.

A detector calibrated on academic essays should not automatically be assumed calibrated on legal documents, translated text or social-media posts.

Ω42 should therefore maintain domain-specific calibration records.

# 9. Adversarial robustness and transformation resistance

## 9.1 Transformation-induced changes

Let \(T\) be a content transformation.

The original content is:

\[
c'=T(c).
\]

The transformation may include:

- Paraphrasing.
- Translation.
- Human editing.
- Summarisation.
- Style modification.
- Reordering.
- Formatting.

A detector is transformation-robust when its performance remains acceptable over a defined transformation class.

Let \(\mathcal T\) be that class.

Define robust risk:

\[
\boxed{
R_{\mathrm{rob}}(f)
=
\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}
\mathbb E[
\ell(f(\Phi(T(c))),Z)
].
}
\]

Here:

- \(f\) is the detector.
- \(\ell\) is a specified loss function.
- \(T\) is a permitted transformation.

The objective is to minimise the worst-case expected loss under the defined transformation class.

This does not guarantee resistance to every possible adversarial modification.

It provides a formal way to measure performance under specified transformations.

## 9.2 Transformation-history advantage

A content-only detector receives:

\[
E_s.
\]

A provenance-aware detector receives:

\[
(E_s,E_p).
\]

Where reliable provenance exists, the second system may possess additional information.

However, the provenance record must be validated, and its completeness limitations must be recorded.

A provenance-aware system may therefore offer stronger verification than content-only classification, but it cannot assume that every historical event has been captured.

# 10. Integration with the established Ω42 mathematical architecture

The existing Ω42 system has a canonical transformation:

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

It also includes the shear transformation:

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

These transformations generate a mathematically defined modular state system.

For content verification, the proposed use is an internal representation and state-management layer.

It is not a claim that AI-generated text follows Fibonacci dynamics.

## 10.1 Feature-state encoding

Let a selected feature vector be:

\[
x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_d).
\]

For a chosen modulus \(3^k\), an integer-valued feature representation may be encoded as:

\[
r_i=x_i\bmod 3^k.
\]

To preserve the original value, retain the quotient:

\[
\boxed{
x_i=3^kq_i+r_i,
\qquad
0\leq r_i<3^k.
}
\]

The quotient and residue must both be preserved.

Otherwise, distinct feature values may become indistinguishable after modular reduction.

For continuous features, an explicitly defined quantisation procedure is necessary before this encoding can be used.

## 10.2 State classification

The encoded content state may be represented as:

\[
\Omega(c)=
(q,r,k,\lambda,\pi),
\]

where:

- \(q\): quotient information.
- \(r\): modular residue.
- \(k\): resolution parameter.
- \(\lambda\): selected contribution or feature parameter.
- \(\pi\): provenance reference.

The state representation permits structured storage, comparison and retrieval.

It does not establish that the modular encoding improves AI detection accuracy.

That claim requires comparative experiments.

## 10.3 Transformation history and noncommutativity

Let \(W\) denote a word in the transformations \(A\) and \(B\).

For example:

\[
W_1=AB,
\qquad
W_2=BA.
\]

In general:

\[
AB\neq BA.
\]

The transformation sequence should therefore be recorded rather than reduced to an unordered collection of operations.

This provides a useful mathematical analogy and possible implementation mechanism for maintaining ordered content-processing histories.

## 10.4 Invariants and verification

An invariant is a function:

\[
I:\mathcal X\rightarrow\mathcal Y
\]

satisfying:

\[
I(Tx)=I(x)
\]

for a specified transformation \(T\).

The existing Ω42 quadratic structure provides an example:

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2,
\]

with:

\[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x).
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q(Ax)^2=Q(x)^2.
\]

This invariant could be used to test the correctness of the mathematical state engine.

It is not an invariant of human or AI authorship.

The distinction must remain explicit.

# 11. Formal Ω42-CAV system architecture

The proposed system is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Input}
\rightarrow
\text{Feature extraction}
\rightarrow
\text{Multiresolution analysis}
\rightarrow
\text{Provenance verification}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence fusion}
\rightarrow
\text{Calibration}
\rightarrow
\text{Decision}
}
\]

## 11.1 Mathematical processing function

Define:

\[
\mathcal V(c,G,D,\theta)
=
(R,\Gamma,\Delta),
\]

where:

- \(c\): submitted content.
- \(G\): available provenance graph.
- \(D\): declared application domain.
- \(\theta\): model and calibration parameters.
- \(R\): verification report.
- \(\Gamma\): confidence and uncertainty information.
- \(\Delta\): decision and evidence classification.

The function is only defined relative to the system's validated models, available evidence and specified operating conditions.

## 11.2 Verification report

A report should contain:

\[
R=
(ID,h,c_s,p_s,p_p,\kappa,\theta,v,t),
\]

where:

- \(ID\): report identifier.
- \(h\): content hash.
- \(c_s\): scope of analysis.
- \(p_s\): statistical findings.
- \(p_p\): provenance findings.
- \(\kappa\): provenance completeness measure.
- \(\theta\): model configuration.
- \(v\): software and model version.
- \(t\): assessment timestamp.

A report should also include:

1. The content examined.
2. The methods used.
3. The evidence identified.
4. The uncertainty.
5. The limitations.
6. The final assessment.
7. The conditions under which the assessment is valid.

## 11.3 Evidence classification

The system should use the following evidence categories:

**Category A — Cryptographically verified provenance**

A valid record supports a specific creation or transformation claim.

**Category B — Strong statistical evidence**

Multiple validated detection methods support a particular production hypothesis.

**Category C — Mixed evidence**

The content contains indicators consistent with more than one production process.

**Category D — Inconclusive evidence**

The evidence does not justify a reliable attribution.

**Category E — No detected indicators**

No relevant signals were detected. This is not proof of human authorship.

# 12. Proposed mathematical results and research hypotheses

The following propositions and hypotheses define future Ω42 research. They should not be represented as established theorems until their conditions and proofs have been completed.

## Proposition 3 — Provenance integrity is distinct from provenance truth

A cryptographically valid provenance record establishes the integrity of the signed record and its binding to the relevant content, subject to the security assumptions of the cryptographic system.

It does not establish that every recorded statement about authorship is factually true.

**Status:** Follows from the distinction between cryptographic integrity and the truth of assertions.

## Proposition 4 — Multiresolution reporting can preserve mixed-content evidence

If the segmentation and feature-extraction process retains traceable parent–child relationships, segment-level findings can be associated with their corresponding document regions.

This enables reporting of heterogeneous evidence rather than requiring one global classification.

**Status:** Architectural proposition; formal guarantees depend on the segmentation and traceability implementation.

## Conjecture 1 — Provenance-enhanced verification improves attribution

For suitable populations and sufficiently reliable provenance records, a system combining statistical evidence and provenance evidence will achieve lower expected attribution loss than a content-only system.

Formally, under suitable assumptions:

\[
R(f_{\mathrm{combined}})
<
R(f_{\mathrm{content}}).
\]

**Status:** Empirical conjecture requiring independent evaluation.

It is not guaranteed for incomplete, misleading or compromised provenance records.

## Conjecture 2 — Multiresolution analysis improves mixed-authorship assessment

For documents containing substantial mixtures of human and AI contributions, a validated segment-level system will provide more informative assessments than a single document-level binary classifier.

**Status:** Empirical conjecture.

## Conjecture 3 — Transformation-aware evaluation improves robustness measurement

A system trained and evaluated on explicitly recorded transformation histories will provide more informative robustness assessments than a system evaluated only on unmodified content.

**Status:** Research hypothesis.

## Conjecture 4 — Domain-specific calibration improves decision reliability

For heterogeneous content populations, appropriately maintained domain-specific calibration will improve probability reliability relative to a single global calibration model.

**Status:** Empirical hypothesis.

# 13. Experimental programme

A mathematical framework becomes a practical verification system only after empirical testing.

## 13.1 Dataset construction

The initial Ω42 dataset should contain five principal production conditions:

1. Entirely human-written content.
2. Entirely AI-generated content.
3. Human-written content edited by AI.
4. AI-generated content substantially revised by humans.
5. Mixed human–AI content.

Each document should have an appropriate ground-truth production record.

The experiment must not infer ground truth merely from the output of another AI detector.

## 13.2 Transformation testing

Each production category should be subjected to selected transformations:

- Paraphrasing.
- Translation.
- Summarisation.
- Human editing.
- AI editing.
- Style modification.
- Reordering.
- Formatting.

The resulting dataset should preserve the original and transformed versions.

## 13.3 Independent test sets

The dataset should be separated into:

\[
D_{\mathrm{train}},
\quad
D_{\mathrm{cal}},
\quad
D_{\mathrm{test}}.
\]

Where:

- \(D_{\mathrm{train}}\): model development.
- \(D_{\mathrm{cal}}\): probability calibration.
- \(D_{\mathrm{test}}\): independent final evaluation.

The same underlying document or near-duplicate material must not leak across these partitions.

Testing should also include previously unseen models, domains and writing conditions.

## 13.4 Evaluation metrics

At minimum, Ω42 should report:

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Precision | Reliability of positive classifications |
| Recall | Proportion of relevant AI-origin cases detected |
| False-positive rate | Human material incorrectly flagged |
| True-positive rate | AI material correctly identified |
| AUROC | Ranking discrimination |
| Brier score | Probabilistic accuracy |
| ECE | Calibration error |
| Selective risk | Error among cases not rejected |
| Coverage | Proportion of cases receiving a decision |
| Robustness | Performance under specified transformations |

The system should report the false-positive rate at the operating threshold used for high-consequence decisions.

Aggregate accuracy alone is inadequate.

## 13.5 Initial acceptance criteria

The following are proposed research targets, not demonstrated Ω42 performance:

- False-positive rate no greater than 1% on a clearly defined high-specificity evaluation population.
- Separate performance reporting for mixed-authorship material.
- Probability calibration assessed on held-out data.
- Robustness testing under paraphrasing and translation.
- Full model and dataset provenance.
- Reproducible evaluation.
- Abstention when evidence falls below specified thresholds.

If these targets cannot be achieved, the system must disclose the actual results rather than imply compliance.

# 14. Implications and potential applications

The mathematical architecture could support several applications.

## 14.1 Academic integrity

Universities could use Ω42-CAV to examine suspicious assignments, compare document versions and identify possible AI assistance.

The system should support investigation, not automatically determine misconduct.

## 14.2 Journalism and publishing

Publishers could use the system to examine the origin and editing history of submitted articles, images and other media.

Provenance and content analysis would be especially useful when evaluating potentially manipulated material.

## 14.3 Corporate document verification

Organisations could examine reports, marketing material, supplier submissions and external documents.

The objective would be greater transparency concerning production processes and document integrity.

## 14.4 Legal and insurance applications

The system could preserve file hashes, record transformation histories and generate technical reports.

Any legal or forensic application would require appropriate validation, expert oversight and chain-of-custody procedures.

## 14.5 Scientific research

Research organisations could record the extent and nature of AI assistance in manuscripts, code, data analysis and research documentation.

This could support transparency and reproducibility.

## 14.6 Ω42 AI certification

The proposed Ω42 AI Certification Authority could eventually evaluate content-verification systems against independently established requirements.

Certification would assess the performance of a detector and its documented operating conditions, not guarantee that every individual decision is correct.

# 15. Implications for the Ω42 mathematical uniqueness question

The development of Ω42-CAV introduces an important opportunity to test the broader architecture.

Three different questions must be distinguished.

### 15.1 Representation capability

Can Ω42 represent content, transformations, evidence and uncertainty?

This is an architectural and mathematical question.

### 15.2 Methodological advantage

Does Ω42 produce better verification, lower error rates, more informative reports or more efficient processing than conventional methods?

This is an empirical and computational question.

### 15.3 Foundational uniqueness

Is the Ω42 mathematical architecture uniquely required to solve the content-verification problem?

This is a much stronger claim.

To investigate it, define a comparison class:

\[
\mathcal C=
\{\text{admissible content-verification architectures}\}.
\]

A conditional uniqueness result would require:

\[
\forall X\in\mathcal C,
\quad
X\text{ satisfies the independently specified axioms}
\implies
X\cong\Omega42.
\]

The comparison class and axioms must be defined independently of the desired conclusion.

Even if such a result were established, it would prove uniqueness only relative to that mathematical class and its equivalence relation.

It would not prove that Ω42 is the fundamental structure of reality.

# 16. Discussion

The principal mathematical contribution proposed here is the integration of several different forms of verification into a common framework.

A content detector alone asks whether observed characteristics resemble those of AI-generated material.

A provenance system asks what recorded events occurred during the creation and transformation of the content.

A cryptographic system asks whether particular records and content bindings have been altered.

A calibration system asks whether reported confidence corresponds to actual performance.

Ω42-CAV combines these questions without treating them as equivalent.

This is important because the future of content authenticity is unlikely to be secured by statistical detection alone.

A determined producer may paraphrase generated material, remove metadata, translate it, combine it with human writing or otherwise modify its characteristics.

The more general verification problem is therefore one of reconstructing and assessing evidence about content production.

The framework also introduces an important commercial and institutional principle: a verification system should be capable of explaining why it reached a conclusion and when it lacks sufficient evidence.

An unreliable positive result should not be converted into an authoritative accusation.

The mathematical ability to abstain is therefore not a weakness of Ω42-CAV. It is one of its most important design properties.

---

# 17. Conclusions

This paper proposes Ω42-CAV as a mathematical architecture for AI-origin and content-authenticity verification.

Its principal components are:

1. A structured content-state representation.
2. Multiresolution analysis.
3. Transformation-history graphs.
4. Cryptographic provenance verification.
5. Statistical evidence fusion.
6. Calibrated probabilistic classification.
7. Decision theory and abstention.
8. Independent empirical evaluation.

The most important theoretical result is the recognition that content-only detection has a fundamental identifiability limitation when human and AI evidence distributions become indistinguishable.

This establishes why Ω42 should combine content analysis with provenance and transformation-history evidence.

The proposed architecture does not establish that Ω42 already detects AI-generated material more accurately than existing systems. That remains an empirical research question.

Nevertheless, it provides a coherent foundation for developing Ω42 Verify as a practical technology and for investigating the broader mathematical questions of authorship, transformation, authenticity and evidential certainty.

The wider implication is that Ω42 could evolve from a mathematical state-navigation framework into a general-purpose architecture for digital evidence verification.

That possibility is worth investigating, but its significance must ultimately be established through mathematical proofs, reproducible computational results and independent empirical performance.

# Most plausible explanation

The most promising mathematical contribution of Ω42-CAV is the combination of content-state representation, transformation history, provenance evidence and calibrated uncertainty.

The system's potential advantage lies in providing a richer and more defensible account of content production than a binary AI detector.

Its actual superiority over existing systems remains unproven.

Develop the programme in this order:

1. Formalise the content-state and provenance data structures.
2. Build the text-based multiresolution analysis engine.
3. Integrate cryptographic content verification and C2PA-compatible provenance.
