# Executive findings

This investigation began with the Ω42 transformation

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
\]

and its determinant-one square

\[
M=A^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The objective was to construct an explicit finite-dimensional quantum representation of \(M\), verify its unitarity and action on quantum operators, and investigate whether its quantum phase-space dynamics preserve the existing Ω42 mathematical structures.

The investigation produced seven principal findings.

1. **A direct quantum implementation exists.** For every odd integer \(N\), an explicit \(N\times N\) unitary operator can be constructed that implements \(M\) on the finite Weyl operators.

2. **The qutrit case is particularly simple.** Modulo 3, \(M\) has order four. Its quantum implementation \(U_3\) also has order four, and its square is the qutrit parity operator.

3. **The quantum and classical projective periods are exactly equal.** For this construction, the projective order of the quantum operator equals the order of \(M\) modulo \(N\).

4. **The existing Ω42 norm classifies quantum phase-space orbits.** For \(N=3^k\), the orbits of \(M\) on \((\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2\) are precisely the fibres of the existing quadratic norm, stratified by coordinate valuation.

5. **The quantum operator has a simple spectrum at powers of three.** The norm-fibre classification implies that the commutant of the quantum cat-map operator has dimension \(N\), which forces all \(N\) eigenvalues to be distinct.

6. **The original matrix \(A\) has an antiunitary quantum realization.** Its anti-symplectic action can be represented by an antiunitary operator, while \(A^2\) has a unitary Clifford representation.

7. **The existing Ω42 architecture needs a quantum-state layer.** It must distinguish Hilbert-space states, amplitudes, relative phases, Weyl operators, measurement contexts, quantum channels and composite states.

The fourth finding is the most important Ω42-specific result. It connects the existing norm, the 3-adic extension, the qutrit phase space and quantum operator dynamics in one mathematical construction.

# 1. Mathematical starting point

The canonical Ω42 matrices are

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M=A^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Their determinants are

\[
\det A=-1,\qquad \det M=1.
\]

The canonical quadratic form is

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2.
\]

It satisfies

\[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x),
\]

and therefore

\[
Q(Mx)=Q(x).
\]

Equivalently, defining the algebraic norm

\[
N(a+b\phi)=a^2+ab-b^2,
\qquad
\phi^2=\phi+1,
\]

we have

\[
Q(a,b)=2N(a+b\phi).
\]

The matrix \(A\) represents multiplication by \(\phi\), while \(M\) represents multiplication by \(\phi^2\).

These facts give us the initial mathematical structure needed for the quantum construction.

## 1.1 Symplectic interpretation

Introduce the standard symplectic matrix

\[
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For any real two-dimensional matrix \(R\),

\[
R^TJR=(\det R)J.
\]

Consequently,

\[
A^TJA=-J,
\]

whereas

\[
\boxed{M^TJM=J}.
\]

Thus:

- \(A\) is anti-symplectic.
- \(M=A^2\) is symplectic.

This distinction is central to the quantum construction.

In classical mechanics, symplectic transformations preserve the canonical phase-space structure. In finite-dimensional quantum mechanics, their discrete counterparts are represented by Clifford or metaplectic operators acting on Weyl operators.

## 1.2 Connection to the existing Lorentzian work

The eigenvalues of \(M\) are

\[
\lambda_\pm=\phi^{\pm2}.
\]

The corresponding phase-space Lyapunov exponents are

\[
\boxed{\chi_\pm=\pm2\ln\phi}.
\]

Numerically,

\[
2\ln\phi\approx0.9624236501.
\]

The Lorentzian representation developed earlier acts on symmetric \(2\times2\) matrices. This is the symmetric-square representation of the original two-dimensional matrix action.

Its eigenvalues are

\[
\phi^4,\quad1,\quad\phi^{-4}.
\]

The associated Lorentzian boost rapidity is therefore

\[
\eta=4\ln\phi\approx1.9248473002.
\]

This gives an exact representation-theoretic relationship:

| Domain | Representation of \(M\) | Hyperbolic exponent |
|---|---|---:|
| Quantum phase space | Two-dimensional symplectic representation | \(2\ln\phi\) |
| Lorentzian geometry | Three-dimensional symmetric-square representation | \(4\ln\phi\) |

The difference is not a numerical coincidence. It comes from the different representations of the same matrix.

# 2. Construction of the finite quantum cat map

We now construct a quantum operator implementing \(M\).

Let \(N\) be an odd positive integer and define

\[
\mathcal H_N=\mathbb C^N.
\]

Use the computational basis

\[
|0\rangle,\ldots,|N-1\rangle.
\]

Define

\[
\omega_N=e^{2\pi i/N}.
\]

The shift and clock operators are

\[
X|j\rangle=|j+1\pmod N\rangle,
\]

\[
Z|j\rangle=\omega_N^j|j\rangle.
\]

They satisfy

\[
ZX=\omega_N XZ.
\]

For odd \(N\), the inverse of 2 exists modulo \(N\). Write it as

\[
h_N=2^{-1}\pmod N.
\]

Define the symmetrically phased Weyl displacement operators

\[
\boxed{
D_N(q,p)=
\omega_N^{h_Nqp}X^qZ^p
}
\]

for

\[
(q,p)\in(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

The \(N^2\) Weyl operators form an orthogonal basis of the full \(N\times N\) matrix algebra.

They are the finite-dimensional quantum operators associated with discrete phase-space labels.

Importantly, these labels are not themselves the quantum state vectors.

## 2.1 Explicit unitary operator

For the Ω42 matrix

\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},
\]

define

\[
\boxed{
(U_N)_{jk}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
\omega_N^{\,j^2-jk+h_Nk^2}
}
\]

for \(j,k=0,\ldots,N-1\), with the exponent evaluated modulo \(N\).

Equivalently,

\[
(U_N)_{jk}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
\omega_N^{\frac{2j^2-2jk+k^2}{2}}.
\]

This is an explicit finite-dimensional metaplectic/Clifford implementation for the chosen symplectic matrix.

## 2.2 Proof of unitarity

Consider two rows \(j\) and \(\ell\).

Their inner product is

\[
(U_NU_N^\dagger)_{j\ell}
=
\frac1N
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
\omega_N^{j^2-\ell^2-(j-\ell)k}.
\]

If \(j=\ell\), the sum equals \(N\).

If \(j\ne\ell\), it is a complete sum of a nontrivial character of \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), and therefore vanishes.

Thus

\[
\boxed{U_NU_N^\dagger=I}.
\]

This proof applies to every odd \(N\), including composite odd dimensions.

## 2.3 Exact operator covariance

Direct calculation gives

\[
\boxed{
U_N X U_N^\dagger=D_N(1,1)
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
U_N Z U_N^\dagger=D_N(1,2).
}
\]

Consequently, for every phase-space label,

\[
\boxed{
U_ND_N(q,p)U_N^\dagger
=
D_N(M(q,p)).
}
\]

Written in coordinates,

\[
\boxed{
(q,p)\longmapsto(q+p,q+2p)\pmod N.
}
\]

This is the exact quantum correspondence we wanted.

The quantum operator does not merely reproduce a similar-looking recurrence. Its conjugation action on the Weyl operators is the same matrix transformation \(M\).

This is the finite-dimensional form of the Egorov correspondence.

# 3. Explicit qutrit construction

The first case is \(N=3\).

Here

\[
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
\qquad
h_3=2.
\]

The quantum Hilbert space is

\[
\mathcal H_3=\mathbb C^3.
\]

This is a qutrit.

The unitary is

\[
\boxed{
U_3=
\frac1{\sqrt3}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&\omega^2&\omega^2\\
\omega&\omega^2&\omega\\
\omega&\omega&\omega^2
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\]

## 3.1 Powers of the qutrit operator

Direct multiplication gives

\[
\boxed{
U_3^2=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\]

This is the parity operator:

\[
U_3^2|j\rangle=|-j\pmod3\rangle.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{U_3^4=I}.
\]

The exact quantum period is four.

## 3.2 Classical phase-space action

Modulo 3,

\[
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its square is

\[
M^2=-I\pmod3.
\]

Its exact order is four.

The action on the nine Weyl labels is:

\[
(q,p)\mapsto(q+p,q+2p).
\]

The complete orbit partition is:

| Orbit | Sequence | Length |
|---|---|---:|
| Zero | \((0,0)\) | 1 |
| First | \((1,0)\to(1,1)\to(2,0)\to(2,2)\) | 4 |
| Second | \((0,1)\to(1,2)\to(0,2)\to(2,1)\) | 4 |

The eight nonzero labels therefore form two four-cycles.

## 3.3 The Ω42 quadratic invariant classifies these cycles

Evaluate

\[
Q(q,p)=2q^2+2qp-2p^2\pmod3.
\]

For the first nonzero orbit,

\[
Q=2.
\]

For the second nonzero orbit,

\[
Q=1.
\]

The zero point has

\[
Q=0.
\]

Thus the three qutrit phase-space orbits are exactly the three possible values of the Ω42 quadratic form:

\[
\boxed{Q=0,\quad Q=1,\quad Q=2\pmod3.}
\]

This is the first manifestation of the norm-fibre theorem developed below.

It also explains why the qutrit case is particularly simple: the quadratic norm separates the two nonzero four-cycles.

# 4. Quantum periods and global phase

The next step was to construct the unitary operators numerically for several odd dimensions and compare their periods with the classical matrix periods.

Two different periods must be distinguished.

**Projective period:** the smallest \(P\) for which

\[
U_N^P=e^{i\theta}I.
\]

**Literal unitary period:** the smallest \(P_{\rm full}\) for which

\[
U_N^{P_{\rm full}}=I.
\]

The first is the physically relevant recurrence for an isolated quantum state considered as a ray. The second depends on the chosen phase lift.

The following results were obtained using the explicit kernel above.

The operator covariance was checked over all Weyl labels for dimensions through 19.

Numerical unitarity residuals were below approximately \(5\times10^{-15}\). Maximum Weyl covariance residuals were below approximately \(5\times10^{-14}\).

The period comparison is exact in principle, not merely an empirical fit, because of the argument in the next section.

## 4.2 The phase-lift result

For \(N=11\), the chosen unitary convention gives

\[
U_{11}^{5}\approx-iI.
\]

For \(N=19\),

\[
U_{19}^{9}\approx-iI.
\]

Thus the projective state returns after 5 or 9 iterations, but the literal matrix returns to identity only after 20 or 36 iterations.

The phase is convention-dependent: the metaplectic operator is determined by its Weyl conjugation action only up to a scalar. A consistent choice of phases can be made when constructing a full representation of the symplectic group.

However, a phase that is global for an isolated evolution can become relative in a controlled-unitary or interferometric construction.

This is a valuable finding for Ω42:

**Quantum phase cannot be discarded indiscriminately. Global phase, relative phase and representation-lift phase must be distinguished.**

# 5. Exact theorem: projective quantum period equals classical matrix order

This is a general consequence of the exact Weyl covariance.

Let \(P\) be the order of \(M\) modulo \(N\).

Then

\[
M^P=I.
\]

Using covariance,

\[
U_N^P D_N(v)U_N^{-P}
=
D_N(M^Pv)
=
D_N(v)
\]

for every Weyl label \(v\).

Therefore \(U_N^P\) commutes with every Weyl operator.

But \(X\) and \(Z\) generate the full matrix algebra \(M_N(\mathbb C)\). Their representation is irreducible. By Schur's lemma, every operator commuting with all of them is scalar.

Hence

\[
U_N^P=e^{i\theta}I.
\]

Conversely, if \(U_N^r\) is scalar, then its conjugation action is trivial. Since the Weyl labels are distinct,

\[
M^r=I\pmod N.
\]

We have therefore established:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{\rm projective}(U_N)
=
\operatorname{ord}(M\bmod N).
}
\]

This theorem applies to the explicit quantum implementation for every odd \(N\).

It is one of the strongest results of QM-01.

# 6. The Ω42 norm-fibre theorem

The most important new result comes from examining the quantum phase-space orbits at powers of three.

Recall

\[
Q(a,b)=2(a^2+ab-b^2)
\]

and

\[
N(a+b\phi)=a^2+ab-b^2.
\]

Let

\[
R_k=\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z.
\]

We identify the two-dimensional module \(R_k^2\) with the quadratic algebra

\[
\mathcal O_k=R_k[\phi],
\qquad
\phi^2=\phi+1.
\]

Under this identification:

\[
A:z\mapsto\phi z,
\]

and

\[
M:z\mapsto\phi^2z.
\]

The norm is multiplicative:

\[
N(zw)=N(z)N(w).
\]

Since

\[
N(\phi)=-1,
\]

we have

\[
N(\phi^2)=1.
\]

Therefore \(M\) preserves the norm exactly.

## 6.1 Why 3 is important

The polynomial

\[
x^2-x-1
\]

has discriminant 5.

Since 5 is not a square modulo 3, it is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\). Thus

\[
\mathcal O_1\cong\mathbb F_9.
\]

The prime 3 is inert in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\).

The norm from the quadratic extension to the base ring is surjective on units.

For level \(3^k\), the number of units in \(\mathcal O_k\) is

\[
|\mathcal O_k^\times|
=
(3^2-1)3^{2k-2}
=
8\cdot3^{2k-2}.
\]

The number of units in \(R_k\) is

\[
|R_k^\times|=2\cdot3^{k-1}.
\]

Consequently, the norm-one kernel has size

\[
\frac{8\cdot3^{2k-2}}{2\cdot3^{k-1}}
=
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
\]

## 6.2 Order of the Ω42 transformation

Modulo 3, \(\phi\) has order eight, so \(\phi^2\) has order four.

Now

\[
\phi^8=13+21\phi.
\]

Hence

\[
\phi^8-1=12+21\phi
=3(4+7\phi).
\]

The second factor is a unit modulo 3. Therefore

\[
v_3(\phi^8-1)=1.
\]

The standard lifting argument for odd-prime units gives

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(\phi^8)=3^{k-1}.
\]

It follows that

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(\phi^2)
=
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

This is exactly the size of the norm-one kernel.

Therefore \(\phi^2\) generates the entire norm-one subgroup modulo \(3^k\).

## 6.3 The theorem

For every primitive element \(z\in\mathcal O_k^\times\),

\[
z\langle\phi^2\rangle
=
z\ker N.
\]

But

\[
z\ker N
=
\{w\in\mathcal O_k^\times:N(w)=N(z)\}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Orb}_M(z)
=
\{w\in\mathcal O_k^\times:N(w)=N(z)\pmod{3^k}\}.
}
\]

**Theorem — Ω42 norm-fibre orbit classification**

At every resolution \(3^k\), the orbit of a primitive vector under \(M=A^2\) is precisely its quadratic norm fibre.

This is not merely a numerical observation. It follows from the norm-one subgroup and the exact order of \(\phi^2\).

The finite computations below provide independent checks of the theorem.

# 8. A new consequence: simple quantum spectrum

The norm-fibre theorem has a further consequence.

The \(N^2\) Weyl operators form a basis for all \(N\times N\) operators.

Under conjugation by \(U_N\),

\[
D_N(v)\mapsto D_N(Mv).
\]

Thus the conjugation action on the operator algebra is a permutation representation of \(M\) on its \(N^2\) phase-space labels.

An operator commutes with \(U_N\) precisely when it is fixed by this conjugation action.

The dimension of the fixed operator space is therefore the number of orbits of \(M\) on phase-space labels.

For \(N=3^k\), the norm-fibre theorem gives exactly

\[
3^k=N
\]

orbits.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\dim\operatorname{Comm}(U_N)=N.
}
\]

Now suppose \(U_N\) has distinct eigenvalues with multiplicities \(m_1,\ldots,m_r\).

The dimension of its commutant is

\[
\sum_{i=1}^r m_i^2.
\]

Since

\[
\sum_i m_i=N,
\]

we have

\[
\sum_i m_i^2\geq N,
\]

with equality only when every \(m_i=1\).

But the commutant dimension is exactly \(N\).

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
U_{3^k}\text{ has a nondegenerate spectrum for every }k\geq1.
}
\]

This is an exact consequence of the norm-fibre orbit theorem and the Weyl covariance.

It is one of the most interesting findings of this investigation.

# 9. The antiunitary representation of the original matrix A

The original matrix \(A\) has determinant \(-1\), so it is anti-symplectic.

Ordinary unitary Clifford transformations implement symplectic transformations. Anti-symplectic transformations instead admit antiunitary implementations.

Define

\[
R=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Complex conjugation \(K\) acts on the Weyl operators as

\[
KD_N(q,p)K^{-1}=D_N(q,-p).
\]

Thus \(K\) implements \(R\).

Now calculate

\[
S=AR
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is \(+1\), so it is symplectic.

Let \(U_N(S)\) be its corresponding unitary Clifford implementation.

Define

\[
\boxed{\Theta_N=U_N(S)K.}
\]

Then

\[
\Theta_ND_N(v)\Theta_N^{-1}
=
D_N(Av).
\]

Thus the original Ω42 matrix \(A\) has an antiunitary realization.

Furthermore, its square is unitary and implements \(A^2=M\):

\[
\boxed{
\Theta_N^2=e^{i\chi_N}U_N(M).
}
\]

For the qutrit construction and phase conventions used here, numerical calculation gives

\[
\boxed{\Theta_3^2=iU_3.}
\]

This is a very interesting connection.

The original Ω42 matrix separates into:

- an antiunitary quantum transformation associated with \(A\);
- a unitary quantum transformation associated with \(A^2\).

This resembles the mathematical distinction between antiunitary symmetries and unitary time evolution.

It does **not** prove that \(A\) represents physical time reversal. That interpretation would require a physical Hamiltonian and a corresponding transformation law for its observables.

# 10. Why the global phase distinction matters

Quantum mechanics represents pure states as rays:

\[
|\psi\rangle\sim e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle.
\]

A global phase is not observable for an isolated state.

But relative phase is observable.

For example, consider

\[
|\psi_+\rangle
=
\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}
\]

and

\[
|\psi_-\rangle
=
\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
\]

In the computational basis, both produce probabilities

\[
\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right).
\]

But after a qutrit Fourier transform, the distributions become

\[
P_+=
\left(\frac23,\frac16,\frac16\right),
\]

and

\[
P_-=
\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right).
\]

The relative phase changes the interference pattern.

This provides a direct test of the proposed Ω42 Quantum State representation.

A state cannot be reduced to a list of probabilities in one basis.

The quantum representation must preserve:

- amplitude magnitude;
- relative phase;
- coherence;
- the measurement basis or context;
- the resulting probability distribution.

The quantum cat-map calculation reinforces this point because the projective recurrence and literal unitary recurrence can differ.

# 11. Relationship to the 3-adic Ω42 architecture

The quantum construction also reveals an important distinction between two limits.

For the classical matrix,

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(M)
=
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
\]

The quantum projective period is exactly the same:

\[
\boxed{
P_{\rm quantum}(3^k)
=
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

Therefore the projective period increases without bound as the modular resolution increases.

This is consistent with the earlier 3-adic result that nonzero inverse-limit states are not periodic.

However, two different limiting procedures must not be confused.

**Semiclassical limit**

For quantum mechanics on a torus, increasing the Hilbert-space dimension \(N\) corresponds to decreasing the effective Planck constant, with the conventional scaling \(\hbar_{\rm eff}\propto1/N\).

**3-adic inverse limit**

The inverse limit

\[
\mathbb Z_3^2
=
\varprojlim_k(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2
\]

is a limit in the 3-adic topology.

These are not automatically the same physical limit.

# 14. Reproducibility: reference implementation

The following Python implementation constructs the quantum operator and checks its defining properties.

It requires NumPy.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import pi, sqrt

M = np.array([
    [1, 1],
    [1, 2]
], dtype=int)

def omega42_unitary(N):
    if N < 3 or N % 2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("N must be odd and at least 3")

    inv2 = pow(2, -1, N)
    omega = np.exp(2j * pi / N)

    U = np.zeros((N, N), dtype=complex)

    for j in range(N):
        for k in range(N):
            exponent = (
                j*j - j*k + inv2*k*k
            ) % N

            U[j, k] = (
                omega**exponent / sqrt(N)
            )

    return U

def weyl_operator(N, q, p):
    omega = np.exp(2j * pi / N)
    inv2 = pow(2, -1, N)

    X = np.zeros((N, N), dtype=complex)

    for j in range(N):
        X[(j + 1) % N, j] = 1

    Z = np.diag([
        omega**j for j in range(N)
    ])

    q %= N
    p %= N

    return (
        omega**((inv2*q*p) % N)
        * np.linalg.matrix_power(X, q)
        @ np.linalg.matrix_power(Z, p)
    )

def classical_order(N):
    I = np.eye(2, dtype=int)
    current = I.copy()

    for n in range(1, 100000):
        current = current @ M % N

        if np.array_equal(current, I):
            return n

    raise RuntimeError("Order not found")

def quantum_projective_order(U, tol=1e-9):
    N = U.shape[0]
    I = np.eye(N, dtype=complex)
    current = I.copy()

    for n in range(1, 100000):
        current = current @ U
        phase = np.trace(current) / N

        if (
            abs(abs(phase) - 1) < tol
            and np.linalg.norm(current - phase*I) < tol
        ):
            return n, phase

    raise RuntimeError("Projective order not found")

def scalar_phase_order(phase, tol=1e-9):
    value = 1 + 0j

    for r in range(1, 100):
        value *= phase

        if abs(value - 1) < tol:
            return r

    raise RuntimeError("Scalar phase order not found")

def verify(N):
    U = omega42_unitary(N)
    I = np.eye(N)

    unitary_error = np.linalg.norm(
        U.conj().T @ U - I
    )

    covariance_error = 0.0

    for q in range(N):
        for p in range(N):

            q2 = (q + p) % N
            p2 = (q + 2*p) % N

            lhs = (
                U @ weyl_operator(N, q, p)
                @ U.conj().T
            )

            rhs = weyl_operator(N, q2, p2)

            error = np.linalg.norm(lhs - rhs)

            covariance_error = max(
                covariance_error, error
            )

    classical = classical_order(N)

    quantum, phase = quantum_projective_order(U)

    full = quantum * scalar_phase_order(phase)

    return {
        "N": N,
        "classical_order": classical,
        "quantum_projective_order": quantum,
        "unitary_order": full,
        "phase": phase,
        "unitarity_error": unitary_error,
        "covariance_error": covariance_error
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for N in [3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19]:
        print(verify(N))
```

# 15. Limitations and unresolved questions

Several distinctions are important before this result is promoted into the formal Ω42 foundations.

1. The finite quantum cat map is a standard mathematical construction. Ω42 has not invented the general theory of quantum cat maps or Clifford representations.

2. The explicit matrix kernel is one phase convention. Other valid metaplectic lifts can differ by scalar phases.

3. The projective-period theorem is exact for the construction given here. The literal matrix period can depend on the phase lift.

4. The modular phase-space labels are labels of Weyl operators. They are not the complete set of physical qutrit states.

5. The calculations do not establish that physical space is 3-adic.

6. The calculations do not derive quantum mechanics from Ω42.

7. The norm-fibre theorem is a mathematical result based on the quadratic extension and norm-one subgroup. Its originality and priority require a literature review.

8. The quantum cat-map model is finite-dimensional. It does not yet constitute a model of relativistic quantum field theory.

9. The antiunitary representation of \(A\) does not establish that \(A\) is physically time reversal.

10. The qutrit and higher-dimensional computations do not yet test entanglement, contextuality, decoherence or experimental quantum transport.

These are the boundaries of the current result, not reasons to abandon the research direction.
