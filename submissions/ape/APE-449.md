## Abstract

This paper develops the mathematical structure of Ω42 from its canonical Fibonacci transformation through its extended matrix-group architecture.

The central construction begins with the matrix

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

acting on the finite state spaces

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

The resulting dynamics reproduce the Fibonacci recurrence and exhibit exact periodicity at every finite level. The paper establishes the order of the transformation, the complete orbit decomposition, the associated quadratic norm invariant and the behaviour of the system in the inverse-limit space \(\mathbb Z_3^2\).

The second construction introduces the shear matrix

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The generated group

\[
G_k=\langle A,B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)
\]

is characterised exactly. Its order, congruence-kernel structure, action on primitive vectors, valuation-stratified orbit decomposition and 3-adic closure are derived.

The resulting architecture has two distinct mathematical regimes:

1. The cyclic Fibonacci system, whose dynamics preserve a quadratic norm up to sign and produce highly structured orbit closures.
2. The Fibonacci–shear system, which expands reachability while preserving coordinate valuation.

The paper also examines the algebraic-number-theoretic interpretation, inverse-limit geometry, p-adic Lie structure, projective dynamics and possible generalisations to other primes, dimensions and transformation families.

Potential applications include finite-state navigation, arithmetic dynamical systems, computational algebra, formal verification, multiresolution classification, reversible computation and the mathematical modelling of structured state spaces.

No claim is made that the 3-adic architecture is the fundamental structure of physical reality. That remains an independent mathematical and empirical question.

**Keywords:** Ω42, Fibonacci matrix, modular dynamics, finite matrix groups, orbit decomposition, quadratic norm, p-adic integers, 3-adic Lie groups, congruence subgroups, state navigation, transformation systems.

T(a,b)=(b,a+b),

# 2. The canonical Fibonacci transformation

## 2.1 Definition

Let:

\[
R_k=\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z.
\]

Define:

\[
T_k:R_k^2\rightarrow R_k^2
\]

by:

\[
T_k(a,b)=(b,a+b).
\]

In matrix form:

\[
T_k(x)=Ax,
\]

where:

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Since:

\[
\det(A)=-1,
\]

the matrix is invertible over every \(R_k\).

Its inverse is:

\[
A^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1&1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Consequently:

\[
T_k^{-1}(a,b)=(b-a,a).
\]

The transformation is therefore exactly reversible.

## 2.2 Connection with the Fibonacci sequence

Let the Fibonacci numbers satisfy:

\[
F_0=0,\qquad F_1=1,
\]

and:

\[
F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n.
\]

Then:

\[
A^n=
\begin{pmatrix}
F_{n-1}&F_n\\
F_n&F_{n+1}
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad n\geq1.
\]

In particular:

\[
\boxed{
A^n
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
F_n\\F_{n+1}
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\]

Thus the Ω42 transformation is a matrix representation of Fibonacci recurrence.

More generally, for an initial state:

\[
x_0=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\a_1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

the resulting sequence satisfies:

\[
a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n
\]

with the appropriate initial values.

This is the first fundamental connection: Ω42's canonical dynamics are generated by a second-order linear recurrence.

# 3. Exact periodicity modulo powers of three

## 3.1 Characteristic polynomial

The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is:

\[
\chi_A(t)=t^2-t-1.
\]

Its discriminant is:

\[
\Delta=5.
\]

Modulo 3:

\[
5\equiv2\pmod3.
\]

Since 2 is not a square in \(\mathbb F_3\), the polynomial is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\).

Consequently:

\[
\mathbb F_3[A]\cong\mathbb F_9.
\]

The action of \(A\) modulo 3 can therefore be identified with multiplication by a nonzero element of the finite field \(\mathbb F_9\).

The multiplicative group \(\mathbb F_9^\times\) has order:

\[
|\mathbb F_9^\times|=8.
\]

Direct computation gives:

\[
A^4\equiv-I\pmod3.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\operatorname{ord}_3(A)=8.
\]

## 3.2 Lifting the order

The key matrix identity is:

\[
A^4=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&3\\
3&5
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Hence:

\[
A^4+I=
3
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Meanwhile:

\[
A^4-I=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&3\\
3&4
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Both matrices are invertible modulo 3 after the factor of 3 has been removed from the first expression.

It follows that:

\[
A^8-I=(A^4-I)(A^4+I)
\]

has exact 3-adic valuation 1.

We use the following lifting lemma.

**Lemma 1.** Suppose:

\[
M=I+3^sD,
\qquad s\geq1,
\]

where \(D\) is not zero modulo 3. Then:

\[
M^3\equiv I+3^{s+1}D
\pmod{3^{s+2}}.
\]

**Proof.**

Expanding:

\[
M^3
=
I+3^{s+1}D
+3^{2s+1}D^2
+3^{3s}D^3.
\]

For \(s\geq1\), the final two terms are divisible by \(3^{s+2}\). Therefore:

\[
M^3\equiv I+3^{s+1}D
\pmod{3^{s+2}}.
\]

\(\square\)

Repeated application shows that the exact 3-adic valuation of:

\[
A^{8\cdot3^r}-I
\]

is \(r+1\).

### Theorem 1 — Exact order of the Fibonacci matrix

For every integer \(k\geq1\):

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(A)=8\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

**Proof.**

The reduction of \(A\) modulo 3 has order 8.

The kernel of:

\[
GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)
\rightarrow GL_2(\mathbb F_3)
\]

is a finite 3-group. Therefore the order of \(A\) modulo \(3^k\) must be 8 multiplied by a power of 3.

The lifting lemma establishes that each increase in modulus from \(3^k\) to \(3^{k+1}\) introduces precisely one additional factor of 3 into the order.

Thus:

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{3^k}(A)=8\cdot3^{k-1}.
\]

\(\square\)

An immediate consequence is:

\[
A^{4\cdot3^{k-1}}\equiv-I\pmod{3^k}.
\]

The projective order is consequently:

\[
\boxed{
4\cdot3^{k-1}.
}
\]

# 4. Complete orbit decomposition

## 4.1 Coordinate valuation

For a vector:

\[
x=(a,b)\in R_k^2,
\]

define its coordinate valuation:

\[
v_k(x)=
\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b),k\}.
\]

The zero vector is assigned valuation \(k\).

The valuation is preserved under \(A\), because \(A\) and \(A^{-1}\) have entries in \(\mathbb Z_3\).

More generally, every matrix in \(GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)\) preserves the ideal generated by the coordinates of a vector.

## 4.2 Primitive vectors

A vector \(u=(a,b)\) modulo \(3^m\) is called primitive if:

\[
\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b)\}=0.
\]

The number of primitive vectors is:

\[
3^{2m}-3^{2m-2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
|\operatorname{Prim}(R_m^2)|
=
8\cdot3^{2m-2}.
}
\]

## 4.3 Period of a vector

Suppose:

\[
x=3^j u,
\]

where \(u\) is primitive modulo \(3^m\), with:

\[
m=k-j.
\]

The period of \(x\) modulo \(3^k\) equals the period of \(u\) modulo \(3^m\).

The irreducibility of the characteristic polynomial modulo 3 implies that no nonzero vector modulo 3 is fixed by \(A^n\) unless \(8\mid n\).

For \(n=8t\), the lifting argument gives:

\[
v_3(A^{8t}-I)=1+v_3(t)
\]

in the sense that the matrix difference is divisible by that power of 3 but not the next.

Its leading coefficient is invertible modulo 3. Consequently, when it acts on a primitive vector, it cannot vanish at a higher 3-adic precision than the matrix valuation permits.

### Theorem 2 — Exact period at each valuation

If \(x\neq0\) has coordinate valuation \(j\), then:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{period}(x)
=
8\cdot3^{k-j-1}.
}
\]

**Proof.**

Write:

\[
x=3^ju
\]

with \(u\) primitive modulo \(3^{k-j}\).

The reduction modulo 3 requires the period to be divisible by 8.

For \(n=8t\), the exact valuation of \(A^{8t}-I\) shows that:

\[
A^{8t}u\equiv u\pmod{3^m}
\]

holds precisely when:

\[
3^{m-1}\mid t.
\]

Thus the least possible positive period is:

\[
8\cdot3^{m-1}
=
8\cdot3^{k-j-1}.
\]

\(\square\)

## 4.4 Number of orbits

All primitive vectors at effective modulus \(3^m\) have the same period:

\[
8\cdot3^{m-1}.
\]

The number of primitive vectors is:

\[
8\cdot3^{2m-2}.
\]

Therefore the number of primitive orbits is:

\[
\frac{8\cdot3^{2m-2}}
{8\cdot3^{m-1}}
=
3^{m-1}.
\]

Summing over all valuation classes gives:

### Theorem 3 — Total number of Fibonacci orbits

The action of \(A\) on:

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2
\]

has:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{3^k+1}{2}
}
\]

distinct orbits.

**Proof.**

There is one zero orbit.

For each effective modulus \(3^m\), where \(1\leq m\leq k\), there are \(3^{m-1}\) primitive orbits.

Thus:

\[
N_k
=
1+\sum_{m=1}^{k}3^{m-1}.
\]

Using the geometric-series identity:

\[
N_k
=
1+\frac{3^k-1}{2}
=
\frac{3^k+1}{2}.
\]

\(\square\)

### Examples

| Modulus | Total states | Number of orbits | Orbit structure |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 3 | 9 | 2 | \(8+1\) |
| 9 | 81 | 5 | \(24+24+24+8+1\) |
| 27 | 729 | 14 | \(72\times9,\ 24\times3,\ 8,\ 1\) |
| 81 | 6,561 | 41 | \(216\times27,\ 72\times9,\ 24\times3,\ 8,\ 1\) |

The notation \(72\times9\) means nine orbits of length 72.

This exact classification is one of the most useful structural properties of the canonical Ω42 system.

# 5. The quadratic norm invariant

## 5.1 Algebraic-number interpretation

Let:

\[
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

Then:

\[
\phi^2=\phi+1.
\]

Associate a vector \((a,b)\) with:

\[
z=a+b\phi.
\]

Multiplication by \(\phi\) gives:

\[
\phi(a+b\phi)
=
b+(a+b)\phi.
\]

Thus the Fibonacci transformation is exactly multiplication by \(\phi\) in the quadratic algebra:

\[
\mathbb Z[\phi].
\]

The algebraic conjugate of \(\phi\) is:

\[
\phi'=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

Since:

\[
\phi+\phi'=1,
\qquad
\phi\phi'=-1,
\]

the norm is:

\[
N(a+b\phi)
=
(a+b\phi)(a+b\phi')
=
a^2+ab-b^2.
\]

Define:

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2.
\]

Then:

\[
Q(a,b)=2N(a+b\phi).
\]

## 5.2 Anti-invariance

Since:

\[
N(\phi)=-1,
\]

multiplication by \(\phi\) reverses the sign of the norm.

Therefore:

### Theorem 4 — Quadratic anti-invariant

For every \(x\in\mathbb Z^2\):

\[
\boxed{
Q(Ax)=-Q(x).
}
\]

Consequently:

\[
\boxed{
I(x)=Q(x)^2
}
\]

is invariant under \(A\):

\[
I(Ax)=I(x).
\]

This invariant extends naturally to modular arithmetic and to \(\mathbb Z_3^2\).

## 5.3 Orbit interpretation

The quadratic norm provides a second classification of the Fibonacci dynamics.

For a nonzero state, the value of \(Q\) alternates between:

\[
Q(x)
\quad\text{and}\quad
-Q(x).
\]

The squared quantity \(Q^2\) remains constant.

In the 3-adic inverse-limit setting, the quadratic norm also characterises the closure of a Fibonacci orbit, as established below.

# 6. The 3-adic inverse-limit system

## 6.1 Construction

The 3-adic integers are defined by:

\[
\mathbb Z_3=
\varprojlim_k\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z.
\]

The Ω42 inverse-limit state space is:

\[
\boxed{
S_\infty=\mathbb Z_3^2.
}
\]

The transformation remains:

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Each finite-level system is a reduction of the same infinite system.

The relationship between levels is:

\[
\mathbb Z_3^2
\rightarrow
(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

This provides a coherent multiresolution structure.

## 6.2 Periodic points

### Theorem 5 — Only zero is periodic in the 3-adic state space

The only periodic point of \(A\) in \(\mathbb Z_3^2\) is:

\[
x=0.
\]

**Proof.**

Suppose:

\[
A^n x=x
\]

for some positive integer \(n\).

If \(x\neq0\), write:

\[
x=3^ju
\]

with \(u\) primitive.

Then:

\[
(A^n-I)u=0
\]

in the quadratic extension associated with the irreducible polynomial \(t^2-t-1\).

Under the identification with \(\mathbb Z_3[\phi]\), this requires:

\[
(\phi^n-1)u=0.
\]

Since \(u\) is a unit in the unramified quadratic extension after removing its common factor \(3^j\), it follows that:

\[
\phi^n=1.
\]

But \(\phi\) is not a root of unity. Indeed, its real algebraic embedding satisfies \(\phi>1\), so no positive power can equal 1.

This is a contradiction.

Therefore \(x=0\).

\(\square\)

This produces a notable distinction:

- Every finite-level state is periodic.
- Every nonzero 3-adic state is nonperiodic.

The finite periodic systems are compatible approximations to an infinite system with no nonzero periodic points.

## 6.3 Orbit closures

The polynomial:

\[
t^2-t-1
\]

is irreducible modulo 3.

Consequently:

\[
\mathcal O=\mathbb Z_3[\phi]
\]

is the ring of integers in an unramified quadratic extension of \(\mathbb Q_3\).

Its residue field is:

\[
\mathcal O/3\mathcal O\cong\mathbb F_9.
\]

The multiplicative group of this residue field has order 8.

The element \(\phi\) reduces to an element of order 8.

The principal units of norm one form a one-dimensional 3-adic analytic group. The identity:

\[
v_3(\phi^8-1)=1
\]

shows that \(\phi^8\) generates the first nontrivial principal-unit layer and is a topological generator of that one-dimensional group.

It follows that:

\[
\overline{\langle\phi\rangle}
=
\{u\in\mathcal O^\times:N(u)\in\{1,-1\}\}.
\]

### Theorem 6 — Quadratic norm description of orbit closures

For nonzero \(x\in\mathbb Z_3^2\):

\[
\boxed{
\overline{\{A^nx:n\in\mathbb Z\}}
=
\{y\in\mathbb Z_3^2:Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2\}.
}
\]

**Proof outline.**

Under the quadratic-algebra identification, the orbit consists of:

\[
\{\phi^n x:n\in\mathbb Z\}.
\]

Its closure is:

\[
x\cdot
\{u\in\mathcal O^\times:N(u)=\pm1\}.
\]

Therefore its elements satisfy:

\[
N(y)=\pm N(x).
\]

Conversely, every \(y\) satisfying this norm condition belongs to the closure.

Since:

\[
Q=2N,
\]

the norm condition is equivalent to:

\[
Q(y)^2=Q(x)^2.
\]

\(\square\)

The theorem identifies a precise geometric description of the closure of the canonical Fibonacci orbit.

# 7. Projective dynamics

Consider the projective space:

\[
\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3).
\]

A primitive vector \((a,b)\) defines a projective point, with scalar multiples by units identified.

Under the quadratic-algebra identification:

\[
\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3)
\cong
\mathcal O^\times/\mathbb Z_3^\times.
\]

The Fibonacci matrix acts by multiplication by \(\phi\).

The quotient is a compact abelian group. The projective image of \(\phi\) has finite order 4 in the residue quotient and a topologically generating component in the one-dimensional principal-unit quotient.

### Theorem 7 — Minimality and unique ergodicity

The projective action induced by \(A\) on:

\[
\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Z_3)
\]

is minimal and uniquely ergodic.

**Interpretation.**

Minimality means that every projective orbit is dense.

Unique ergodicity means that there is a unique invariant Borel probability measure, namely the normalised Haar measure on the compact group.

This is a substantially different form of dynamics from the finite periodic action on \(S_k\).

The same transformation therefore exhibits:

- Exact finite periodicity.
- Nonperiodicity in the inverse limit.
- Dense projective orbits.
- A unique invariant probability measure on projective space.

# 8. The Fibonacci–shear extension

## 8.1 Definition

Introduce:

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

It acts as:

\[
B(a,b)=(a,3a+b).
\]

Its determinant is:

\[
\det(B)=1.
\]

Define:

\[
G_k=\langle A,B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z).
\]

This is the extended Ω42 transformation group.

The inverse shear is:

\[
B^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
-3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Both transformations are invertible at every finite level and over \(\mathbb Z_3\).

## 8.2 Noncommutativity

Direct multiplication gives:

\[
AB=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&1\\
4&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

whereas:

\[
BA=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&4
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{AB\neq BA.}
\]

The transformation history matters.

A word in the generators:

\[
W=A^{i_1}B^{j_1}\cdots A^{i_r}B^{j_r}
\]

must retain its operation order.

This is one of the defining features of the extended architecture.

## 8.3 Effect on the quadratic invariant

The shear does not generally preserve \(Q^2\).

For example:

\[
Q(1,0)=2.
\]

But:

\[
B(1,0)=(1,3),
\]

and:

\[
Q(1,3)=-10.
\]

Thus:

\[
Q(1,0)^2=4
\]

while:

\[
Q(B(1,0))^2=100.
\]

The original quadratic invariant is therefore not an invariant of the full Fibonacci–shear group.

The extended group retains coordinate valuation, but its orbit structure is larger.

# 9. Exact conjugate directions

Let:

\[
E_{21}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then:

\[
B=I+3E_{21}.
\]

For each integer \(i\), define:

\[
C_i=A^iBA^{-i}.
\]

Since conjugation respects multiplication:

\[
C_i=I+3A^iE_{21}A^{-i}.
\]

Modulo 3, the first-order directions of four such conjugates are:

\[
X_0=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
X_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
2&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
X_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&2\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
X_3=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

These matrices are trace-zero over \(\mathbb F_3\).

The matrices \(X_0,X_1,X_3\) are linearly independent and span:

\[
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

Since:

\[
\dim_{\mathbb F_3}\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3)=3,
\]

the conjugates of the shear generate all first-order special-linear directions.

This observation is the key to the exact group theorem.

# 10. The congruence kernel

Define:

\[
K_k=
\ker\left(
SL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)
\rightarrow SL_2(\mathbb F_3)
\right).
\]

Its successive congruence layers satisfy:

\[
K_r/K_{r+1}
\cong
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

Consequently:

\[
|K_r/K_{r+1}|=3^3.
\]

There are \(k-1\) nontrivial layers, so:

\[
\boxed{
|K_k|=3^{3(k-1)}.
}
\]

The lifting identity:

\[
(I+3^nX)^3
\equiv I+3^{n+1}X
\pmod{3^{n+2}}
\]

for \(n\geq1\) allows first-order directions to be lifted successively through all congruence levels.

The conjugate shears provide a basis of the first-order Lie algebra. Their successive powers provide the corresponding directions at every higher level.

### Proposition 1 — Generation of the principal congruence kernel

For every \(k\geq1\):

\[
\boxed{K_k\subseteq G_k.}
\]

**Proof.**

The conjugates \(C_i\) belong to \(G_k\cap K_k\).

Their first-order terms span:

\[
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

For each \(n\geq1\), raising the conjugates to appropriate powers of 3 produces elements whose first nonzero congruence terms span:

\[
K_n/K_{n+1}.
\]

Products of these elements add their leading Lie-algebra terms modulo the next congruence layer.

Thus the image of \(G_k\cap K_n\) in \(K_n/K_{n+1}\) is surjective at every level.
