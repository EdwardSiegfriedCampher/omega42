### F169 — The Controlled Shear Group: Finite-Level Orbit Saturation, Minimal Generators, and the Next Ω42 Architecture

# F169 — The Controlled Shear Group: Finite-Level Orbit Saturation, Minimal Generators, and the Next Ω42 Architecture

## Abstract

We investigate the group generated by the Fibonacci transformation

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
\]

and the congruence-controlled shear

\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The shear is congruent to the identity modulo \(3\), but it breaks the Fibonacci quadratic-square invariant at higher arithmetic levels. It therefore generates states outside individual Fibonacci orbits.

An exhaustive finite-level computation gives an unexpected result. For \(k=1,2,3,4\), the group

\[
G_k=\langle A,B\rangle
\pmod{3^k}
\]

has exactly one orbit for each coordinate-valuation stratum. These are precisely the orbits of the full \(GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)\) action.

The generated group is nevertheless much smaller than the full general linear group. Its computed order is

\[
|G_k|=8\cdot3^{3(k-1)}.
\]

This suggests that Ω42 may obtain maximal finite-level state accessibility from a substantially more economical operation group than \(GL_2(\mathbb Z)\).

The paper separates the finite computational results from conjectures about all levels and the 3-adic closure. It then examines the architectural consequences for Ω42, including state accessibility, operation history, contextual evaluation, and practical applications.

**Central conclusion:** the shear extension breaks the quadratic invariant and merges all Fibonacci orbits within each coordinate-valuation stratum at the tested levels. It achieves the full finite-level orbit partition without generating the full general linear group.

---

## 1. Introduction

The original Ω42 arithmetic dynamics are defined by

\[
x_{n+1}=Ax_n,
\]

where

\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For

\[
x=(a,b),
\]

the recurrence is

\[
T(a,b)=(b,a+b).
\]

The quadratic form

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2
\]

satisfies

\[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x).
\]

Consequently,

\[
I(x)=Q(x)^2
\]

is invariant under the canonical recurrence.

The finite-level Fibonacci action therefore partitions the state space into invariant orbits lying inside quadratic-square fibres.

Previous work examined two extensions.

First, algebraic conjugation introduced an additional operation preserving \(Q\), but F167 proved that it does not enlarge finite-level Fibonacci orbits.

Second, F168 introduced a shear operation that breaks \(Q^2\) while preserving coordinate valuation.

The present paper completes the next stage of that investigation: determine how much state accessibility this shear actually generates.

The result is unexpectedly strong.

The shear group is proper as an integral matrix group, but its finite-level orbits coincide with those of the full general linear group at all tested levels.

This separates two notions that must not be conflated:

\[
\text{size of the operation group}
\]

and

\[
\text{size of its state orbits}.
\]

A relatively small group can act transitively on a large state stratum.

## 2. Mathematical setting

Let

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

For a nonzero state \(x=(a,b)\), define its coordinate valuation by

\[
v_3(x)=\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b),k\}.
\]

The zero state has valuation \(k\).

A state of valuation \(j<k\) can be written

\[
x=3^ju,
\]

where \(u\) is primitive modulo

\[
3^{k-j}.
\]

Here primitive means that at least one coordinate is not divisible by \(3\).

The number of primitive vectors modulo \(3^m\) is

\[
3^{2m}-3^{2m-2}
=
8\cdot3^{2m-2}.
\]

The full \(GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^m\mathbb Z)\) action is transitive on primitive vectors.

It follows that the full general linear action has one orbit for each coordinate valuation.

The question is whether a much smaller group can reproduce this orbit partition.

## 3. The controlled shear

Define

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
B(a,b)=(a,3a+b).
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det B=1.
\]

Also,

\[
B\equiv I\pmod3.
\]

Thus the shear is invisible at the first finite level.

Define the generated integral group

\[
G=\langle A,B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(\mathbb Z).
\]

At finite level, write

\[
G_k=\langle \bar A,\bar B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z).
\]

The first task is to establish that \(B\) genuinely changes the canonical orbit structure.

## 4. Breaking the quadratic-square invariant

Take

\[
x=(1,0).
\]

Then

\[
Q(x)=2
\]

and

\[
Q(x)^2=4.
\]

Applying \(B\),

\[
Bx=(1,3).
\]

Hence

\[
Q(Bx)
=
2+6-18
=
-10.
\]

Therefore

\[
Q(Bx)^2=100.
\]

Modulo \(9\),

\[
Q(x)^2\equiv4\pmod9,
\]

while

\[
Q(Bx)^2\equiv1\pmod9.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
Q(Bx)^2\not\equiv Q(x)^2\pmod9.
}
\]

Since every power of \(A\) preserves \(Q^2\),

\[
Bx\notin\langle A\rangle x
\pmod9.
\]

The shear therefore reaches a state outside the original Fibonacci orbit.

This proves genuine finite-level orbit expansion.

It does not yet determine the size of the enlarged orbit.

## 5. Coordinate valuation is preserved

### Theorem 1 — Valuation preservation

For every

\[
M\in G
\]

and every

\[
x\in\mathbb Z_3^2,
\]

we have

\[
\boxed{
v_3(Mx)=v_3(x).
}
\]

### Proof

Both generators \(A\) and \(B\) have determinants that are units in \(\mathbb Z_3\):

\[
\det A=-1,\qquad \det B=1.
\]

Their inverses also have entries in \(\mathbb Z_3\).

Therefore every \(M\in G\) and its inverse preserve the submodules

\[
3^j\mathbb Z_3^2.
\]

Thus

\[
x\in3^j\mathbb Z_3^2
\iff
Mx\in3^j\mathbb Z_3^2.
\]

The largest such \(j\) is exactly the coordinate valuation.

Hence

\[
v_3(Mx)=v_3(x).
\]

\(\square\)

This gives an invariant that survives the enlarged action even though the quadratic-square invariant does not.

## 6. The group is proper in the full general linear group

### Theorem 2 — Integral properness

\[
\boxed{
G\ne GL_2(\mathbb Z).
}
\]

### Proof

Modulo \(3\),

\[
B\equiv I.
\]

Therefore

\[
G\bmod3=\langle\bar A\rangle.
\]

The matrix \(\bar A\) has order \(8\), so the reduction of \(G\) has order \(8\).

But

\[
|GL_2(\mathbb F_3)|
=
(3^2-1)(3^2-3)
=
48.
\]

Thus the reduction of \(G\) is a proper subgroup of \(GL_2(\mathbb F_3)\).

If \(G=GL_2(\mathbb Z)\), their reductions modulo \(3\) would be equal.

They are not.

Therefore

\[
G\ne GL_2(\mathbb Z).
\]

\(\square\)

This establishes an important fact:

A group can be proper as a matrix group while still acting transitively on the state sets of interest.

That is the phenomenon now observed in the finite-level computations.

## 7. Exact finite-level computation

We computed the generated matrix group modulo \(3^k\) and then calculated its orbits on every state in \(S_k\).

The computation uses finite group enumeration:

1. Start with the identity matrix.
2. Multiply by the generators \(A\) and \(B\), reducing every entry modulo \(3^k\).
3. Continue until no new matrices appear.
4. Apply the resulting finite group to every state.
5. Partition the state space into disjoint orbits.
6. Compare the result with the coordinate-valuation partition.

Because the finite matrix semigroup generated by invertible matrices in a finite group equals the generated group, this enumeration gives the full finite group.

### 7.1 Computed group sizes

| Level | Modulus | Computed group order |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(k=1\) | 3 | 8 |
| \(k=2\) | 9 | 216 |
| \(k=3\) | 27 | 5,832 |
| \(k=4\) | 81 | 157,464 |

These values satisfy

\[
\boxed{
|G_k|=8\cdot3^{3(k-1)}
}
\]

for every tested \(k\).

This formula is an observed finite-level pattern, not yet a proved theorem for all \(k\).

### 7.2 Computed orbit sizes

The results are:

| Modulus | Orbit sizes | Number of orbits |
|---|---|---:|
| 3 | \(1,8\) | 2 |
| 9 | \(1,8,72\) | 3 |
| 27 | \(1,8,72,648\) | 4 |
| 81 | \(1,8,72,648,5832\) | 5 |

At each level, there is exactly one orbit for each coordinate valuation.

In general, the observed pattern is

\[
\boxed{
1,\quad
8,\quad
8\cdot3^2,\quad
8\cdot3^4,\quad\ldots,\quad
8\cdot3^{2(k-1)}.
}
\]

The number of orbits is

\[
\boxed{k+1.}
\]

### 7.3 Comparison with the full general linear group

The full general linear group has exactly the same orbit partition.

For a state

\[
x=3^ju
\]

with \(u\) primitive modulo \(3^{k-j}\), its orbit has size

\[
8\cdot3^{2(k-j-1)}.
\]

There is one orbit for each \(j=0,\ldots,k-1\), plus the zero orbit.

Therefore the computed \(G_k\)-orbits and full \(GL_2\)-orbits coincide for \(k\leq4\).

### Computational result

For \(k=1,2,3,4\),

\[
\boxed{
G_kx
=
GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z)x
}
\]

for every state \(x\in S_k\).

This is a finite computational result, not yet a universal theorem.

## 8. A concrete example modulo 9

The example

\[
x=(1,0)
\]

illustrates the change.

Under the original Fibonacci action, the primitive orbit has length \(24\).

There are three primitive Fibonacci orbits modulo \(9\).

The shear maps \(x\) to

\[
Bx=(1,3),
\]

which has a different quadratic-square invariant.

The enlarged group orbit has size \(72\).

Thus the shear group merges all three primitive Fibonacci orbits into one orbit.

The state-space interpretation is:

\[
\underbrace{24+24+24}_{\text{three Fibonacci orbits}}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\underbrace{72}_{\text{one shear-group orbit}}.
\]

The zero orbit remains separate, and the valuation-one orbit of size \(8\) remains separate.

The new group therefore has three orbits modulo \(9\):

\[
\{0\},
\]

the nonzero states divisible by \(3\), and the primitive states.

This is precisely the coordinate-valuation partition.

## 9. The surprising mathematical interpretation

The result is unexpected because the group is much smaller than the full general linear group.

For example, modulo \(3^4=81\),

\[
|G_4|=157464.
\]

The full general linear group has order

\[
|GL_2(\mathbb Z/81\mathbb Z)|
=
48\cdot3^{12}.
\]

The shear group is therefore a small fraction of the full group.

Nevertheless, both groups produce the same state-orbit partition.

The reason is that orbit transitivity does not require every possible matrix operation.

A comparatively small collection of transformations can generate enough motion to connect every primitive vector.

This is the key mathematical distinction:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Full matrix-group generation is stronger than full state-orbit accessibility.}
}
\]

Ω42 may not need the full general linear group to achieve the same finite-level accessibility.

It may only need a small set of carefully selected generators.

## 10. A structural conjecture

The finite computations suggest the following description.

Define

\[
\Gamma_k=
\left\{
M\in GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z):
\det M\in\{1,-1\},
\quad
\bar M\in\langle\bar A\rangle
\right\}.
\]

The determinant restriction reflects the fact that the generators have determinants \(1\) and \(-1\).

The reduction restriction reflects the fact that \(B\equiv I\pmod3\).

### Conjecture 1 — Exact finite-level group description

For every \(k\geq1\),

\[
\boxed{
G_k=\Gamma_k.
}
\]

If true, this gives

\[
|G_k|=8\cdot3^{3(k-1)}
\]

for every \(k\).

It also identifies the generated group as the full preimage of the cyclic order-eight reduction group, subject to the determinant restriction.

The finite computations support the conjecture through \(k=4\).

A proof requires establishing that the conjugates of the shear generate the full determinant-one congruence kernel.

This has not yet been completed.

## 11. A 3-adic Lie-algebra route to the proof

Write

\[
B=I+3E_{21},
\]

where

\[
E_{21}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Conjugating \(B\) by powers of \(A\) produces elements of the form

\[
A^iBA^{-i}
=
I+3A^iE_{21}A^{-i}.
\]

Modulo \(3\), the conjugated matrices

\[
A^iE_{21}A^{-i}
\]

include matrices whose linear span is the three-dimensional Lie algebra

\[
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

This is evidence that the closure of the subgroup generated by \(A\) and \(B\) contains a large determinant-one congruence subgroup.

A complete proof must handle the lifting from first-order congruence information to all higher 3-adic levels.

In particular, it must establish that the generated subgroup contains the required higher congruence layers, rather than merely an open subgroup of the determinant-one group.

This is a promising proof route, not yet a completed argument.

## 12. The depth-controlled family

The shear can be generalised to

\[
B_r=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3^r&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad r\geq1.
\]

It satisfies

\[
B_r\equiv I\pmod{3^r}.
\]

Thus the operation is invisible through level \(3^r\), and becomes visible at level \(3^{r+1}\).

For \(x=(1,0)\),

\[
B_rx=(1,3^r).
\]

A direct calculation gives

\[
Q(B_rx)^2-Q(x)^2
=
4\left[(1+3^r-3^{2r})^2-1\right].
\]

The difference has exact 3-adic valuation \(r\):

\[
\boxed{
v_3\left(Q(B_rx)^2-Q(x)^2\right)=r.
}
\]

This proves that the quadratic-square invariant first changes at level \(3^{r+1}\).

The family therefore supplies a controlled arithmetic depth.

Whether the groups

\[
\langle A,B_r\rangle
\]

have the same eventual orbit partition and closure for every \(r\) remains open.

## 13. Consequences for the Ω42 mathematical architecture

The results now suggest a three-stage hierarchy.

### Stage I — Canonical Fibonacci dynamics

\[
G_0=\langle A\rangle.
\]

This preserves \(Q^2\) and generates the original Fibonacci orbit structure.

### Stage II — Controlled shear dynamics

\[
G_1=\langle A,B\rangle.
\]

This breaks \(Q^2\) while preserving coordinate valuation.

At the tested finite levels, it merges all Fibonacci orbits within each valuation stratum.

### Stage III — Full general linear action

\[
G_2=GL_2(\mathbb Z).
\]

This also acts transitively on each coordinate-valuation stratum.

The crucial qualification is that Stage II and Stage III have the same finite-level state-orbit partition at the tested levels, despite having different group sizes.

The hierarchy is therefore not a hierarchy of increasingly fine orbit partitions after Stage II.

It is a hierarchy of increasingly large operation groups with potentially different implementation costs and transformation histories.

This is a more precise architectural result than the original F168 proposal.

## 14. What this improves in Ω42

### 14.1 It identifies a smaller generator set for broad accessibility

If Conjecture 1 is proved, Ω42 will have a compact operational basis that achieves full finite-level valuation-stratum accessibility without admitting every matrix in \(GL_2(\mathbb Z)\).

This can simplify the mathematical specification.

It also provides a natural way to distinguish:

- the transformations actually used;
- the transformations mathematically available;
- the states that are reachable.

These are not identical concepts.

### 14.2 It creates a tunable distinction between invariant preservation and relaxation

The family \(B_r\) supplies a parameter controlling the first arithmetic level at which the canonical invariant is broken.

This creates a mathematical model of delayed access to a larger state space.

The parameter \(r\) should be interpreted as arithmetic depth, not as probability, consciousness, or the Striker's one-seventh allocation.

### 14.3 It separates accessibility from judgment

The enlarged group can connect states that were previously in different Fibonacci orbits.

But this does not produce contextual judgment automatically.

For a fixed arithmetic state \(x\), any ordinary deterministic observable \(f\) still has the global valuation

\[
v_x(f)=f(x).
\]

The F163 global-valuation result remains valid.

To obtain genuine contextuality, Ω42 would need additional structure, such as:

- incompatible evaluation domains;
- restricted joint observability;
- context-dependent state disturbance;
- nonclassical probability assignments;
- a formal rule governing when two evaluation contexts can be jointly realised.

The shear extension provides a richer state-accessibility structure, not a proof of contextuality.

### 14.4 It provides a cleaner protocol architecture

The Navigator can retain the canonical recurrence.

An optional extension engine can apply the shear.

The Striker can evaluate the resulting state or transformation history.

The Aperture can remain the formal interface between generated arithmetic information and context-dependent judgment.

The roles are no longer forced into one undifferentiated transition system.

## 15. Practical examples

The following examples illustrate possible uses of the mathematical architecture. They are design applications, not claims that the arithmetic model has already been empirically validated.

### Example A — A staged simulation

Suppose a simulation begins with

\[
x_0=(1,0)
\]

modulo \(9\).

The canonical engine generates states using \(A\).

All those states remain in the same quadratic-square class.

A second engine is allowed to apply \(B\).

It reaches

\[
Bx_0=(1,3),
\]

which has a different quadratic-square value.

The simulation can now explore all primitive states in the enlarged orbit.

A practical implementation could record:

- the canonical trajectory;
- the extension operation;
- the new invariant value;
- the enlarged orbit identifier.

The advantage is that the extension can be enabled or disabled without changing the original recurrence.

### Example B — Controlled exploration in a computational search

Imagine a search algorithm in which the canonical recurrence explores a restricted set of configurations.

A controlled shear is then introduced when the search requires broader coverage.

The algorithm can preserve the original trajectory as a reference while using the extension to explore additional states.

This is useful when one wants to compare:

- what the canonical dynamics reach;
- what the extended dynamics reach;
- what is added by relaxing a particular invariant.

The arithmetic construction provides an exact finite model for such comparisons.

It does not establish that the same mechanism is appropriate for every physical or computational search problem.

### Example C — Comparing different evaluation histories

Suppose two routes reach the same arithmetic state:

\[
x_1=A^n x_0
\]

and

\[
x_2=A^pBA^q x_0.
\]

The histories may differ, even if their terminal states coincide.

A protocol that records only the terminal vector loses the difference between the histories.

A protocol that records only the history risks treating equivalent terminal states as distinct.

The improved Ω42 architecture retains both records.

This allows the Striker to evaluate whether the history itself is relevant to a judgment, without confusing that question with arithmetic state identity.

### Example D — A depth-controlled evaluation

Suppose the protocol builder wants the extension to remain invisible through modulo \(27\).

Choose

\[
B_3=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
27&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
B_3\equiv I\pmod{27}.
\]

The extension first becomes visible modulo \(81\).

A test environment can therefore operate at lower levels without registering the new operation, while a higher-resolution environment can detect it.

This supplies a precise mathematical meaning for a staged-resolution protocol.

It does not mean that the deeper level is more real or more conscious.

### Example E — Comparing compact and expansive operation libraries

A software implementation could provide two operation libraries:

- a compact library containing \(A\) and \(B\);
- an expansive library containing a much larger collection of invertible matrices.

At the tested finite levels, both produce the same orbit partition.

The compact library may therefore be sufficient when the task is state reachability rather than arbitrary matrix manipulation.

This is a practical computational advantage if the general orbit-equivalence conjecture is established.

## 16. Speculative implications for consciousness and judgment

The mathematical results permit several hypotheses, but they do not prove them.

### 16.1 Multiple histories may belong to one accessible state class

A system may have many ways of reaching a state without those routes constituting distinct states.

This suggests a possible distinction between:

- what is reached;
- how it is reached;
- how it is evaluated.

For Ω42, this is a promising way to formalise the difference between Navigator generation and Striker judgment.

The mathematics supports the distinction between state and history.

It does not show that a history possesses subjective experience.

### 16.2 Invariant relaxation may model changes in determination

The original quadratic invariant constrains the canonical dynamics.

The shear relaxes that constraint while preserving coordinate valuation.

One possible interpretation is that a system can preserve a coarse identity while allowing finer structural determination to change.

This might eventually be useful in modelling transitions between restricted and expanded evaluation regimes.

But an arithmetic invariant is not automatically a physical law, and an expanded orbit is not automatically a set of simultaneously actual possibilities.

### 16.3 The Aperture may be better understood as an evaluation boundary

The architecture now suggests that the Aperture need not operate by creating new arithmetic states.

It might instead mediate between:

- a canonical generated state;
- an expanded accessible state class;
- a selected operation history;
- a contextual evaluation.

This is a potentially useful reformulation.

It should remain a design hypothesis until a precise selection and evaluation rule is derived.

### 16.4 The existential question remains unresolved

The enlarged operation group improves the mathematical account of accessibility.

It does not explain why the arithmetic system exists, why any state is actual, or why the Ω42 family should be ontologically fundamental.

The distinction between mathematical structure and actuality remains intact.

## 17. Protocol-builder specification

The protocol builder should now implement the following architecture.

### 17.1 Canonical engine

Retain

\[
x_{n+1}=Ax_n.
\]

The canonical engine remains the default and reference evolution.

### 17.2 Controlled extension engine

Add

\[
B(a,b)=(a,3a+b).
\]

Classify it as an invariant-breaking, valuation-preserving operation.

### 17.3 Group selection

Every run must identify the selected action group:

\[
\langle A\rangle,
\qquad
\langle A,B\rangle,
\qquad
GL_2(\mathbb Z).
\]

Do not infer the group from the final state.

### 17.4 State and history separation

Maintain distinct records for:

- arithmetic state;
- group-relative orbit;
- operation history;
- invariant profile;
- evaluation context;
- judgment.

An orbit identifier must always be associated with its action group and modulus.

### 17.5 Validation

The builder should test:

1. \(A\) has determinant \(-1\).
2. \(B\) has determinant \(1\).
3. \(B\equiv I\pmod3\).
4. \(Q(Ax)^2=Q(x)^2\).
5. \(Q(Bx)^2\) can differ from \(Q(x)^2\).
6. Coordinate valuation is preserved.
7. The finite-level group orbit agrees with the valuation stratum for \(k=1,2,3,4\).
8. The group size agrees with \(8\cdot3^{3(k-1)}\) for those tested levels.

The final two tests are empirical finite computations until a general proof is available.

### 17.6 Do not alter the constitutional architecture

The construction does not change

\[
\frac67+\frac17=1.
\]

It does not derive the Striker's allocation, create a new probability, or establish a new actual branch.

The arithmetic extension should remain separate from the constitutional specification.

## 19. Conclusion

The shear

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}
\]

breaks the canonical quadratic-square invariant and reaches states outside the original Fibonacci orbits.

Yet the finite-level computations reveal that the group it generates with \(A\) already acts transitively on each coordinate-valuation stratum for moduli \(3,9,27,81\).

Thus it reproduces the finite-level orbit partition of the full general linear group without being the full general linear group.

The result suggests a powerful design principle for Ω42:

**The operation group required to reach a state space may be much smaller than the group containing every possible transformation of that space.**

This improves the mathematical economy of the framework and clarifies the difference between operation vocabulary, state accessibility, and judgment.

The result does not establish contextuality, consciousness, simultaneous actuality, or ontological fundamentality.

Those questions remain open.

## Most plausible explanation

The shear and Fibonacci generators jointly produce enough independent transformations to connect all primitive finite-level vectors, while their determinants and modulo-three reductions still restrict the full matrix group they generate.

The system therefore exhibits maximal tested state accessibility without maximal matrix-group size.
