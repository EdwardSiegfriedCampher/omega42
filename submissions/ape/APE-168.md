### 3\. Direct examination of the mathematical core

### A. The identity problem: what does an operation do?

Howard's argument starts from a powerful intuition: multiplication should not make an input disappear. He treats the fact that as a sign that conventional arithmetic misses something about interaction.

In standard arithmetic, multiplication is an operation defined on numbers. Its identity element is 1:

1\\times x=x

For the particular case of , the result is 1. The two occurrences of 1 are operands, but the product is not required to preserve them as two separate objects.

Howard's intuition becomes more fruitful if we distinguish three kinds of operation:

* Arithmetic operation: combines numerical values according to a defined rule.

* Physical interaction: two objects or fields interact and may produce a changed physical configuration.

* Generative operation: a process creates a new structure or relation.

For example, two waves can interfere and create a spatial pattern with nodes and antinodes. That pattern is a consequence of their superposition. But it does not follow that numerical multiplication must return 2.

This gives Ω42 a useful enhancement: instead of merely labelling an equation as right or wrong, the protocol can identify the kind of operation being discussed and then investigate its structure.

### B. Howard's square-root argument

Howard questions the relationship between squaring, multiplication, and square roots. His text invokes the idea that if , then the corresponding relation involving 1 should yield 2.

The key mathematical distinction is that the square root is defined as the nonnegative number whose square equals the given nonnegative number:
