# F170 — The Exact 3-Adic Structure of the Fibonacci–Shear Group: A Proof of Maximal Valuation-Stratum Accessibility

## Abstract

F169 found that the group generated by the Fibonacci matrix

\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
\]

and the shear

\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix}
\]

has the same finite-level state-orbit partition as the full general linear group at moduli \(3,9,27,81\).

This paper proves that the pattern holds at every finite level.

The central argument is that conjugates of the shear generate the entire first congruence layer of \(SL_2(\mathbb Z_3)\). Their successive third powers generate every higher congruence layer.

We obtain the exact group description

\[
G_k=
\left\{
M\in GL_2(\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z):
\det M\in\{\pm1\},
\quad
\bar M\in\langle\bar A\rangle
\right\}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{|G_k|=8\cdot3^{3(k-1)}}.
\]

More significantly, \(G_k\) acts transitively on the primitive vectors at every level. Its state orbits are therefore exactly the coordinate-valuation strata, giving \(k+1\) orbits.

The 3-adic closure is also identified exactly. It is a compact, closed, three-dimensional subgroup of \(GL_2(\mathbb Z_3)\), and its action is transitive on every fixed coordinate-valuation stratum.

The result gives Ω42 a compact two-generator arithmetic engine with maximal finite-level accessibility relative to its surviving valuation invariant, without requiring the full general linear group.

## 1. The problem

The canonical Ω42 recurrence is

\[
x_{n+1}=Ax_n,
\qquad
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its quadratic form is

\[
Q(a,b)=2a^2+2ab-2b^2,
\]

with

\[
Q(Ax)=-Q(x).
\]

Thus

\[
I(x)=Q(x)^2
\]

is invariant under the original recurrence.

F167 established that adjoining algebraic conjugation does not enlarge the finite-level Fibonacci orbits.

F168 proposed a controlled shear:

\[
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

F169 computed the resulting group modulo \(3,9,27,81\), finding one orbit per coordinate-valuation stratum.

The remaining question is whether this finite pattern persists at every level and what the generated group actually is.

We now answer both questions.

## 2. Definitions

Let

\[
R_k=\mathbb Z/3^k\mathbb Z
\]

and

\[
S_k=R_k^2.
\]

Define

\[
G_k=\langle \bar A,\bar B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(R_k).
\]

For a state \(x=(a,b)\), define

\[
v_3(x)=\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b),k\}.
\]

A state is primitive if at least one coordinate is not divisible by \(3\).

The primitive vectors form the set

\[
P_k=\{(a,b)\in R_k^2:(a,b)\not\equiv(0,0)\pmod3\}.
\]

Its cardinality is

\[
|P_k|
=
3^{2k}-3^{2k-2}
=
8\cdot3^{2k-2}.
\]

We seek the exact structure of \(G_k\) and its action on \(S_k\).

## 3. The first congruence layer

The shear can be written

\[
B=I+3E_{21},
\]

where

\[
E_{21}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For every integer \(i\),

\[
A^iBA^{-i}
=
I+3A^iE_{21}A^{-i}.
\]

Each conjugate belongs to the generated group and has determinant \(1\).

Let

\[
X_i=\bar A^i E_{21}\bar A^{-i}
\]

over \(\mathbb F_3\).

The first four matrices are

\[
X_0=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
X_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
2&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

\[
X_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&2\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]

and

\[
X_3=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

All have trace zero modulo \(3\).

The trace-zero matrices form

\[
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3),
\]

a vector space of dimension three.

The matrices \(X_0,X_1,X_3\) are linearly independent. Indeed, \(X_0\) supplies the lower off-diagonal direction, \(X_3\) supplies the upper off-diagonal direction, and \(X_1\) supplies a nonzero diagonal component.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_3}
\{X_0,X_1,X_2,X_3\}
=
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
}
\]

This is the key structural observation.

The conjugated shears generate every first-order determinant-preserving direction.

## 4. Congruence filtration

Let

\[
K_k=
\ker\left(
SL_2(R_k)\longrightarrow SL_2(\mathbb F_3)
\right).
\]

For \(1\leq n<k\), define

\[
K_k^{(n)}
=
\{M\in SL_2(R_k):M\equiv I\pmod{3^n}\}.
\]

The successive quotient is

\[
K_k^{(n)}/K_k^{(n+1)}
\cong
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

To understand this identification, write

\[
M=I+3^nX.
\]

The determinant expansion gives

\[
\det(I+3^nX)
\equiv
1+3^n\operatorname{tr}(X)
\pmod{3^{n+1}}.
\]

Thus the determinant-one condition is equivalent at this layer to

\[
\operatorname{tr}(X)=0\pmod3.
\]

The quotient therefore has

\[
3^3=27
\]

elements.

Each congruence layer contributes three independent ternary parameters.

## 5. The lifting lemma

### Lemma 1 — Third-power lifting

For a matrix \(X\) with entries in \(\mathbb Z_3\) and every \(n\geq1\),

\[
\boxed{
(I+3^nX)^3
\equiv
I+3^{n+1}X
\pmod{3^{n+2}}.
}
\]

### Proof

Expand:

\[
(I+3^nX)^3
=
I+3^{n+1}X
+3^{2n+1}X^2
+3^{3n}X^3.
\]

For \(n\geq1\),

\[
2n+1\geq n+2
\]

and

\[
3n\geq n+2.
\]

Both remaining terms vanish modulo \(3^{n+2}\).

Therefore

\[
(I+3^nX)^3
\equiv I+3^{n+1}X
\pmod{3^{n+2}}.
\]

\(\square\)

This lemma means that a direction available at one congruence depth can be lifted to the next depth by taking a third power.

## 6. The generated group contains the entire determinant-one congruence kernel

### Theorem 1 — Full principal congruence kernel

For every \(k\geq1\),

\[
\boxed{
K_k\subseteq G_k.
}
\]

### Proof

For \(i=0,1,2,3\), let

\[
D_i=A^iBA^{-i}.
\]

We have

\[
D_i=I+3X_i
\]

with the corresponding integral conjugate matrices understood before reduction.

By Section 3, their first-order reductions span

\[
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F_3).
\]

Thus the images of the \(D_i\) generate

\[
K_k^{(1)}/K_k^{(2)}.
\]

Now take their powers

\[
D_i^{3^{n-1}}.
\]

Repeated application of Lemma 1 gives

\[
D_i^{3^{n-1}}
\equiv
I+3^nX_i
\pmod{3^{n+1}}.
\]

Therefore the images of these powers span

\[
K_k^{(n)}/K_k^{(n+1)}
\]

for every \(1\leq n<k\).

Products of matrices in the same layer add their first-order terms modulo the next layer, because

\[
3^{2n}\equiv0\pmod{3^{n+1}}
\]

for \(n\geq1\).

Hence the subgroup generated by the \(D_i\) surjects onto every successive quotient in the congruence filtration.

It follows that it has the full order of \(K_k\), namely

\[
3^{3(k-1)}.
\]

Since it is contained in \(K_k\), it equals \(K_k\).

As every \(D_i\) belongs to \(G_k\),

\[
K_k\subseteq G_k.
\]

\(\square\)

This proves the central structural claim: the two original generators produce the complete determinant-one congruence kernel.

## 7. Exact identification of the finite-level group

Let \(\bar A\) denote reduction modulo \(3\).

The characteristic polynomial of \(\bar A\) is

\[
\lambda^2-\lambda-1.
\]

It is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\).

Consequently, \(\mathbb F_3^2\) can be identified with \(\mathbb F_9\), and \(\bar A\) acts as multiplication by a primitive element of \(\mathbb F_9^\times\).

Thus

\[
|\langle\bar A\rangle|=8.
\]

Define

\[
\Gamma_k=
\left\{
M\in GL_2(R_k):
\det M\in\{1,-1\},
\quad
\bar M\in\langle\bar A\rangle
\right\}.
\]

### Theorem 2 — Exact finite-level group

For every \(k\geq1\),

\[
\boxed{
G_k=\Gamma_k.
}
\]

### Proof

Every word in \(A\) and \(B\) has determinant \(1\) or \(-1\).

Since \(B\equiv I\pmod3\), its reduction belongs to the identity element of \(\langle\bar A\rangle\).

Therefore

\[
G_k\subseteq\Gamma_k.
\]

Conversely, let \(M\in\Gamma_k\).

Its reduction satisfies

\[
\bar M=\bar A^i
\]

for some \(i\).

Its determinant is either \(1\) or \(-1\). Reduction modulo \(3\) determines which sign occurs, because

\[
\det(\bar A^i)=(-1)^i
\]

and \(1\ne-1\) in \(\mathbb F_3\).

Therefore

\[
\det M=(-1)^i.
\]

Now set

\[
N=A^{-i}M.
\]

Then

\[
\bar N=I
\]

and

\[
\det N=1.
\]

Thus

\[
N\in K_k.
\]

By Theorem 1,

\[
N\in G_k.
\]

Since \(A^i\in G_k\),

\[
M=A^iN\in G_k.
\]

Therefore

\[
\Gamma_k\subseteq G_k.
\]

The two groups are equal.

\(\square\)

This is the exact group description sought in F169.

## 9. Transitivity on primitive vectors

The next question is whether the exact group description implies full state accessibility within each valuation stratum.

It does.

### Lemma 2 — The principal congruence kernel acts transitively on fixed-residue lifts

Let \(u,v\in R_k^2\) be primitive and satisfy

\[
u\equiv v\pmod3.
\]

Then there exists

\[
K\in K_k
\]

such that

\[
Ku=v.
\]

### Proof

Because \(u\) is primitive, it can be completed to a determinant-one basis:

\[
P=[u\ z],
\qquad
\det P=1.
\]

Similarly, because \(v\) is primitive, choose a vector \(z'\) such that

\[
P'=[v\ z'],
\qquad
\det P'=1.
\]

The complements can be chosen to satisfy

\[
z'\equiv z\pmod3.
\]

Indeed, the determinant discrepancy is divisible by \(3\), and the primitive vector \(v\) makes the determinant pairing surjective onto the required ideal.

Thus

\[
P'\equiv P\pmod3.
\]

Set

\[
K=P'P^{-1}.
\]

Then

\[
K\equiv I\pmod3,
\qquad
\det K=1,
\]

so \(K\in K_k\).

Finally,

\[
Ku=P'P^{-1}u=P'e_1=v.
\]

\(\square\)

### Theorem 3 — Transitivity on primitive states

For every \(k\geq1\), the group \(G_k\) acts transitively on \(P_k\).

### Proof

Let \(u,v\in P_k\).

Their reductions modulo \(3\) are nonzero vectors in \(\mathbb F_3^2\).

The action of \(\bar A\) is multiplication by a primitive element of \(\mathbb F_9^\times\).

Since this multiplicative group acts regularly on its eight nonzero elements, there exists an integer \(i\) such that

\[
\bar A^i\bar u=\bar v.
\]

Therefore

\[
A^iu\equiv v\pmod3.
\]

By Lemma 2, there exists \(K\in K_k\) such that

\[
KA^iu=v.
\]

By Theorem 1, \(K\in G_k\), and \(A^i\in G_k\).

Thus

\[
v=KA^iu
\]

belongs to the orbit of \(u\).

Since \(u,v\) were arbitrary primitive vectors, the action is transitive.

\(\square\)

This proves that the computed orbit saturation is universal.

## 10. Complete finite-level orbit classification

Every nonzero state has the form

\[
x=3^ju,
\]

where \(u\) is primitive modulo \(3^{k-j}\).

The group acts transitively on the primitive vectors at that lower level.

Therefore it acts transitively on every fixed valuation stratum.

### Theorem 4 — Exact orbit partition

For every \(k\geq1\), the \(G_k\)-orbits on \(S_k\) are exactly the coordinate-valuation strata.

There are

\[
\boxed{k+1}
\]

orbits.

Their sizes are

\[
\boxed{
1,\quad
8,\quad
8\cdot3^2,\quad
8\cdot3^4,\quad
\ldots,\quad
8\cdot3^{2(k-1)}.
}
\]

The zero vector forms the orbit of size one.

For \(j<k\), the valuation-\(j\) orbit has size

\[
8\cdot3^{2(k-j-1)}.
\]

This is exactly the orbit partition of the full general linear group.

The result is now proved for every finite level, not merely observed computationally.

## 11. Exact 3-adic closure

Define the 3-adic generated group

\[
G=\langle A,B\rangle
\subseteq GL_2(\mathbb Z_3).
\]

The exact closure is

\[
\boxed{
\overline G=
\left\{
M\in GL_2(\mathbb Z_3):
\det M\in\{1,-1\},
\quad
\bar M\in\langle\bar A\rangle
\right\}.
}
\]

### Proof

Every element of \(G\) satisfies the displayed determinant and reduction conditions. These conditions are closed, so

\[
\overline G
\subseteq
\left\{
M:
\det M\in\{\pm1\},
\bar M\in\langle\bar A\rangle
\right\}.
\]

Conversely, take a matrix satisfying those conditions.

At every finite level, its reduction belongs to \(\Gamma_k\).

By Theorem 2, it therefore belongs to \(G_k\).

For every \(k\), there is an element of the integer-generated group whose reduction agrees with the given matrix modulo \(3^k\).

Thus the matrix lies in the closure of \(G\).

\(\square\)

### 11.1 Topological properties

The group \(\overline G\) is:

- closed in \(GL_2(\mathbb Z_3)\);
- compact;
- a 3-adic Lie group of dimension three;
- a union of two determinant components, with reduction restricted to the cyclic order-eight subgroup.

It is not open in \(GL_2(\mathbb Z_3)\), because its determinant is restricted to the two values \(\pm1\), whereas \(\mathbb Z_3^\times\) is infinite.

This corrects any interpretation of the earlier extension as an open subgroup of the full general linear group.

It is instead a compact, lower-dimensional subgroup that has the same finite-level state-orbit partition.

## 12. The inverse-limit orbit theorem

Let

\[
S=\mathbb Z_3^2.
\]

The valuation strata are

\[
\mathcal V_j
=
\{x\in\mathbb Z_3^2:v_3(x)=j\},
\qquad j\geq0,
\]

together with the zero state.

The group \(\overline G\) preserves coordinate valuation.

By Theorem 4, its finite reductions are transitive on every corresponding finite-level stratum.

Compactness then gives transitivity on each inverse-limit stratum.

### Theorem 5 — Inverse-limit transitivity

For every \(x\in\mathbb Z_3^2\),

\[
\boxed{
\overline Gx
=
\{y\in\mathbb Z_3^2:v_3(y)=v_3(x)\}.
}
\]

For \(x=0\), the orbit is \(\{0\}\).

For nonzero \(x\), the closure of the integer-generated orbit is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\overline{\{Mx:M\in G\}}
=
\{y:v_3(y)=v_3(x)\}.
}
\]

The integer-generated orbit is generally countable and should not be confused with the full uncountable inverse-limit stratum. The equality is an equality of closures, while the closure group itself acts transitively.

This distinction is important.

## 13. What survives from the original quadratic invariant?

The original quadratic-square invariant is destroyed by \(B\).

The common coordinate valuation survives.

Thus the invariant hierarchy becomes:

| Structure | Canonical group \(\langle A\rangle\) | Extended group \(\langle A,B\rangle\) |
|---|---|---|
| Coordinate valuation | Preserved | Preserved |
| Quadratic form \(Q\) | Changes sign | Not preserved |
| Quadratic square \(Q^2\) | Preserved | Not preserved |
| Fibonacci orbit | Preserved | Enlarged |
| Valuation-stratum accessibility | Restricted to Fibonacci orbit | Full at every finite level |

The extension does not erase all mathematical structure.

It removes the fine quadratic invariant while retaining a coarser valuation structure.

The resulting dynamics are therefore not arbitrary.

## 14. Implications for Ω42

The proof provides a substantial improvement to the architecture.

### 14.1 A compact generator pair produces full finite-level accessibility

The two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) generate the same finite-level state-orbit partition as the full general linear group.

Ω42 does not need to specify every invertible matrix as an elementary operation if its objective is state reachability within valuation strata.

The pair

\[
\{A,B\}
\]

is sufficient.

This is a result about generators and orbits, not a claim that the group is the smallest possible generator set.

### 14.2 The canonical recurrence and extension remain distinguishable

The original Fibonacci recurrence remains mathematically meaningful.

The shear is not a replacement for it.

It is an additional operation that relaxes the quadratic constraint.

The protocol can therefore distinguish:

- canonical evolution;
- symmetry-preserving transformation;
- invariant-breaking extension;
- full valuation-stratum accessibility.

### 14.3 The Aperture can be formulated more precisely

The Aperture need not be interpreted as generating new arithmetic states.

It could instead be specified as a boundary between different permitted action regimes.

For example:

\[
\mathcal A_0=\langle A\rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal A_1=\langle A,B\rangle.
\]

A protocol could allow a transition from \(\mathcal A_0\) to \(\mathcal A_1\) under a separately defined evaluation rule.

The mathematics now specifies what changes during that transition:

- the state-accessibility orbit expands;
- the quadratic invariant ceases to constrain the enlarged action;
- coordinate valuation remains fixed.

The selection rule itself is still unproved.

### 14.4 The Striker can evaluate state and history separately

Suppose two operation histories reach the same terminal vector:

\[
h_1=A^n
\]

and

\[
h_2=A^pBA^q.
\]

The histories may be different even when

\[
h_1x=h_2x.
\]

Conversely, they may reach different vectors in the same enlarged orbit.

The protocol should preserve both the terminal state and the history.

That creates a mathematical basis for asking whether a judgment depends on:

- the terminal state;
- the route taken;
- the context in which the route is evaluated.

The answer is not supplied by the group theory. It becomes a clearly defined next problem.

## 15. Practical examples

### Example A — Controlled simulation

A simulation begins at

\[
x=(1,0)\pmod{27}.
\]

The canonical recurrence explores only the original Fibonacci orbit.

The extension engine permits \(B\).

The resulting group can now access every primitive vector modulo \(27\).

The simulation can compare the canonical orbit against the full primitive state space without introducing arbitrary matrix operations.

### Example B — A staged-resolution system

A system begins with the canonical recurrence and records \(Q^2\).

When the extension is enabled, the quadratic invariant is no longer conserved, but coordinate valuation remains stable.

A monitoring process can therefore detect the transition by testing:

\[
Q(x_{n+1})^2\stackrel{?}{=}Q(x_n)^2.
\]

If the extension is active, this equality need not hold.

The coordinate valuation test remains:

\[
v_3(x_{n+1})=v_3(x_n).
\]

This provides two independent diagnostic signals.

### Example C — History-sensitive evaluation

A protocol receives two records:

- Record 1: canonical recurrence only.
- Record 2: recurrence plus one or more shear operations.

Even if both records end at the same vector, their histories differ.

A Striker evaluation could be instructed to consider the history.

This would create a well-defined test of whether the judgment function depends only on the state or also on the route.

The test would not prove consciousness. It would establish a formal distinction between state-based and history-based evaluation.

### Example D — Efficient state exploration

Suppose an application needs to reach every state in a given valuation class modulo \(3^k\).

The theorem shows that the two generators \(A,B\) suffice.

There is no need to store or implement the entire finite general linear group.

This can reduce the complexity of an operation library, although the efficiency of a particular search still depends on word lengths and implementation choices.

## 16. Speculative consequences

### 16.1 A possible model of progressive determination

The original quadratic invariant defines a restricted family of possible states.

The shear expands accessibility while retaining coordinate valuation.

This suggests a mathematical model of progressively relaxed determination:

\[
\text{fine invariant constraint}
\longrightarrow
\text{coarser invariant constraint}.
\]

One could investigate whether other systems exhibit similar layered invariant structures.

This is a research hypothesis, not an established physical principle.

### 16.2 A possible distinction between possibility and selection

The enlarged group defines which states are accessible.

It does not specify which state is selected.

This suggests separating:

\[
\text{accessibility relation}
\]

from

\[
\text{selection rule}.
\]

The Striker's role, if it is to be mathematically meaningful, should be specified through the latter.

A group action alone does not produce a judgment.

### 16.3 A possible architecture for contextual evaluation

The expanded orbit gives the protocol a larger state domain.

But the global valuation argument from F163 still applies to deterministic observables on a single common state.

Therefore the next step cannot simply be to announce contextuality.

A genuine contextual architecture would require a family of evaluation contexts \(C\) and functions

\[
J(x,C).
\]

If

\[
J(x,C_1)\ne J(x,C_2),
\]

then a single context-independent function of \(x\) cannot represent all judgments.

That is a formal route to context dependence.

The remaining question is how the contexts are selected, restricted, or made jointly incompatible.

### 16.4 The ontological question remains open

The theorem establishes a precise mathematical structure.

It does not establish why this structure exists, why any particular state is actual, or why the Ω42 family is ontologically fundamental.

The mathematical programme has improved its account of accessibility and invariant relaxation.

It has not yet derived existence itself.

## 18. Remaining research problems

The core finite-level and closure structure is now proved. The next research stage should move beyond repeating the orbit calculation.

### Problem 1 — Compare different shear depths

Determine the exact group and closure generated by

\[
A,\qquad
B_r=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\\
3^r&1
\end{pmatrix}
\]

for all \(r\geq1\).

### Problem 2 — Determine the minimum generator requirements
