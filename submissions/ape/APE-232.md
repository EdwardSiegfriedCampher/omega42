# The Ω42 Arithmetic Tower: Quadratic Norms, 3-Adic Dynamics, and Cycle Refinement

## Abstract

This paper develops three mathematical avenues for extending the Ω42 transition system: (I) its quadratic norm and associated algebraic structure, (II) its inverse-limit dynamics over the 3-adic integers, and (III) the refinement of finite cycles across the depth tower. The objective is to determine which properties follow exactly from the transition rule, how the observed finite orbit census can be proved uniformly, and what additional mathematical structure becomes accessible once the finite systems are treated as a compatible hierarchy.

The principal result is a unified classification. The transition is multiplication by the golden-ratio unit in an unramified quadratic extension of the 3-adic integers. This identifies the quadratic form as an algebraic norm, explains its polarity under the transition, and yields an exact valuation law. The same representation proves the order of the transition modulo every power of 3 and gives the complete finite orbit census. In the inverse limit, the transition is an isometry preserving Haar measure and having zero topological entropy, while its only periodic point is the zero state. Thus finite periodicity and infinite-depth periodicity are sharply distinguished.

The paper concludes with research directions in symmetry classification, quadratic character sums, spectral analysis, and generalizations to other primes and recurrence systems. The expected rewards include a complete arithmetic classification, compact invariants for the tower, and a framework for determining which features of Ω42 are special to its 3-primary construction.

**Keywords:** Ω42; quadratic forms; Fibonacci matrix; golden ratio; 3-adic integers; arithmetic dynamics; inverse limits; orbit classification; cycle refinement.

## 1. Introduction

Ω42 is defined by the finite state spaces

\[
S_k=(\mathbb Z/9^k\mathbb Z)^2
\]

and the transition

\[
T(a,b)=(b,a+b)\pmod{9^k}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
T(s)=As,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The determinant is \(-1\), so the transition is invertible at every depth. Each finite state therefore belongs to a periodic orbit.

The quadratic form

\[
Q(a,b)=a^2+ab-b^2
\]

satisfies

\[
Q(Ts)=-Q(s),\qquad Q(T^2s)=Q(s).
\]

The observed order of the transition is

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{9^k}(A)=24\cdot9^{k-1}.
\]

The corresponding finite orbit counts exhibit a regular multiplicity pattern. These observations motivate three connected questions:

1. Can the transition and quadratic form be explained by a common algebraic structure?
2. What mathematical object is obtained when all finite depths are combined?
3. Can the finite orbit census be proved uniformly and understood as a rule of cycle refinement?

The paper answers these questions by using the quadratic algebra associated with the characteristic polynomial

\[
f(x)=x^2-x-1.
\]

The three avenues are not independent. The algebraic norm identifies the invariant structure; the 3-adic completion unifies the depths; and the norm and valuation structure make the cycle classification possible.

### 1.1 Scope and standing conventions

## 2. The quadratic algebra underlying the transition

### 2.1 The algebraic representation

Let

\[
R_n=(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)[\phi],
\qquad \phi^2=\phi+1.
\]

Every element of \(R_n\) has a unique representation

\[
z=a+b\phi.
\]

Multiplication by \(\phi\) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\phi(a+b\phi)
&=a\phi+b\phi^2\\
&=a\phi+b(\phi+1)\\
&=b+(a+b)\phi.
\end{aligned}
\]

Under the coordinate identification \(a+b\phi\leftrightarrow(a,b)\), this is exactly

\[
(a,b)\mapsto(b,a+b).
\]

Thus the Ω42 transition is multiplication by \(\phi\) in the quadratic algebra:

\[
\boxed{T(z)=\phi z.}
\]

This is an exact representation of the transition, not an approximation or analogy.

### 2.2 Why the prime 3 is structurally useful

The discriminant of \(f(x)=x^2-x-1\) is \(5\). Modulo 3, the polynomial has no root:

\[
f(0)=2,\qquad f(1)=2,\qquad f(2)=1
\pmod3.
\]

Therefore \(f\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\).

Consequently,

\[
\mathbb Z_3[\phi]
\]

is the ring of integers of the unramified quadratic extension of \(\mathbb Q_3\). Its residue field is \(\mathbb F_9\).

This gives Ω42 a natural arithmetic setting: the two coordinates are a basis for a quadratic 3-adic algebra, and the transition acts by multiplication by a distinguished unit.

### 2.3 The norm identity

The conjugate of \(\phi\) is

\[
\phi'=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2},
\]

which satisfies

\[
\phi+\phi'=1,\qquad \phi\phi'=-1.
\]

The algebraic norm is

\[
\begin{aligned}
N(a+b\phi)
&=(a+b\phi)(a+b\phi')\\
&=a^2+ab(\phi+\phi')+b^2\phi\phi'\\
&=a^2+ab-b^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{N(a+b\phi)=Q(a,b).}
\]

Since the norm is multiplicative,

\[
N(\phi z)=N(\phi)N(z)=-N(z).
\]

This proves

\[
\boxed{Q(Ts)=-Q(s).}
\]

Applying the transition twice gives

\[
Q(T^2s)=Q(s).
\]

The polarity of the quadratic form is therefore the norm law associated with multiplication by a unit of norm \(-1\).

**Reward of this avenue:** the transition and its quadratic form become two aspects of one algebraic construction. This supplies a basis for studying units, valuations, norm classes, and arithmetic symmetries.

## 3. The 3-adic valuation of the quadratic form

The norm representation yields a stronger result than sign reversal.

Let \(a,b\in\mathbb Z_3\), not both zero, and define

\[
r=\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b)\}.
\]

Write

\[
a=3^r a_0,\qquad b=3^r b_0,
\]

where at least one of \(a_0,b_0\) is a 3-adic unit. By homogeneity,

\[
Q(a,b)=3^{2r}Q(a_0,b_0).
\]

We now show that \(Q(a_0,b_0)\) is a unit.

If \(Q(a_0,b_0)\equiv0\pmod3\), then

\[
a_0^2+a_0b_0-b_0^2\equiv0\pmod3.
\]

If \(b_0\not\equiv0\pmod3\), division by \(b_0^2\) would produce a root of \(x^2+x-1\) modulo 3. This polynomial has no root modulo 3. If \(b_0\equiv0\pmod3\), the equation forces \(a_0\equiv0\pmod3\), contradicting primitivity.

Therefore \(Q(a_0,b_0)\) is not divisible by 3.

### Theorem 1. Norm valuation

For every nonzero \((a,b)\in\mathbb Z_3^2\),

\[
\boxed{v_3(Q(a,b))=2\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b)\}.}
\]

The zero state is exceptional because \(Q(0,0)=0\), whose valuation is conventionally \(+\infty\).

### Consequences

1. The norm valuation is always even.
2. The norm determines the common divisibility depth of the two coordinates.
3. Multiplication by \(\phi\) preserves this depth because it changes \(Q\) only by sign.
4. The state space decomposes into invariant divisibility shells.

For example, the shell of exact common divisibility \(3^r\) consists of states divisible by \(3^r\) but not both divisible by \(3^{r+1}\).

This result provides an exact relationship between a quadratic invariant and the 3-adic filtration of the state space.

## 4. The order of the transition modulo powers of 3

The finite orbit classification depends on the order of \(A\) modulo \(3^n\).

The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is \(x^2-x-1\). Modulo 3, the associated unit \(\phi\) lies in \(\mathbb F_9^\times\), a group of order 8. Direct calculation shows that \(\phi\) has order 8. Therefore

\[
\operatorname{ord}_3(A)=8.
\]

To lift this order to higher powers of 3, compute

\[
A^8=
\begin{pmatrix}
13&21\\
21&34
\end{pmatrix}
=I+3B,
\]

where

\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}4&7\\7&11\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Modulo 3,

\[
B\equiv
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}
\pmod3,
\]

and its determinant is \(1\) modulo 3. Thus \(B\) is invertible modulo 3.

### Lemma 1. Order lifting

For every integer \(r\ge0\), there exists a matrix \(B_r\), invertible modulo 3, such that

\[
A^{8\cdot3^r}=I+3^{r+1}B_r.
\]

#### Proof sketch

The case \(r=0\) is the displayed identity for \(A^8\).

Suppose

\[
A^{8\cdot3^r}=I+3^{r+1}B_r,
\qquad B_r\not\equiv0\pmod3
\]

with \(B_r\) invertible modulo 3. Cubing yields

\[
(I+3^{r+1}B_r)^3
=I+3^{r+2}B_r+3^{2r+3}B_r^2+3^{3r+3}B_r^3.
\]

The first nonidentity term has exact 3-adic valuation \(r+2\); the remaining terms have strictly greater valuation. Hence the new leading coefficient is congruent to \(B_r\) modulo 3 and remains invertible. Induction proves the lemma.

### Theorem 2. Exact matrix order

For every \(n\ge1\),

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_{3^n}(A)=8\cdot3^{n-1}.}
\]

#### Proof

The lemma shows

\[
A^{8\cdot3^{n-1}}\equiv I\pmod{3^n}.
\]

It also shows that

\[
A^{8\cdot3^{n-2}}\not\equiv I\pmod{3^n}
\]

for \(n\ge2\). Reduction modulo 3 requires the order to be divisible by 8, while each successive power of 3 in the modulus requires one additional factor of 3 in the order. Thus the order is exactly \(8\cdot3^{n-1}\).

For the Ω42 levels, \(9^k=3^{2k}\), so

\[
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_{9^k}(A)=24\cdot9^{k-1}.}
\]

The order-lifting theorem also yields the half-period relation

\[
\boxed{A^{4\cdot3^{n-1}}\equiv-I\pmod{3^n}.}
\]

At \(n=2k\), this becomes

\[
A^{12\cdot9^{k-1}}\equiv-I\pmod{9^k}.
\]

## 5. Complete finite orbit classification

We now use the quadratic algebra to classify every orbit at every finite level.

Let \(z=a+b\phi\in R_n\). Define its common divisibility depth \(r\) by

\[
r=\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b),n\}.
\]

If \(r=n\), the state is zero modulo \(3^n\). Otherwise, write

\[
z=3^r u,
\]

where \(u\) is a unit modulo \(3^{n-r}\).

The period of \(z\) is the least positive integer \(m\) such that

\[
\phi^m z\equiv z\pmod{3^n}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
3^r(\phi^m-1)u\equiv0\pmod{3^n}.
\]

Since \(u\) is a unit, this is equivalent to

\[
\phi^m\equiv1\pmod{3^{n-r}}.
\]

The period is therefore the order of \(\phi\) modulo \(3^{n-r}\):

\[
\operatorname{period}(z)=8\cdot3^{n-r-1}.
\]

This gives a complete classification by common divisibility depth.

### Theorem 3. Orbit census modulo \(3^n\)

For every \(n\ge1\), the action of \(A\) on \((\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)^2\) has:

- one fixed point, namely the zero state;
- one cycle of length \(8\);
- for each \(m=2,\ldots,n\), exactly \(3^{m-1}\) cycles of length \(8\cdot3^{m-1}\).

#### Proof

At effective modulus \(3^m\), the number of primitive pairs—pairs not both divisible by 3—is

\[
3^{2m}-3^{2m-2}
=8\cdot9^{m-1}.
\]

Every such pair has period \(8\cdot3^{m-1}\). Hence the number of cycles at this effective modulus is

\[
\frac{8\cdot9^{m-1}}{8\cdot3^{m-1}}
=3^{m-1}.
\]

The zero state contributes the single fixed point. At \(m=1\), the primitive states form one cycle of length 8. Summing the contributions yields

\[
1+8+\sum_{m=2}^{n}
3^{m-1}(8\cdot3^{m-1})
=3^{2n}.
\]

All states are accounted for exactly once.

### Corollary 1. Ω42 census

At depth \(k\), where \(n=2k\), the cycles are:

\[
1^1,\quad8^1,\quad
\{(8\cdot3^{m-1})^{\,3^{m-1}}:2\le m\le2k\}.
\]

Here \(L^c\) means \(c\) cycles of length \(L\).

The first levels are therefore:

- \(k=1\): \(1^1,8^1,24^3\).
- \(k=2\): the preceding cycles, together with \(72^9,216^{27}\).
- \(k=3\): the preceding cycles, together with \(648^{81},1944^{243}\).
- \(k=4\): the preceding cycles, together with \(5832^{729},17496^{2187}\).

The census is not merely a numerical pattern. It follows from the algebraic unit structure and the exact order-lifting theorem.

**Reward of this avenue:** an exhaustive finite computation becomes a uniform theorem, valid at every depth.

## 6. Avenue II: the inverse-limit dynamical system

### 6.1 Construction

The reductions

\[
(\mathbb Z/3^{n+1}\mathbb Z)^2
\longrightarrow
(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)^2
\]

commute with the transition. The inverse limit is

\[
X=\varprojlim_n(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)^2
\simeq\mathbb Z_3^2.
\]

The transition extends continuously to

\[
T:X\to X,\qquad T(x)=Ax.
\]

Since \(\det A=-1\) is a 3-adic unit, \(T\) is a homeomorphism.

This limiting system is not one very large finite state space. It consists of compatible residues at every finite precision.

### 6.2 The 3-adic metric and isometry

For \(x,y\in\mathbb Z_3^2\), use the maximum 3-adic norm

\[
\|x-y\|_3
=\max\{|x_1-y_1|_3,|x_2-y_2|_3\}.
\]

The matrix \(A\) and its inverse both have entries in \(\mathbb Z_3\). Consequently,

\[
\|A(x-y)\|_3\le\|x-y\|_3
\]

and, applying the same inequality to \(A^{-1}\),

\[
\|x-y\|_3\le\|A(x-y)\|_3.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{\|Tx-Ty\|_3=\|x-y\|_3.}
\]

The transition is an isometry. It preserves 3-adic distances and therefore preserves the nested congruence structure.

### 6.3 Periodic points in the inverse limit

At every finite level, all states are periodic. The infinite system behaves differently.

Suppose \(x\in\mathbb Z_3^2\) is periodic. In the quadratic algebra, this means

\[
\phi^m z=z
\]

for some positive integer \(m\), where \(z\) is the element corresponding to \(x\). Therefore

\[
(\phi^m-1)z=0.
\]

The ring \(\mathbb Z_3[\phi]\) is an integral domain, and \(\phi\) is not a root of unity. Hence \(\phi^m-1\ne0\), so \(z=0\).

### Theorem 4. Periodic points in the inverse limit

The only periodic point of \(T\) on \(\mathbb Z_3^2\) is

\[
\boxed{x=0.}
\]

This is a significant distinction between finite depth and infinite depth. Each finite reduction returns to its starting state after some finite number of steps, but a nonzero compatible 3-adic state does not return exactly after any finite number of steps.

Finite periodicity is therefore compatible with infinite-depth nonperiodicity.

### 6.4 Topological entropy

The isometry property also determines the topological entropy.

For an isometry, the distance between two points remains unchanged under every iterate. Thus a set separated at a given scale for a time interval of length \(N\) is exactly as separated as it was at time zero. The maximum size of such a separated set does not grow exponentially with \(N\).

It follows that

\[
\boxed{h_{\mathrm{top}}(T)=0.}
\]

This does not mean that every orbit is periodic or that every trajectory is trivial. It means that the system has no exponential growth of distinguishable orbit segments in the topological-entropy sense.

### 6.5 Invariant measure

The compact additive group \(\mathbb Z_3^2\) has a normalized Haar measure \(\mu\). Since \(T\) is a continuous group automorphism, it preserves this measure:

\[
\boxed{\mu(T^{-1}E)=\mu(E)}
\]

for every measurable set \(E\).

This gives a natural invariant measure without requiring an arbitrary choice of probability distribution.

The finite uniform measures are consistent with this construction: every residue class at a fixed level has equal Haar measure, and its measure agrees with the proportion of finite states represented by that class.

### 6.6 Orbit closures and equicontinuity

The isometry property implies equicontinuity of the iterates. For every \(j\),

\[
d(T^j x,T^j y)=d(x,y).
\]

The closure of an orbit is compact and invariant. Determining the exact orbit closure of a general nonzero 3-adic point is a further classification problem, but the system is already constrained by its group structure and equicontinuity.

The finite orbit census supplies periodic approximations to these limiting trajectories. It does not imply that the limiting trajectory itself is periodic.

**Reward of this avenue:** the finite Ω42 tower becomes a single topological dynamical system with a natural metric, invariant measure, zero entropy, and a precise account of the difference between finite return and infinite return.

## 7. Avenue III: cycle refinement across the tower

### 7.1 Projection between depths

For each \(n\), let

\[
X_n=(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)^2.
\]

Reduction defines a map

\[
\pi_n:X_{n+1}\to X_n.
\]

Since the transition is given by an integer matrix,

\[
\pi_n\circ T_{n+1}=T_n\circ\pi_n.
\]

Therefore every cycle at depth \(n+1\) projects onto a cycle at depth \(n\), although the projected trajectory may traverse its parent cycle more than once.

This gives a natural hierarchy of cycles, with the orbit structure at each level refining the preceding level.

### 7.2 Exact parent–child relation

Let a state at level \(n+1\) have common divisibility depth \(r\), where \(0\le r\le n\). Its period is

\[
8\cdot3^{n-r}.
\]

After reduction to level \(n\), two cases occur:

1. If the reduction has the same common divisibility depth, its period decreases by a factor of 3.
2. If the state lies in the zero residue class modulo \(3^n\), its projection is the fixed point.

More generally, a cycle with period \(L\) at the fine level maps onto a cycle whose period divides \(L\) at the coarse level. The number of fine steps required to close the orbit can exceed the number of steps needed to close the projected orbit.

### 7.3 A valuation-based description of the lift structure

The norm valuation gives a concise way to identify the depth shell of a state:

\[
v_3(Q(a,b))=2r.
\]

At modulus \(3^n\), a state with common divisibility depth \(r<n\) belongs to the effective modulus \(3^{n-r}\). Its period is exactly the order of \(\phi\) at that effective modulus:

\[
L_r=8\cdot3^{n-r-1}.
\]

Thus the orbit length is determined by the state’s divisibility depth.

This yields a cycle-refinement principle:

> **A state’s finite period is determined by the effective 3-adic precision remaining after its common divisibility is factored out.**

The principle is a theorem in the present linear system, not a universal statement about all modular dynamical systems.

### 7.4 The lift tree

One can encode the entire tower as a directed graph whose nodes are cycles and whose edges describe projection from fine-level cycles to coarse-level cycles.

A node may record:

- its modulus;
- its period;
- its number of states;
- the valuation shell containing its states;
- its parent cycle under projection;
- the number and type of its descendants.

The exact orbit classification gives the number of cycles at every depth. The lift tree would add relational information: which particular cycles descend from which earlier cycles.

The global cycle counts alone do not specify every edge of this tree. A complete lift-tree classification therefore remains a useful computational and theoretical task.

### 7.5 What the lift tree could reveal

A full classification would answer:

1. How many distinct fine cycles lie above each coarse cycle?
2. Is the number of descendants determined solely by the parent period and valuation shell?
3. Which cycle features persist unchanged under lifting?
4. Can the tree be generated by a finite recursive rule?
5. Can the limiting orbit closure of a 3-adic point be recovered from its sequence of parent cycles?

These questions connect finite arithmetic to infinite dynamics. In particular, the compatible sequence of residue classes of a 3-adic point determines a nested sequence of finite orbit locations.

**Reward of this avenue:** the tower becomes a structured hierarchy of trajectories rather than a list of independent finite systems.

## 8. How the three avenues reinforce one another

The three developments form a logical chain.

### 8.1 Algebra determines periods

The quadratic algebra identifies the transition with multiplication by \(\phi\). The order of \(\phi\) modulo powers of 3 determines the periods of unit states.

### 8.2 Norm valuation determines the effective modulus

The norm valuation identifies the common divisibility depth of a state. Factoring out this divisibility reduces the period calculation to a smaller modulus.

### 8.3 Periods determine the census

Counting primitive pairs at each effective modulus gives the complete orbit multiplicities.

### 8.4 The tower determines the limit

Compatible projections combine all finite levels into \(\mathbb Z_3^2\). The same algebraic unit acts continuously on the limiting space.

### 8.5 The limit explains the finite–infinite distinction

Finite residues are periodic because each state belongs to a finite permutation. A nonzero 3-adic state is not periodic because multiplication by \(\phi\) is not a root-of-unity action in the characteristic-zero quadratic algebra.

This chain is the central mathematical result of the paper: the norm, period, census, and inverse-limit behavior are connected consequences of one algebraic representation.

## 9. Further research enabled by the three avenues

The principal structure is now understood well enough to support more specialized questions.

### 9.1 Symmetry groups

Let \(C_n(A)\) be the centralizer of \(A\) in \(\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)\):

\[
C_n(A)=\{B:BA=AB\}.
\]

Under the quadratic-algebra representation, matrices commuting with \(A\) correspond to multiplication maps by elements of \(R_n\). The invertible members correspond to units of \(R_n\). Thus the centralizer has a direct algebraic description.

The orthogonal group of \(Q\) is a different object:

\[
O(Q,R_n)=\{B\in\mathrm{GL}(2,R_n):Q(Bx)=Q(x)\}.
\]

One can also study transformations satisfying \(Q(Bx)=-Q(x)\). The full classifications of these groups, and their intersections with the centralizer or normalizer of the transition, can reveal how the norm and the dynamics constrain one another.

**Expected reward:** a group-theoretic account of orbit equivalence and a sharper understanding of which symmetries preserve, reverse, or reorganize trajectories.

### 9.2 Quadratic character sums

For \(t\in\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z\), define

\[
G_n(t)=
\sum_{(a,b)\in X_n}
\exp\!\left(
\frac{2\pi i\,tQ(a,b)}{3^n}
\right).
\]

Since \(T\) permutes \(X_n\) and \(Q(Tx)=-Q(x)\),

\[
\boxed{G_n(t)=G_n(-t).}
\]

This identity is immediate. The complete evaluation of \(G_n(t)\) is a separate problem.

The sums may admit recursive formulas in \(n\), depending on the 3-adic valuation of \(t\). Evaluating them would compress the distribution of the quadratic form into exact arithmetic data.

**Expected reward:** explicit formulas for norm distributions and a compact family of invariants that can be compared across depths.

### 9.3 Spectral decomposition

Let \(U_n\) act on functions \(f:X_n\to\mathbb C\) by

\[
(U_nf)(x)=f(Tx).
\]

Every cycle of length \(L\) contributes the \(L\)-th roots of unity to the spectrum of \(U_n\). Thus the orbit census determines the spectrum as a multiset.

A more substantial result would identify a useful arithmetic basis in which the action becomes transparent, and then relate that basis to additive characters and the quadratic form.

**Expected reward:** an independent representation of the dynamics, a spectral verification of the orbit census, and a route to studying depth-compatible modes.

### 9.4 Other primes

The same matrix can be reduced modulo \(p^n\) for primes \(p\ne3\). The behavior depends on the characteristic polynomial modulo \(p\), especially on whether it splits or remains irreducible.

At primes where \(x^2-x-1\) is irreducible, the quadratic algebra has an unramified local structure analogous to the 3-adic case. At primes where it splits, the algebraic representation has a different form. The prime 5 requires separate treatment because it divides the discriminant.

**Expected reward:** a classification of which features are specific to the 3-primary tower and which belong to a wider family of arithmetic recurrence systems.
