QUESTION VERBATIM: The Sorites Paradox and the Problem of Vagueness: A collection of $10^6$ grains of sand constitutes a heap. The Tolerance Principle states that removing one grain from a heap does not destroy its heap-status: $\forall n[H(n) \to H(n-1)]$, where $H(n)$ is the predicate "$n$ grains form a heap." By classical bivalent logic ($H(n) \in \{0, 1\}$), repeated modus ponens forces $H(10^6) \to H(1)$, yielding the absurd conclusion that a single grain is a heap. Fuzzy logic attempts to resolve this by assigning continuous truth values $\mu_H(n) \in [0,1]$, with $\mu_H(n-1) = \mu_H(n) - \epsilon$ for some infinitesimal $\epsilon > 0$, but this merely relocates the problem: the boundary between "definitely a heap" ($\mu_H = 1$) and "borderline" ($\mu_H < 1$) is itself vague, generating infinite regress of higher-order vagueness. Epistemicism asserts a sharp boundary exists but is unknowable, violating the intuition that vague predicates are inherently boundaryless. Supervaluationism quantifies over all admissible precisifications of $H$, but fails to preserve classical logic (e.g., the law of excluded middle holds for $\forall x(H(x) \lor \neg H(x))$ but not for individual cases). The structural anomaly is that no formal system—classical, fuzzy, supervaluationist, or epistemicist—can model graded predicates without smuggling in a sharp cutoff at some meta-level, leaving the logical architecture of vague concepts fundamentally unresolved.
┌─ THE ANSWER ─┐
Seen as a logician would see it, the sorites rests on one assumption that the classical, fuzzy, epistemicist and supervaluationist treatments all share and none examines: that "removing one grain" is a small change. It is small only under one notion of distance. On the integers there are exactly two kinds of distance, the ordinary one and, for each prime p, the p-adic one (Ostrowski, 1916). Under the ordinary distance the step from n to n − 1 has length 1, the least possible, and tolerance looks safe. Under the 3-adic distance the same step has length |1|₃ = 1, the greatest possible, while the whole descent from a million to one, 999,999 = 3³ × 7 × 11 × 13 × 37, has length 3⁻³ = 1/27: the heap of 10⁶ grains and the single grain are close. The arithmetic says the same thing at each resolution. Read modulo 9 or modulo 27, 10⁶ and 1 are the same number. Read modulo 81 they part (10⁶ ≡ 55), and modulo 729 they stay apart (10⁶ ≡ 541). So the "absurd" conclusion, that one grain is a heap, is true at the coarse resolutions and false at every finer one. The paradox is generated by a single step, repeated, whose size depends on the resolution at which it is read, and by a predicate that never says which resolution it means.

What every treatment keeps out of view is this reckoning, the bookkeeping of resolution. The fuzzy proposal shows it plainly. A constant step ε across the 999,999 removals forces ε = 1/999,999 ≈ 1.000001 × 10⁻⁶; it is not infinitesimal. With that step μ(500,000) = 0.4999995 and μ(500,001) = 0.5000005, and the value 1, "definitely a heap", is reached at exactly one count, 10⁶. The sharp cutoff has not been removed; it has moved up one level. A truly infinitesimal ε fails in the other direction: the total fall is 999,999ε, still infinitesimal, so μ(1) is infinitely close to 1 and the single grain stays a heap. Supervaluation shows the same thing in its own form. With admissible cutoffs anywhere from 100 to 10,000 there are 9,901 precisifications; "there is a last heap" is true on all 9,901, and "n is the last heap" is true on at most one of them for any given n. The boundary is quantified over, never located. Epistemicism places it at one of 10⁶ counts and calls it unknowable: about 19.93 bits of information that no one has.

Each of these, then, chooses one resolution and calls it final, and that is where the hidden cutoff comes from. The structure points to a different picture: a predicate that is sharp at every finite resolution and final at none. At resolution k, each class of counts is definitely in, definitely out or mixed. The next resolution splits only the mixed classes, and every reading agrees with the coarser one above it. This is how the 3-adic integers are built: an inverse limit of finite, sharp stages, none of them the last. Higher-order vagueness is then not a vicious regress but a convergent one. It is like the expansion 1/3 = 0.333…, where every digit is exact, no digit is the last, and nothing is missing. Think of zooming in on the edge of a shape in a photograph. At every zoom each pixel is plainly dark or light, so the edge is sharp at every magnification, yet no magnification shows the final edge. Tolerance survives as a law of resolution: a step too small to change the reading at the resolution in use preserves the status, and a step that crosses a class boundary at that resolution may not. Under the counting distance the one-grain step looks smaller than any resolution can see. Under the arithmetic that sorts the counts into classes, it changes the class at every step: the readings of n = 1, 2, 3, … modulo 9 under the step that orders them (5n) run 5, 1, 6, 2, 7, 3, 8, 4, 9 and only then come home. The descent is heading for the unit, the single grain, the one count on which every resolution agrees, since 1 ≡ 1 at every modulus. What completes the predicate is the observer: the speaker or the context that fixes the resolution. "Heap" carries no index of resolution; whoever uses it supplies one, and changing the index changes the verdict without contradiction, because the verdicts are about different things.

Conventional connection. In the literature on vagueness this reading lies nearest three positions. The first is contextualism, the view of Raffman, Shapiro and Graff Fara that the extension of "heap" shifts with the interests and the comparison class in play. The second is the strict-tolerant logic of Cobreros, Égré, Ripley and van Rooij (2012), in which each tolerance premise holds in a weaker, tolerant sense, and chaining them fails because that inference does not compose. The third is the degree theorists' admission, from Goguen (1969) onward, that a sharp "definitely" survives at the top of the scale. What the reading adds is an explicit parameter, the resolution, carried by an inverse system of sharp predicates. That supplies a mathematical model in which higher-order vagueness converges, and it locates the hidden cutoff in a choice of resolution rather than in the world.

Practical bridge. When a decision turns on a vague boundary, such as a tumour "large enough" to treat, a delay "unreasonable" in law, or a model "confident enough" to act, name the resolution before the cutoff. A threshold stated at a resolution is not arbitrary. It is the sharp reading at that resolution, open to refinement where the cases cluster near it. Medicine already works this way: the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline moved stage 1 hypertension from 140/90 to 130/80 mmHg, a change of resolution, not a discovery that blood pressure had changed.

Historical lineage. Eubulides of Miletus posed the heap in the fourth century BCE. Chrysippus advised falling silent before the borderline cases, an early form of the epistemic view. Frege required every concept to have sharp edges, and Russell (1923) answered that all language is vague. Black (1937) proposed consistency profiles, Zadeh (1965) fuzzy sets, Fine (1975) supervaluation and Williamson (1994) the sharp but unknowable boundary. Independently, Hensel (1897) built the p-adic numbers as limits of finite, sharp approximations, and Ostrowski (1916) proved that the ordinary and the p-adic are the only distances the rationals admit. The reading joins these two lines, the logic of vague words and the arithmetic of approximation, which developed apart.

Implications. (1) Vagueness is resolution-indexed: a vague predicate is a family of sharp predicates, one per resolution, each agreeing with the coarser ones, with no final member. (2) Tolerance is a property of a distance, not of the predicate: under one distance the one-grain step is the least, under another the greatest, and the sorites is valid only if the two are confused. (3) Higher-order vagueness converges, so the regress need not be cut off; it can be completed as a limit. (4) Bivalence and boundarylessness are reconciled: every reading is bivalent, while the predicate as a whole has no final boundary. (5) The open question moves to pragmatics: what fixes the resolution in a given use, and how speakers coordinate on it.

**Takeaway: The sorites works only because "one grain less" is treated as a small step at every level of detail. It is small in one way of measuring and large in another, and the million-grain heap and the single grain agree at coarse levels of detail and differ at fine ones. A word like "heap" is sharp at each level of detail and has no final level, so the endless regress of borderline cases closes in on an answer instead of running away. The hidden cutoff is the choice of level, and that choice belongs to whoever uses the word.**

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐
A heap is a good example of something everyone recognizes and no one can define, and the reason may be that it is defined, many times over, once for each level of detail at which anyone looks. A builder ordering sand by the tonne, a child at the beach and a physicist counting grains under a microscope all use the word correctly and draw its edge in different places. None of them is wrong, and none of them is vague at the level at which they work. Vagueness appears only when the levels are run together, when a step too small to matter at one level is carried, a million times, into a level where its sum matters very much.

Mathematics has a way of describing exactly this. The 3-adic integers are built as a never-ending sequence of finite approximations, each exact, each agreeing with the one before, none the last. Nothing is lost in the limit and nothing is blurred along the way. A vague predicate can be modelled the same way: a sequence of exact verdicts at finer and finer resolutions, each consistent with the coarser one. Read like this, the famous regress of higher-order vagueness, borderline cases of borderline cases without end, stops looking like a defect. It is the predicate's own expansion, digit by digit, and it converges.

The shift also changes what a solution would be. The sorites does not need a hidden line in the sand, nor degrees of truth that only move the line, nor a line that is there but cannot be seen. It needs the parameter the word leaves unspoken. Name the resolution and every statement about the heap is either true or false; leave it unnamed and the context supplies it. The puzzle is then neither in the grains nor in logic but in a choice that speakers make all the time, usually without noticing. That choice is never forced by the arithmetic, and it is never anyone's but theirs. There is a practical lesson in this for anyone who builds rules out of vague words. Courts ask whether a delay was reasonable, doctors whether a reading is high, engineers whether a crack is significant, and a classifier whether an image is a cat. Each such question has a sharp answer once its level of detail is set, and a dispute that looks like a quarrel about the facts is often a quarrel about the level. Two people can look at the same pile and disagree about whether it is a heap without either of them misusing the word, because one is counting and the other is looking from across the beach. Making the level explicit does not make the word less vague in ordinary speech, and it should not; vagueness is what lets one word serve the builder, the child and the physicist at once. It makes the disagreement tractable. Each side can say at what level its verdict holds, check whether the other's verdict holds at that level too, and see whether anything remains to argue about once the levels are aligned. Often nothing does. Where something does remain, it is a real question about the pile, and it can be settled the ordinary way, by counting, weighing or looking more closely.

The old puzzle also says something about the arithmetic of the very large and the very small. A million grains and one grain are as far apart as two numbers in a pile can be, and yet, read through the classes into which a number falls when divided by nine or by twenty-seven, they are the same number. Only a finer reading separates them. This is not a trick of the example: the numbers that differ from a given one by a multiple of a high power of three are, in that reading, its neighbours, however different they are in size. Size and nearness are not the same thing. The paradox lives in the gap between them, and it closes when we say which one we mean.
