Hydrogen Embrittlement in High-Strength Steels: The phenomenon where atomic hydrogen ($H$) diffuses into the crystalline lattice of high-strength steels ($\sigma_y > 700 \text{ MPa}$), causing a catastrophic loss of ductility and delayed brittle fracture at stresses far below the material's yield strength. Despite over a century of study, no unified mechanistic model predicts the time-to-failure $t_f$ from first principles. The dominant competing theories—Hydrogen Enhanced Localized Plasticity (HELP) and Hydrogen Enhanced DecoHEsion (HEDE)—cannot be reconciled: HELP posits that hydrogen increases dislocation mobility and localized shear, while HEDE posits that hydrogen lowers the atomic cohesive energy at grain boundaries. The structural anomaly is that hydrogen atoms ($r_H \approx 0.37 \text{ \AA}$) are small enough to occupy interstitial sites in the body-centered cubic (BCC) iron lattice, yet their concentration at crack tips can reach $10^3$ to $10^4$ times the bulk concentration due to hydrostatic stress gradients, $\nabla \sigma_h$. The threshold stress intensity factor for hydrogen-assisted cracking, $K_{IH}$, can drop to $\approx 10 \text{ to } 20 \text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$ (compared to a fracture toughness $K_{IC} \approx 50 \text{ to } 100 \text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$ in hydrogen-free conditions), and the exact coupling between hydrogen diffusion ($D_H \approx 10^{-8} \text{ to } 10^{-9} \text{ cm}^2/\text{s}$), trapping at microstructural defects, and the quantum-mechanical reduction of the Fe-Fe bond cohesive energy remains unresolved. This prevents the safe deployment of high-strength steels in hydrogen energy infrastructure, pipelines, and aerospace components.
