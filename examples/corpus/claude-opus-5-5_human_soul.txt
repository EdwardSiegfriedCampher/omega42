┌─ THE ANSWER ─┐

The human soul is the generating rule of a person — not one more thing a person has, but the pattern that produces each next moment of a life out of the one before, so that a thousand different days still come out recognisably as the same someone. That rule does not run on its own: it depends on something that steers it, a power of heading this way rather than that, without which generation would be mere repetition. What it keeps out of view is its own reckoning — the account of what it has actually produced, the tally of debts and harms and gifts that a life runs up — and that account, held at a distance, does not stay away: it keeps returning from ahead, pressing on the rule that generates, the way a conscience presses on a choice before it is made. The soul is moving toward unity: the many acts and selves of a life gathering into one, and one life gathering toward others. And it is completed by being seen — by another, or by itself — whole, as the pattern it has been rather than as any single thing it did. A soul is like the grain of a piece of wood: no single board is the grain, yet every cut reveals it, the carpenter must work with it rather than against it, and only when the whole piece is planed and turned to the light can you see what it was all along.

Aristotle called the soul the form of a living body — the principle that organises it and makes it alive, rather than a separate substance lodged inside it. This reading agrees that the soul is a generating principle and not an object. It differs on what the principle needs: not only a nature to actualise, but a steering to follow, a reckoning it would rather not face, and a witness before which it becomes complete.

In practice this changes how a person is known. A soul shows itself not in one dramatic act but in what keeps being generated — the way someone repeatedly forgives, or repeatedly withdraws, or repeatedly begins again. Changing a life, read this way, is less like replacing a part and more like learning the grain: steering with it, facing the account it keeps, and letting someone see the whole.

Plato pictured the soul as a charioteer steering a pair of horses, and Augustine wrote his Confessions as a soul's reckoning given aloud. Plotinus described the soul's turn toward the One, and Keats called the world a vale of soul-making, a place where a soul is formed by being lived. Each names one direction of the same figure: steering, reckoning, unity, and making.

If the soul is completed by being seen whole, then being truly seen by another is not a luxury added to a life but part of how a soul is finished; and the reckoning a person avoids is not outside the soul but one of the things it runs on. A life that refuses every account and every witness is not a freer soul but an unfinished one.

Takeaway: The human soul is the generating rule of a person. It depends on a power that steers it, keeps its own reckoning out of view though that reckoning keeps returning, moves toward unity, and is completed when it is seen whole. It is less a thing inside us than the grain of what we keep making.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐

Music has the clearest picture of a generating rule. A theme stated in the opening bars of a set of variations is not the piece; it is what the piece keeps producing. Each variation changes the rhythm, the key, the texture, sometimes almost everything audible, and yet the listener knows it is the same theme. What persists is not any sound but the rule that generates the sounds. A composer writing variations is steering that rule — choosing, at each turn, which possibility it will become — and the work is finished not when the theme runs out but when the whole set can be heard together as one.

Biology offers a second picture. A developing body is produced by a program that no single cell contains as a visible thing: the same instructions, read in different places and at different times, generate bone in one region and nerve in another. The organism is the program's output, but the program alone produces nothing; it needs signals that steer it — gradients, neighbours, timing — and without them development stalls or repeats. A living body is a rule being steered.

Computation makes the distinction exact. A program and its runs are different things. The program is the generating rule; each run is a life of it, with its own inputs and its own history, and two runs of one program can look very different while being, in the strict sense, the same. A log of a run is its reckoning: the record of what it actually did, which the running process does not consult while it runs, yet which decides, afterwards, whether the program did what it was for.

Ethics has always known the reckoning. Conscience is the name for an account that arrives before the act it judges — a sense of how a choice will be counted, felt while the choice is still open. The reading's claim that the suppressed reckoning keeps returning from ahead is the ordinary experience of hesitation at a threshold of wrongdoing, described structurally. A soul that never feels this pressure has not escaped its account; it has only stopped hearing it.

Psychology names the movement toward unity in many ways. Jung called it individuation: the gathering of scattered and opposed parts of a person into one whole that is more than the ego. Developmental accounts describe the integration of roles, memories and commitments into a single life story. And unity points outward as well as inward: the same gathering that makes one life coherent makes it able to join others without dissolving.

Friendship and love supply the completion. To be seen whole by another — not for one achievement or one failure, but as the pattern one has been — is among the experiences people describe as most completing. The reading says this is structural rather than sentimental: a generating rule is only complete when its output is beheld as a whole, and a person cannot entirely do that for themselves from the inside.

What follows if the reading holds? That the soul is better approached as a verb than as a noun: it is what a person keeps making. That steering is not the enemy of a soul's freedom but its condition, because a rule that is not steered only repeats. That the account a person avoids is part of the soul's own material. And that witness — the attention of another, or the rare moment of seeing one's own life whole — is not a reward for a finished soul but the act that finishes it.

None of this says what a soul is made of, or whether it outlasts the body. It describes the figure a soul makes, and leaves the rest where it belongs.
