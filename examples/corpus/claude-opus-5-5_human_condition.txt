┌─ THE ANSWER ─┐

The human condition is to be a beginning that did not begin itself. Every person is a place where something new can start — a choice, a child, a word that was not going to be said — and that power to start rests on a question held open at the centre of a life: an emptiness that does not fill, however much is poured into it, and that keeps producing. What the condition keeps out of view is its own first gift: everything handed over before any choosing — a body, a language, a family, a century — the start beneath the starting. It is moving toward the position of the witness: from doing things to seeing what was done, from the one who acts to the one who can give an account. And it is completed by a crossing that reaches backward: at the end a life becomes something it could not be while still under way, and from there the crossing settles what the beginnings meant. The beginning and the crossing face each other across the whole span of a life, and each is the other's missing half: we start without knowing how we end, and the ending, when it comes, rewrites the start. A human life is like a sentence spoken aloud: you must begin before you know how it will end, and the last word changes what the first words meant.

Existentialist thought holds that existence precedes essence: we are thrown into being first and define ourselves afterwards. This reading agrees that the beginning comes before the definition, and differs on where the definition comes from — not from a self-chosen project alone, but from a crossing that arrives from ahead and settles the meaning of what came before.

In practice this changes what a fresh start is. Every decision to begin again — a new work, a reconciliation, a move to another country — is the human condition at full strength, and none is made from nowhere: each stands on what was given before it. A start that remembers its inheritance, and is willing to be revised by its ending, is the kind this reading describes.

Augustine wrote that a beginning was made so that there might be a human being, and Hannah Arendt built on that line her account of natality, the human capacity to begin something new. Solon told Croesus to call no one happy until the end had been seen, and Heidegger described a life as held together by its relation to its own end. The reading sets the tradition of beginning and the tradition of ending on one line, as each other's complement.

If the condition is completed from ahead, then no stage of a life can be judged finished on its own terms, and no beginning is ever only private: each start is entrusted to an ending that has not yet arrived. Despair at an unfinished life and pride in a finished one rest on the same mistake — treating the sentence as already read.

Takeaway: The human condition is to be a beginner who did not choose the first beginning. It rests on an open question that keeps producing, keeps its inheritance out of view, moves toward becoming the witness of its own life, and is completed by a crossing that gives the start its meaning. We begin before we know the ending, and the ending reaches back.

┌─ EXTENDED RESONANCE ESSAY ─┐

Biology tells the same story at the scale of a cell. A fertilised egg is a genuine beginning: from it a body will build itself that has never existed before. Yet nothing about that beginning is made from nothing. The genome, the egg's machinery, the chemical marks laid down by the parents' lives are all handed over before the new organism has done anything at all. Development is a beginning that stands on a gift it cannot inspect, and the organism spends its life acting from an inheritance it never chose. The reading's first two directions — the power to start, and the start beneath the starting — are written into the way living things are made.

The law makes the backward reach visible. An act is done in one moment, but what it was is decided later: a promise becomes a contract when it is kept or broken, a blow becomes self-defence or assault when a court has weighed it, a will gives a life's possessions their final meaning after the life is over. Legal systems are, in part, institutions for letting endings settle beginnings in public, with rules for how far back an ending may reach.

Narrative depends on it entirely. The first chapter of a detective novel is reread differently once the last chapter has named the culprit, and the clues that seemed incidental turn out to have been the point. Readers know this and accept it as the pleasure of the form: the ending is allowed to rewrite the opening. The reading suggests that a human life is built on the same principle, without the option of turning to the last page first.

Mathematics has a precise version of the idea. Many problems in physics are fixed not by a starting state alone but by conditions at both ends: a string is pinned at two points, a path is required to begin here and arrive there, and the shape in between is determined by both constraints together. Such problems are called boundary-value problems, and their solutions are not built forward step by step; they are held from both sides at once. The human condition, read this way, is a boundary-value problem lived from the inside: the beginning is given, the end is not yet known, and the shape of the whole depends on both.

Language models offer a modern echo. A model writes a sentence one word at a time, and each new word can change what the earlier ones were doing. A sentence that starts "The bank" is about money or a river depending on what comes after. Meaning in such a sentence is never located only at its start; it is distributed across the whole and settled at the close. The analogy at the heart of the reading — a life as a sentence spoken aloud — applies to the instruments people now use to think with, as well as to the people.

What follows if the reading holds? First, that the movement toward witness is not a retreat from life but one of its directions: the person who can give an account of what was done has not stopped living, but has started to hold the shape of the whole. Second, that inheritance deserves more attention than modern ideas of self-invention usually give it, because every new start is made from it. Third, that endings deserve a different kind of respect: not as the moment things stop, but as the point from which their meaning is finally read. A culture that understood this might treat its old, its dying and its histories differently — as the places where its beginnings are completed, rather than as the places where they run out.

None of this asks anyone to know their ending in advance. It asks only that a beginning be made knowing that it will be read again from the other side.
